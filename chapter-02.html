Anytime a prince or princess left the capital city, the kingdom held a banquet in their honor, an occasion for well wishes and gifts to be given, something like a going-away party. The banquet was held in the same room where his exile was decided. His father, Harrold, had privately called for him beforehand to explain where he'd be sent. He'd be governing a barony on the outskirts of the capital, called Kaldo, named for the knight who founded it, Kal, a man who'd earned enough merit in service that he was given the choice to leave the capital or stay, and chose to leave and build something of his own instead.

The banquet had commenced, and the local nobility filed through to offer their wishes and gifts. Being the third prince meant Emeric was never going to receive anything truly significant; he was too many steps removed from real power, and most of the capital's nobles were the kind of two-faced people who plotted quietly and only gave gifts of real weight when they expected a favor returned later. His father went first, pressing a pouch of fifty gold Rand into his hands, more formality than warmth, the kind of gift a king gives when he can't say what he actually means. Alongside it came a modest heap of mana crystals, uncut, unremarkable, the sort of thing an affluent magic tutor would hand a promising student rather than a king would hand a son. Emeric understood the shape of it without needing it explained. His father had never stopped hoping some trace of the boy who used to be enthralled by crystals, reading copious books on them just to see how they worked, would resurface given enough idle hours in a quiet barony. The chest of raw mana stones was a great way to accrue a couple more coins if he pawned them off. Blacksmiths loved these things.

Corbin, his eldest brother, the crown prince, went next and made a show of it. "Something to bring back the old passion," he said, holding out a set of Hollow Crystals with a smile that read as nostalgic to anyone not paying attention. "Unchanging," he added, turning one over in his palm before handing it across. "Just like you. Keep staying true to you, little brother."

The word sat there, pleasant enough on its surface. Emeric took it apart anyway, out of old habit, the way he'd have parsed a clause that compiled clean but ran wrong. Unchanging wasn't a compliment for a gift; it was a diagnosis. Everyone called them Hollow because nobody had ever gotten one to do anything, dead weight carved to look purposeful, worthless enough that giving copious amounts of them away cost Corbin nothing. Unchanging, then, wasn't nostalgia. It was a verdict dressed as one. Just like you. Third son, legitimate by blood and nothing else, and never going to become anything more than that no matter what happened next. Corbin hadn't given him a hobby. He'd handed him a sentence wrapped in warmth so the room would read the wrong one.

"Your mother gave you one of these once," Corbin said, softer now, almost gentle. "Thought it might mean something, you having another."

Something in Emeric's chest went quiet and heavy at that, no fear in it, nothing sharp, just the particular ache of a memory he tried to suppress. He didn't know why the rune itself pulled at that ache the way it did, only that it did, some old grief surfacing without paperwork attached to explain itself. Corbin was already moving on to the next pleasantry, and whatever the smile on his face was doing, it wasn't genuine.

General Voss came next, a soldier's gift for a soldier's road: a heavy oilskin cloak, the collar reinforced, stitched the way gear got stitched for months on campaign rather than a single season at court. No speech went with it, just a short nod and, "You'll need this more than a crystal where you're going, Your Highness." It was the first gift all evening that assumed he'd actually be cold.

Armand found him a few gifts later, unassuming as ever, holding out a slim leather-bound notebook with both hands as if it might bruise. "My own notes," he said, not quite meeting Emeric's eyes. "On crystal theory. I never did finish teaching you properly. Maybe you'll get further with it than I did." It wasn't an apology, not exactly, but it sat close enough to one that Emeric didn't know what to do with it, so he thanked him and let the line move on before either of them had to stand in what neither of them had said.

Then came a duke Emeric barely knew, old-blood, from a house said to be as old as the main royal line of the Randillia family. His gift was a set of wooden carved toy soldiers, the kind given to little boys playing war in their bedrooms. The kind of toys you'd give to a boy half Emeric's age. He offered it plainly with an imperceptible smirk on his face, the kind most people would miss. The duke had this air of pure disdain when it came to Emeric; he came to the banquet just as an act of courtesy, like most nobles in the capital. Duke Aldane hadn't even bothered dressing it up as a genuine gift; he hadn't thought Emeric was worth the effort of a good lie. The whole event felt like its own insult.

The rest of the room moved through the same motions, minor nobles and foreign envoys offering generic luxuries out of obligation, the kind of gifts given because attendance demanded it, not because anyone in that room expected Emeric to matter again.

The carriage waiting for him in the courtyard was plain, built for distance rather than display, but Emeric's eye caught on the undercarriage before he'd even reached the door. Four squat black stones with starlike sparkles, it looked as if someone chipped away at the night sky. They sat fitted into the axle housings, carved in a dense, repeating lattice he didn't recognize as decoration. Nobody carved a pattern that consistent, that structurally repetitive, unless the pattern was the point.

He crouched to look closer, and one of the stable hands, misreading the interest as concern, rocked the carriage gently to demonstrate. It gave, the whole frame settling and rebounding in a slow, cushioned wobble, the kind of give a cart axle had no business having on its own. Emeric had seen that motion before, just never here. A stone carved to resist and release force at a fixed threshold instead of transmitting it straight up through the frame. No moving parts, no linkage, nothing to lubricate or replace. Someone had figured out how to make a rock behave like a spring, and done it without a single Hollow Crystal anywhere in the build.

It shouldn't have surprised him as much as it did. He'd spent time in many of these carriages going through the capital, having a leisurely time. Today, for some reason, it bothered him. He wondered why he'd stopped caring about these mechanics that used to interest him as a kid.

Not wanting to delay the day's proceedings any longer, he climbed in without saying anything, and spent the first mile of the ride watching the road, the castle, the people through the window, feeling the carriage sway soft and slow over every rut instead of jolting through it, and thinking that he'd have to remember this feeling the next time he caught himself assuming he already understood what this world could do.

The castle looked like it had grown out of the hill rather than been built on it, tier stacked on tier all the way up, each ring of wall sitting a little higher and a little tighter than the one below. From the carriage window, the whole city read as a single fortified spiral: outer wall first, hugging the river's curve, then a band of packed rooftops, then a second wall, then more rooftops, narrower now, until the pattern ran out of room and the castle itself took over, spires catching the light above everything else. The river did most of the work at the bottom, looping almost all the way around the base of the hill before doubling back on itself, so that from above the city would look like it was sitting inside a loop of its own moat. Bridges crossed it at a handful of fixed points, no more, each one narrow enough for a squad to hold and probably meant to be held that way. He counted the rings without meaning to, the way he used to count load paths in a structure, and the count came out clean: nothing about the layout was accidental. Someone had designed this the way you'd design a filter, with only so many ways in, and every one of them watched.

It just dawned on him that the capital didn't smell terrible at all. The him from this world doesn't remember a time the capital smelled, but the him from Earth remembers reading about the heinous smells of kingdoms in the medieval era. Back in the day, when he was more studious, he'd often read about the kingdom in the books his tutor would give him. The capital was one of the best places to live. The sheer number of utility mages they had made life here great. Mages handled farming, waste management, and many more items every kingdom needed.

The mage tower itself was a marvel in mage engineering. Emeric couldn't even start to gauge how they'd gotten the upper gallery to sit where it did, a full ring of stone hanging clear of the tower's spine with nothing beneath it but air and rune-light, until he'd stood there long enough to notice the mages.

There were three of them at any given time, stationed at the tower's crown, each one gripping a crystal the size of a melon, eyes half-closed, jaw set with the particular concentration of someone holding a weight they couldn't set down. A fourth stood just behind the nearest, watching his hands the way a spotter watches a lift, ready to take the load the instant the first mage's grip on it slipped. Every so often one of the three would step back, shoulders dropping, and another would step forward to take his place mid-stride, the handoff so practiced it barely registered as a handoff at all.

The mage tower rose past the rooftops well before the carriage reached the bridges, and Emeric found himself watching it through the window without meaning to. Too far off to make out anything at that distance, no gallery ring, no mages, just the tower's shape against the sky and the fact, sitting in his head with no picture attached, that his old tutor had once taken him up there as a boy.

He didn't remember the day. He had no sense of the stairs, the tutor's voice, what the air had smelled like that high up. What he had was the outcome, flat and factual: he'd stood at the tower's crown once and watched a full council session, twenty mages strong, trade a floating gallery between them by hand, and he'd asked an acolyte why nobody had simply built the thing to hold itself up. The acolyte's answer had stuck, word for word, the way a fact sticks when nothing else survives around it. The gallery held the Circle's council, and it floated because the Circle answered to no staircase. The mages holding it weren't laborers; they were the order's best initiates, competing for the honor of doing it. Somewhere in that same conversation, a boy who hadn't yet lost interest in how things worked had decided the whole arrangement was the coolest thing he'd ever seen.

That verdict hadn't traveled with him. Only the record of having reached it had. Looking at the same tower now, with nothing behind his eyes but an engineer's read on the problem, Emeric couldn't find the boy's answer anywhere in himself. Three men, and before them three others, and before them three more, trading an unsolved engineering problem back and forth by hand because somebody, generations ago, had decided the cost was the point rather than the flaw. It wasn't cool. It was the single most impressive waste of skilled labor he'd seen since arriving, propped up by a story good enough that nobody currently holding that gallery up had ever been given a reason to ask why they still had to. He caught himself doing it again as the tower slid past the window, the same sorting he'd been doing since he woke up in this body: that reaction was the boy's, this one is mine, as if the two of them were still standing in separate rooms comparing notes.

Without noticing when, Emeric had noticed he was at the kingdom's capital gate.
"Goodbye, sweet Randillia City," he said with a hint of sadness in his voice.

The roads inside the castle were smooth, and that's without accounting for the magic suspension of this carriage. The roads immediately outside the kingdom city's gates were cobbled. They weren't terrible, but the roads in the kingdom's city were better. The roads after that were horrendous. The lack of a properly paved path put the suspension to work. If this were Earth, he'd need to go to a mechanic the second he got to the destination.

The trip to Kaldo would take the better part of a week by carriage, longer than any horse alone would need, but a prince didn't ride bareback through hostile road conditions if there was another option. Six guards rode with him: Ward at the lead, Rylan, Declan, Osric, Petram, and a lean, quiet man named Corin who'd said maybe ten words the entire trip. Two, maybe three days from now, they'd hit the worst stretch, the forest road past Wayford, and the horses would need real rest before that, not just a water break.

Petram brought it up first, reins loose in his hands, voice carrying that easy, reasonable tone guards used when they weren't trying to be noticed. "Horses won't make the forest road fresh, Your Highness. Better we camp here, push through in daylight tomorrow."

It was sound advice. Nobody argued with sound advice.

The fire had burned down to a low orange bed by the time the stories started, the kind of talk men fall into on the road when there's nothing left to do but wait for morning. Osric went first, some half-true tale about a bandit camp outside Grayrock that grew a little in the telling, three men becoming five, five becoming a "small army" by the time he finished. Declan called him on it without much heat, and the two of them argued the number back down while Petram laughed into his cup.

"You're all soft," Ward said, not looking up from the blade he was sharpening. "Try a real posting sometime. You'd stop counting bandits and start counting hours until you're relieved."

It wasn't much of a story, and Ward clearly meant it to end there. Nobody pushed. Something in the flat way he'd said it told them not to.

Rylan filled the quiet instead, gentler.

"Kaldo used to be good, before all that," he said, poking at the fire with a stick, more to himself than the group. "My wife's family's been there three generations. When I was a boy, the harbor still had real trade coming through, not just fishing boats patching their own nets. My mother used to take me down to watch the ships unload." He shook his head, something soft and a little sad in it. "War took the men, plague took the rest. My family stayed through both. Don't know if that was courage or just nobody had anywhere else to go."

"Your wife still there?" Petram asked, easy, like it was nothing.

"Nessa, yeah. And our boy Finn. Little one, Wren, too." Rylan's voice warmed just saying their names. "Haven't seen them in four months. Figured I'd get a real homecoming out of this posting, at least."

Corin, quiet as always, didn't say much. He just listened, the way he'd been listening the whole trip.

Petram had taken some tea out from the carriage storage and steeped it. Emeric didn't think twice about the tea Petram passed around the fire that night, bitter and herbal, the kind of thing men drank on the road all the time. He didn't think about it because there was nothing to think about, not until the world went soft and slow around the edges and every guard but one slumped where they sat.

"Fuck, we've been poisoned," Emeric said while fighting drowsiness.

Ward didn't go down. He swayed, fought it, planted himself between the fire and the carriage like a wall that refused the idea of falling, and whatever dose was meant to drop a normal man barely touched a frame built like his.

Emeric didn't go down either. His vision blurred, his limbs turned to lead, but something in him, something that had always run a little hotter and cleaner than it should have, burned the fog off before it could finish the job. He came back to himself in time to see Petram's knife already drawn, already moving toward Ward's back.

It happened fast after that. Ward turned faster than a drugged man should have been able to, took the blade across his forearm instead of his spine, and put Petram down hard enough that he wouldn't be getting up to run anywhere. Emeric was surprised he was still breathing after that. Ward had enough sense left in him, even half-fogged, to know a dead traitor told you nothing.

Corin, the apparent second traitorous guard, was already moving, boots hitting the treeline before anyone thought to stop him. Ward's bracer sat dead against his skin, the Kinetic Rune in it useless with his mind this fogged, no clean focus to draw the crystal's charge through. He could still catch up to Corin, probably, muscle and instinct alone, but chasing a healthy man through dark woods without the boost, on legs he couldn't fully trust, was a good way to end up dead himself. He let him go. Someone would have to find Corin later, if he was going to be found at all.

Emeric sat in the dirt afterward, hands shaking, watching Ward bind his own wound with his teeth and one working hand, and something in his chest cracked open that had been sealed shut for fifteen years.

"I froze," he said, voice smaller than he meant it to be. "Just like then."

Ward didn't ask what he meant. He didn't need to.

"You were seven," Ward said. "You're not seven anymore."

But Emeric was already somewhere else, in a carriage that smelled like his mother's perfume, watching her push him behind her body a half-second before the world ended. He hadn't moved then either. He hadn't moved, and she'd died, and somewhere in the years since, a small silent part of him had decided that not moving was safer than moving wrong.

"From now on," he thought to himself silently, wiping his face with the back of a filthy hand, "there's no Emeric and Marcus. There's just me. I don't get to hide behind either name anymore, not when standing there quietly nearly got my men killed."
