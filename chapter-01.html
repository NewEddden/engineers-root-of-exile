The rain hadn't started yet. Marcus noticed that, the way you notice small things right before everything changes. He'd signed the consent forms three weeks ago in a waiting room that smelled like new carpets and old coffee, six months after the layoffs. The savings he had saved over the years were rapidly draining, and a source of funds was needed yesterday. Direct skill induction, the recruiter had called it. Cognitive scaffolding transfer, said the forms. What it meant, in practice, was a headset, a chair that reclined too far, and one session a week of someone else's decade of engineering intuition getting pressed into the grooves of his brain like a foot into wet sand. A human lab rat to sum it up kindly.

At first he was skeptical, and not just for the reason everyone else was, the horror-movie version where the recipient loses their mind or dies in some gruesome way. His skepticism ran a layer deeper than that, an engineer's instinct for what a system actually implies versus what it's marketed as. If skill could be pressed into a person from outside, that meant a person, the whole assembly of someone's mind, was portable in principle. Nobody at the company had said that part out loud, and there was no reason to think they ever would. He sat with that for longer than the consent form deserved, then signed anyway. There was no real choice left to make about it.

Several weeks earlier, in the immediate moment of the layoffs, he had signed the applications for several companies: Denholm, Metalworks, Furna, to name a few. The companies he interviewed for were taking their sweet time calling him back, and he desperately needed some funds. He had gotten the invite to this clinical trial. They said they wanted people of his expertise and laid it out flat for him. The company was a publicly traded company with good standing, and this test was announced to the world; hell, even the recipients were known, listed with their personal info hidden, of course. They made it nearly impossible to bury the evidence for themselves, which made it easier for Marcus to accept the invitation. They had amassed a decent group of people from those who are learned in certain fields and those who aren't learned at all. They were testing multiple things at once and needed a wide sample.

Scientists have speculated this could be possible for the longest time. The human brain is a marvel of engineering that nobody has taken ownership of. If god is real, then he, she, or it is truly "built different." The human brain not only handles an absurd amount of computations passively, but also has an estimated memory storage capacity of roughly 2.5 petabytes. Aside from the extensive storage and computation, it also does that while using only 20 watts of power. That much power can handle powering a small electronic device like a smartphone fast charger, some LED light bulbs, a small Wi-Fi router, or a small DC desk fan. The greatest fear about this whole ordeal so far is the possibility of being killed off and used as some macabre dystopian brain storage. Well, it's too late now; the papers are signed, and the commitment is assured. The team that handles the clinical trial emailed Marcus and prompted him to continue living normally, and they would monitor him for any changes. Within the month the clinical trial test started, Marcus finally received a job offer from the small local manufacturing firm he interviewed for. It paid well, and the clinical trial offset what it couldn't.

The two schedules ran alongside each other easily enough at first. One day a week he drove to the trial facility, put on the headset, let the chair recline further than felt structurally reasonable, and let someone else's decade of engineering intuition get pressed into his brain in ninety-minute doses. The other days he was just the new guy at Denholm, learning where the good coffee was hidden and which drawings were trustworthy and which were aspirational. Nobody at the firm knew what he was doing on the mornings he came in a little late.

Nobody at the trial knew what he was doing with what they were giving him. For a while, that division held, and it worked. That was the part nobody warned him about, how well it worked. He caught himself, halfway through session four, sketching a load path on a napkin, tracing the route a force would take through a stack of components down to the ground, one member to the next, until it was fully carried. It was the kind of thing that took a good engineer years to see without effort, tracing the chain instead of just reading a single part in isolation, and he'd done it in the margin of a napkin without once stopping to think about it.

By session 6, he'd noticed at work that the numbers on Denholm Fabrication's spec sheet didn't agree with themselves. It was a Tuesday call, nothing marked urgent on the invite, just the usual thirty minutes with Denholm's applications engineer walking Procurement and Quality through the machined brackets for the new housing assembly. Marcus was half-listening, sketching in the margin of his notepad, when his eyes snagged on a tolerance callout for the mounting boss and didn't let go. He pulled up the mating part on his second monitor. Then the fastener spec behind that. Three components, three separate drawings, three separate engineers who'd each done their own job correctly and never once talked to each other. Individually, every tolerance was fine. Stacked, worst case on worst case, the assembly wouldn't close. He sat with it for a second, waiting for the doubt that usually showed up right about here, the second-guessing, the "surely somebody already checked this." It didn't come. What came instead was just certainty, quiet and total, the way you're certain a door you just walked through is a door.

"Hey, can we back up?" he said, cutting into the applications engineer's sentence without quite meaning to. "The boss tolerance on the bracket, the shaft OD on the sub-frame, and the fastener's minimum engagement length- run those worst-case together and there's no clearance left. It's going to bind."

Silence on the line; the first part of the silence is from the fact that no newcomer has ever called out a potential error like this. Most don't see an issue, and if they did, then there's no way they'd be confident enough to call it out. The second part of the silence is of people scrambling to pull up drawings they were only pretending to have memorized.

"...Let me check that," the applications engineer said, in the tone of a man who was going to be checking it for the rest of the afternoon. The fact that the newcomer broke all conventionality and spoke up warranted a double check.

He was right. It took Denholm two days to confirm it and revise the drawing, and it cost the company nothing except the mild indignity of Procurement asking Marcus, three separate times, how he'd caught it, like the answer might change if they asked again. He didn't have a good answer. Two months ago he wouldn't have known to look, and even if he saw it he wouldn't have trusted himself. No way these experts would make this mistake an amateur like me could find. Now the looking just happened, unbidden, the way you notice a crooked picture frame in someone else's house. He put it out of his mind and went back to his sketch. It wasn't until the drive home that it occurred to him to wonder what session ten was going to feel like, if session six felt like this.

By session 10, his knowledge had expanded past anything resembling his field.

It was Saturday. Marcus had been invited to a barbecue, of all places, a coworker's kid's birthday, holding a beer he wasn't really drinking, when the coworker's father-in-law started coughing in that particular way, wet and effortful, that everyone else at the table politely pretended not to notice. The old man waved it off, something about his heart medication, a new one, doctor's still figuring out the dose. Marcus heard the name of the drug and the dose in the same breath and felt something click into place that had no business clicking, a whole shelf of knowledge sliding out where a second ago there'd been nothing. Contraindications. Interaction profiles. The specific mechanism by which that drug, at that dose, in a man that age with that cough, could be doing more harm than the condition it was treating.

He'd never studied medicine. He wasn't even sure, sitting there with a warm beer in his hand, that he could have told you the difference between systolic and diastolic a year ago. And yet here it was, arriving whole, confident and uninvited. The feeling and knowledge came the same way session six's tolerance stack-up had arrived, except this time there was no napkin, no CAD file, nothing in his actual life that explained why he'd know it.

"You should mention that cough to whoever adjusted the dose," he said, careful to make it sound like a guess. "Sooner than your next appointment."

The old man's eyes narrowed, more amused than offended, but not by much. "You a doctor now?"

"No." Too fast. He heard it land wrong even as it left his mouth.

"Mechanical engineer, right? Kevin said you build brackets for a living." The old man wasn't letting it go the way he was supposed to. "So how do you know what my heart medication does?"

"Read about it. Had a relative on something similar." The lie came out smoother than the truth would have, which bothered him more than the lie itself did.

Kevin, two seats down, had stopped mid-conversation to watch this happen, the specific stillness of a host recalibrating what he actually knew about a guest he'd invited. Marcus caught the look and understood exactly what it meant: not suspicion yet, just a data point Kevin would sit with later, the same way Marcus was learning to sit with his own.

The old man laughed it off eventually, but the laugh came a beat too late to undo the pause before it, and he didn't bring the cough up again for the rest of the afternoon. Marcus let it go too, because pushing harder would have meant explaining how he knew, and he didn't have an explanation that wouldn't get him a very different kind of appointment.

Driving home, he did the math nobody at the trial had asked him to do out loud. Session four: structural engineering, adjacent to his own field, explainable as an unusually good teacher. Session six: still mechanical, still his lane. Session ten: a domain he had zero connection to, arriving with the same unearned fluency as the rest. Whatever the trial was actually doing to him, it had stopped respecting the fences around what he knew and finally stepped into the yard of another field. He made a note to bring it up at his next monitoring session.

By session 12, he'd started having intense moments of focus, the kind where he could work on anything for hours without noticing the hours pass. It reminded him, oddly, of the way his college friends used to describe Adderall, that specific narrowing where the rest of the world goes quiet and only the problem in front of you stays lit.

Even more surprising, the focus wasn't limited to work. There was an instance at Denholm where he noticed a coworker walking toward what was very likely going to be a fall. Someone had forgetfully left a small cylindrical pipe on the floor, and based on the distance of each of the coworker's steps, Marcus could see it coming the way you see the end of a sentence before someone finishes saying it: he'd roll his foot on it, lose his balance, fall backward, and probably catch his head on the edge of a design floor desk on the way down.

Part of Marcus wanted to see if he was right. The part of him that still thought like an engineer, the part that liked confirming a prediction, wanted the data. But being right would cost the guy a concussion to prove it, so he let that part lose the argument.

"Hey," he said, already moving, "there's a pipe right 3 steps from where you're walking to, watch out for that."

Marcus had copious amounts of knowledge readily available to him once he deemed it necessary, and even instances where the knowledge would come passively. Although the amount felt substantial, there was no overload. He'd wake up feeling normal, if it wasn't for the fact he had to go to the trial weekly and the sureness of answering damn near any question; he wouldn't be able to tell if there were any changes.

By session 14, he'd stopped worrying about the company's secret and started worrying about his own. Structural engineering was explainable, a good teacher, a lucky fit with his own field. Medicine wasn't, and neither was the pipe he'd seen coming three steps out. If the fences between fields came down that easily, he didn't know what was still standing on the other side of the one that used to be him. He signed the log for the day, same as always, and didn't write any of that down.

For the rest of the day, he was pensive about it. Since the session was over, he walked back the same route he always took. At this point, the path was automatic; he could've walked it blindfolded. Hell, he could've walked it while sleeping. Today wasn't going to be one of those days.

Marcus was crossing the street, still turning the earlier realization over, when his mind snapped back to full consciousness at the sight of a little girl stepping into the road.

Everything happened too fast to file in order. A crash. He was on the ground, no pain, emotions muted, the way a room goes quiet right before an alarm you haven't registered yet. Realization hadn't set in.

A woman on her phone, a hand that was holding a smaller hand and then wasn't, a kid in a yellow jacket taking two quick delighted steps off a curb because a curb is the most interesting geography a four-year-old knows. Marcus was already moving before he'd decided to move, some borrowed reflex getting there faster than he could. He got the kid clear. He didn't get clear himself.

There was a strange, unhurried moment afterward, the kind you're not supposed to get. He seemed to be standing a few feet above the street looking down at the shape of himself and the shape of a bumper and a woman screaming into a phone at the 911 operator. Later, if there was a later, he'd want to know how long that actually took. It felt like minutes. It was probably less than four seconds. That gap alone was worth a whole session's thinking, if he ever got the chance to think again. He noted, a couple of seconds before the crash, without much feeling, that the woman was so entangled with whatever was on that phone that she'd let go of the kid. He noted the driver's face through the windshield, the particular blankness of someone dissociating while driving. He found it a little funny how ironic it was. Given that he had been paying attention, he could have saved the girl and potentially survived too.

Then the shout came.

"Emeric!" shouted this man on a high ledge.

One moment he'd been on a street, a car, a woman, a child, panicked bystanders. The next, he was standing in a room with high ceilings and gold-threaded banners hanging still in air that didn't move. The man shouting his name wore a crown, his face gone rigid with something that might have been rage or disappointment.

Who in the flying fuck is this man yelling at me and why, Marcus thought, with what felt like the last clear thought that was entirely his own.

Within the same thought, it felt as if a dam had collapsed into a tsunami of memories.

Twenty-two years of memories all arriving not as a story but as a texture. They came the same way a smell arrives before you've decided to notice it. He knew, suddenly and without being told, that the banners were his father's colors. He knew the shape of this room the way you know the shape of your childhood kitchen with your eyes closed, hip-checking the same drawer for twenty years. He knew, distantly, that his hands and body were smaller than they should be. There was this peculiar disconnect of self. He couldn't gauge; was he the man from Earth or the man from this world? Who got whose memory?

He did not know yet why the man was shouting. That came a half-second later, in fragments. In the newly gotten memories he saw a diplomat's face turning cold at a party he couldn't quite picture, a room full of people looking at him, the specific held-breath quality of a scandal that hadn't finished happening yet. It arrived the way session ten's medicine knowledge had arrived at that barbecue. Everything happening so far culminated in him not paying attention to anything his father said. The second his brain fully tuned in to his current reality, his father was already in the middle of a sentence he'd heard without truly registering.

"---have made your choice for you, since you haven't made any for yourself in twenty-two years."

Marcus, who had been dead four seconds ago by his own best accounting, and was now apparently twenty-two years old, and possibly a prince. He made an attempt to open his mouth but truly had absolutely nothing to say.

The walk to his chambers took longer than it should have, mostly because Marcus, no, Emeric, kept stopping to stare at things he shouldn't remember but does. He's still reeling about who he is: Marcus of Earth or Emeric of Ostenne. The memories gave the mundane items of the castle this new and old feeling. A tapestry. A particular crack in a particular column. His feet knew where to turn before he did, and he let them, since arguing with his own legs felt like a fight he'd lose.

The guard walked half a step behind him, silent, hands loose at his sides in the specific way of someone who'd spent fifteen years making sure his hands were never far from something. He was enormous, easily six foot six, built like a wall that had been given a grudge. Emeric's new memories supplied the name: Ward.

Fifteen years. He had known this man since he was a child.

"Do you have any thoughts on today's events?" Emeric said, mostly to hear his own voice in this body.

"Nothing comes to mind," Ward said, evading the whole question.

That was it. That was the whole answer, delivered flat, and somehow it was so exactly correct, so clearly the shape of fifteen years of this exact dynamic, that Emeric almost laughed. Almost.

"Fuck," he said instead, quiet, mostly to himself. "This can't be. An isekai?"

Ward didn't ask what that word meant. He just kept walking, half a step behind, like a man who'd long ago stopped being surprised by what this prince was doing.

The room, his room, apparently, was bigger than his old apartment and colder than he expected. A trunk sat open at the foot of the bed already, like someone had known this conversation with his father would end exactly like this before it started. Emeric moved through it on autopilot, hands doing the folding while his mind sat somewhere just behind his own eyes, still catching up.

He was almost done when his hand closed around something small at the back of a drawer he hadn't consciously decided to open.

A crystal. Dull, greyish white, the kind of thing that looked like it belonged in a child's rock collection rather than a prince's chambers. He knew, instinctually, the way he now seemed to know everything, that the household considered it worthless. Decorative. A trinket he'd stopped bothering with years ago. He felt there was a valid reason why he kept it yet paid it no mind, but it was as if that memory was actively eluding him.

He almost put it back.

Instead, his fingers curled around it, and something in his chest loosened that he hadn't realized was tight. Not relief exactly. Something quieter than that. Something like recognition and comfort.

He didn't know why. He put it in his pocket anyway.

Since his departure was early, he decided to go to sleep after. Maybe this was all a dream, and he'd wake up on Earth with the memories of a man from another world. Unfortunately for him, it wasn't.
