The judiciary chamber wasn't large. Emeric had expected something built for spectacle, the kind of room a kingdom used to remind people what power looked like. Instead it was close, plain, a long table and hard chairs and windows too high to see out of, built for work rather than performance. That told him something about Keller before the man even spoke.

Keller was older than Emeric expected, gray at the temples, a stack of papers already squared in front of him like he'd read everything twice before anyone arrived. He didn't stand when they entered. He didn't need to.

"Prince Emeric." A nod, nothing more. "Baron Wexler."

Wexler sat across the table, composed in a way that cost him visible effort. Cold and calculated, same as Kaldo, but the wariness had a new edge to it now, a man doing math on his own odds and not liking the answer as much as he used to.

"I petitioned this hearing," Wexler said, "because rumor has a way of becoming record if left unanswered. I'd rather answer it directly."

"Then answer it directly," Keller said. "What rumor?"

"That I've misused parish debt records for personal gain." Wexler's voice stayed even. "I haven't. I'd like that stated plainly, for the record, before anything else gets attached to my name that doesn't belong there."

Keller didn't respond to that. He turned instead to Emeric. "You have evidence, or you don't. I'm not interested in which of you says it louder."

Ferra stepped forward before Emeric could answer for her, which he preferred. This was hers to carry.

"Three parish ledger pages, scraped and rewritten." She laid them flat. "Original entries recorded a debt discharge routed through a waypoint marker, not a merchant house. That route belongs to a man named Cobb." She placed a second paper beside the first, one that hadn't existed two days ago, the rider's delivery, still creased from hard travel. "This confirms the rest. Cobb's payment records, cross-referenced against parish coin marks. The coin that discharged those debts came from Baron Wexler's own treasury seal. Not laundered through a house. Direct."

The room went quiet in the specific way rooms go quiet when something just became harder to argue with.

"Timed," Ferra added, "to within a week of the attempt on Prince Corbin's life."

Wexler's composure didn't break. It just stopped moving, which was worse.

Keller studied the pages for longer than felt necessary, the way a man reads something twice not because he doubts it but because he wants to be sure he isn't wrong about how bad it is. "Baron. Explain the seal."

"I don't control every payment that leaves my treasury down to the coin."

"You control the seal." Keller didn't raise his voice. He didn't need to. "That's what a seal is for. Try again."

Wexler tried again. It didn't hold up any better the second time, and Emeric watched something happen he hadn't expected to feel satisfaction from and did anyway, briefly, a man's own story failing to survive contact with a process that didn't care whose title was in the room. Wexler had run to the crown believing procedure would protect him. Procedure was the thing taking him apart.

Keller set the pages down. "I have what I need." He looked at Wexler without anything that could be called sympathy or its opposite. "The judiciary finds sufficient evidence of treasonous conspiracy, specifically the financing of an attempt on the life of the crown's own heir apparent. Sentence is death, to be carried out by crown order, not baronial discretion, not personal grievance." A glance at Emeric, brief, almost a warning. "This is the crown's judgment. Not yours. Remember the difference."

"I will," Emeric said, and meant it more than Keller probably expected him to.

There wasn't a scene after that. No dramatic removal, no last words worth recording. Wexler was escorted out by men who did this without needing to feel anything about it, and the chamber emptied the way rooms empty after the actual work is finished, quietly, everyone already thinking about the next thing.

Ward found him before he left the capital. Didn't ask how it felt. Didn't need to.

"Cobb's still out there," Ward said.

"He is." Emeric put the memory of the hearing away, next to things he'd stopped expecting to resolve cleanly. "That's a thread for later. Not this one."

The tower hadn't changed. Emeric stood at its base longer than he meant to, looking up at the same climb he remembered as a boy, before he'd made himself go up.

Armand looked older than the memory, though the memory was fifteen years out of date, so that was hardly fair. He came down when word reached him a prince was asking at the gate, and whatever he'd expected to see, it took him a moment to reconcile it with the man standing in front of him.

"Your Highness." Then, quieter, dropping the formality like it had never quite fit him. "Emeric."

"I need you to see something." No greeting, no easing into it, and Emeric caught himself doing it and didn't correct course anyway. "Not hear about it. See it. Better, put your hands on it."

Armand's eyebrows went up slightly, the specific look of a man who'd spent decades being difficult to surprise. "That's not how you used to talk to me."

"I've made a discovery." Emeric didn't dress it up further than that, on purpose. "Crystal work. The kind of thing that changes what you think a crystal actually is, not just what it can do. I'm not going to explain it standing in a courtyard. You'd need to be there for it to mean anything."

Something shifted behind Armand's expression, old instinct recognizing itself, a teacher hearing a student describe something worth walking toward instead of assigning.

"I have obligations here," Armand said. "The tower doesn't run itself, and I've spent longer building what runs it than I have left to spend rebuilding it if I walk away carelessly."

"That's not a no."

"It isn't." Armand's mouth moved, not quite a smile, something closer to a man recalculating a decision he'd already half made. "Settle what needs settling here first. Then, best believe I'll be there."

Emeric nodded, once, and didn't push it further than that. He'd learned that much, at least, that some things went worse the harder you pressed them.

He looked up at the tower one more time before he left, the same floating gallery he'd stood under as a boy, initiates still holding it aloft on nothing but discipline and cost. He knew now exactly how much that discipline was worth and exactly how little it needed to be. He didn't say so. Some things you let a man arrive at on his own.

They rode back to Kaldo without much said, and Kaldo, when they reached it, was a barony without a baron for the first time in longer than anyone could remember, its fields, its unassigned labor, its administrative knots that had existed purely because Wexler had wanted them to, all of it sitting open now, waiting for someone to decide what came next.

Emeric found out three days later, secondhand, the way most executions probably reached most people who weren't standing close enough to make it personal. A line in a crown dispatch. Sentence carried out. Nothing more.

He read it twice anyway. Habit now.

It should have felt like something closing. It didn't, not the way he'd expected. Wexler was dead, and the coin trail, the route, the timing, all of it had held up exactly the way Ferra built it to hold up, clean, provable, undeniable even to a man as procedurally cold as Keller. Emeric had done everything right. The system had worked exactly as designed.

Tam's name was still sitting where he'd left it, and no ruling anywhere had touched it. Nobody had asked about the guard on the duty roster, because nobody could prove Wexler had ordered that specifically, only that Wexler had paid for the attempt in general, and general was enough for a treason charge and nowhere near enough for the thing Emeric had actually wanted answered.

He'd gotten a win. He turned that word over the way he turned the crystal over, without quite trusting either of them anymore. It didn't feel like one. He wasn't sure it was supposed to.

Emeric already knew what came next. He just hadn't let himself think about it until now, because thinking about the farm meant admitting the other thing was as finished as it was ever going to get.
