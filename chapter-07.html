"Keep whatever conclusions you draw from that to yourself, until you've understood why," Emeric thought, turning the words over the way he'd been turning the crystal over in his pocket for an hour now, out of habit more than intent. Either the baron genuinely has a reason for a barony this thin on its margins, or he's hoping I'll read guilt as incompetence. What are you hiding, Baron?

He started sorting the way he'd sorted the parish ledgers months back: category first, structure before content. Revenue in one pile, expenditure in another, then finer: tithe remittance, garrison pay, castle upkeep, procurement, and one small pile that kept growing faster than it should have: debts forgiven, obligations discharged. Debt didn't usually just disappear. Most of it got collected, or defaulters got written down as a loss, on the books, traceable. A cluster of quiet write-offs was the kind of thing that stood out precisely because it was supposed to not stand out.

The crystal warmed against his fingers without him deciding to hold it any tighter, and the ledger data in front of him resolved into something cleaner, the same layering sensation from the nights with the crystal alone, structure surfacing underneath what his eyes were already looking at. It didn't tell him anything the pages didn't already say. It just let him hold all of it at once, sorted, filtered, searchable the way his hands alone couldn't manage fast enough. A discharged debt sorted by date instead of by page number. That was all it was doing. Speed, not insight, the same way Ferra's head for numbers wasn't magic either, just faster than the method everyone else was stuck using.

Sorted by date, one entry sat exactly where he didn't want it to sit.

A debt, discharged in full, dated to the same week the carriage had been ambushed on the road into Kaldo.

"No fucking way," Emeric said it out loud, to an empty room. He read the line again, in case the date was going to change the second time. It didn't. "Baron. Baron, baron, baron. What are the odds you're just unlucky?"

He didn't have a name on the other end of that debt yet, only a date that lined up too well to be nothing. That wasn't proof. It wasn't even a lead he could hand to anyone. It was a coincidence with a shape, the kind that meant something only once it had a second coincidence standing next to it to lean on.

Emeric headed over to Bram's place with the documents in tow. At this point, it was ritualistic: Emeric would leave with Rylan, and Ward would follow them secretly. They made it in and, noticing the urgency in Emeric, Ferra took the ledgers the way Bram used to take a cracked blade, without comment, like the problem itself was the only thing in the room worth acknowledging. She didn't sort by category the way Emeric had. She sorted by hand, the actual handwriting, cross-referencing entries against each other until she found what she was looking for.

"This one." She turned the page toward him, tapped a line he'd already flagged, the discharged debt. "Whoever wrote this wasn't hiding the amount. They were hiding who it went to." The name on the entry was a merchant house, plain as anything, except the ink sat a shade different from the entries around it, added later, over something scraped away first. "Somebody wanted a name here badly enough to write one in after the fact. That's not sloppy bookkeeping. That's covering a gap."

"Can you get under it? What was there before?"

"Given time." She didn't look up. "This isn't the only page that's been touched. Give me the rest of the quarter, not just this line."

It wasn't a name yet. It was a shape with a name-sized hole in it, which was still more than he'd had that morning.

Tam was on the door when they came for Petram, same as every rotation since Ward set the schedule. He didn't ask why twice, just stepped aside the way he had the first time, as the asking had already been settled somewhere Emeric hadn't been present for.

Petram looked worse than he had a week ago and better than he had any right to. He didn't waste breath pretending he didn't know why they'd come.

"You're not here to kill me," he said. "You'd have done that already."

"No."

"Then you want a name." He said it flat, not a question, working out the shape of the trade before either of them said the terms out loud. "You know I can't give you the one you actually want. I never met the man. I met the one he sent."

"Give me that one, then."

"Cobb," Petram said it like a man handing over the only coin he had left. A courier's name, someone who moved between towns often enough that nobody would think twice about him showing up in any of them. Tam, close enough to the bars to be doing his job and not close enough to be obviously listening, went very still for the last part of it. Emeric caught it and said nothing. Neither did Petram, though the look he gave the guard said he'd caught it too, and filed it as a problem for later, the way a man in a cell files everything as a problem for later.

Word came before dinner. Tam hadn't shown up to relieve the evening rotation. Found near the mill road, close enough to town that it read, on paper, like exactly the kind of opportunistic violence a barony this poor and this desperate produced with grim regularity. Nothing missing that a robbery wouldn't take. Nothing that pointed anywhere.

Except the timing, which pointed everywhere.

Emeric stood in the cold outside the guardhouse for a long moment before he trusted himself to speak.

"He's gonna regret that."

He didn't say Wexler's name. He didn't need to. And underneath the anger, cold and separate from it, the engineer's part of his mind was already doing what it always did, sorting the loss into what it cost and what it proved. Tam had heard enough to be worth killing for. Which meant the name Petram gave him was real, worth more than Emeric had let himself hope for an hour ago.

That didn't make it easier to walk past the well near the burial ground afterward. Rylan slowed without being asked to, looking at the same street Emeric was.

"Sela's boy first," he said, not really to Emeric, more to the street itself. "Now this."

He didn't explain further. He didn't need to. Emeric filed the name away next to everything else that had cost the same family twice, for a war that had never technically declared her the enemy either time.

The rage came back, sharper this time, and it took him a second to place where it was actually pointed. Not at Wexler. At the version of himself from that morning who'd walked past Tam without once asking his name, without once thinking to ask who was standing guard over the one piece of leverage that mattered. His nails had dug into his palms, drawing blood.
