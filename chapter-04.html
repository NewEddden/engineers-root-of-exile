He already knew better than to walk into the town square and explain why. Bacteria, invisible creatures living in water, some helpful, some lethal, killing people from the inside without anyone lifting a weapon against them; that story would get a foreign engineer burned as a heretic or locked up as mad, prince or not. He didn't need them to believe in germs.

He needed the water moved and the outcome to speak for itself, and if he was going to convince anyone here of anything, it wasn't going to be with a lecture. It was going to be with a prediction that came true, and a set of numbers pulled from records they already trusted, their own.

That was the plan, in theory. In practice, he needed two things he didn't have yet: someone in this town who'd talk to him honestly, and a way into records nobody was going to hand a prince just because he asked nicely.

"Who keeps the death records here?" he asked Rylan on the walk back. "Baptisms, burials, whatever the parish logs."

"Priest keeps the parish book. Has for years." Rylan said it like it was obvious, then caught himself. "You're not thinking of asking him for it."

"Not yet. I'm thinking about who'd vouch for me first, so asking him doesn't go the way everything else here has gone since I arrived." He already knew what that meant, practically. He needed a name the town trusted more than they'd ever trust his title, someone whose word carried weight because they'd earned it over years, not inherited it.

"Bram," Rylan said, before Emeric had finished the thought out loud. "Blacksmith. People bring him more than horseshoes, if you understand me. He's fair, and he's been here longer than the war took anybody. If he says you're worth listening to, some of them will listen." A pause. "If he says you're not, it won't matter what you actually do."

That was either the best lead he'd get or the fastest way to lose this before it started. Emeric filed it away next to the well positions and the burial ground and the shape of the plan that still didn't have a shape yet, and decided the smith's forge was the next place his feet needed to take him.

The walk there cut past the edge of town, where the buildings thinned out into what should have been farmland and mostly wasn't. Fields sat fallow in long stretches, soil gone hard and pale where nothing had been turned in a while, weeds doing better than any crop would have. Further on, a mill wheel sat motionless over water that clearly still had current enough to turn it. He didn't stop to look closer at either. Not yet. He just added them to the same mental column as the wells and the burial ground, unsorted, unexplained, waiting on more data before they meant anything specific.

The smith's forge sat in a decent-sized corner of town, the air thick with iron, smoke, and sweat. At the main forge stood a man whose stocky, burly build didn't diminish the sense of strength around him, the body of someone who'd swung a hammer down the second he could walk and lift one. Bram never looked up from his work, and Emeric didn't want to bother him. He understood what it meant to have true focus in your craft, and he understood just as well how much it stung to get pulled out of it. So he waited, watching Bram work through the end of the day. The metallurgy was good. Emeric could still see a dozen places he'd improve it, given the tools and the go-ahead. He had a mind built to lift this whole town if someone let him.

"Do you fancy strong old men, boy?" Bram said, stoic enough that the line still somehow got a laugh out of him.

Emeric nearly lost it. "No, sir. I like women."

Bram's mouth twitched, the smirk of a man who made it a personal challenge to be amused by anything. "Guessing you're a noble of some kind. I don't have all day, so what do you want? You've been watching me all day. I worked extra hard hoping you'd leave, damn near burned myself stalling. If it's all the same to you, I don't have anything to sell, and I'm not buying. Something needs fixing, bring it here, and I'll sort it."

"Straight to the point. I like that. I think I'm going to like you."

"Rylan, is he sure he doesn't like me?"

"No, sir. From what I know, His Highness likes women. If he didn't, there's a good chance we wouldn't be here." Rylan said it with the same straight-faced respect he'd give actual royalty, which somehow made it funnier.

"If there's a god, he's on my side today," Emeric said.

"Watch your mouth, boy. The priest's a stickler for that kind of talk."

"Fair." Emeric let the smile drop, just enough to signal he meant what came next. "Let's get to it, then. I've got a request, and I'm hoping you'll hear me out before you decide what to think of me. I could tell you I'm not like other nobles and that you should trust me, but you and I both know talk's cheap. I'm not here for your trust today. I'm here because I need something, and you're the only person in this town who can get it for me.

"The priest keeps the parish records, births and deaths both. I want to see them, going back as far as they go. I'm trying to find the shape of this plague, when it started, who it's taken, whether there's a pattern nobody's had reason to look for. I can't walk up to a priest I've never met and ask for his death ledger. He's got no reason to hand it over, and every reason not to. You do. Rylan tells me if you vouch for a man in this town, people listen. I'm hoping that includes the priest.

"I won't pretend I've earned that from you. I haven't. I just got here, and I know exactly what I look like walking around with a title and no track record. I'm not going to offer you money; I don't think you'd take it. All I've got is this: the kingdom doesn't look twice at towns like this one, and either nobody's reporting what's happening here or somebody's making sure they don't. I want to fix that, starting with knowing what actually killed who and when. I can't do that without those records, and I can't get those records without you."

Bram set the hammer down slow, studying him the way he'd study a piece of metal he wasn't sure had a flaw in it yet. Most nobles who wanted something from a smith didn't ask; they told, and didn't explain themselves to a commoner either way. This one had just spent two minutes doing both.

"I'm not vouching for you," Bram said finally. "Not yet. I'll walk you to the priest myself and tell him you're worth hearing out. What he does after that's his business, and what this town thinks of you after that is yours to earn, not mine to hand you." He picked the hammer back up, already half-turned back to the forge. "Don't make me regret the walk."

Rylan caught Emeric's eye behind Bram's back and gave the smallest possible nod, the kind that said that went about as well as it could have gone.

Neither of them said much on the walk out of the forge district. Rylan seemed to sense that Emeric had spent whatever words he had for the day, and didn't try to fill the space with any of his own. The light was already sliding toward evening, throwing shadows further than they'd reached that morning, and the town seemed to be settling into itself the way it did before dark, less a place with people in it than a held breath waiting to be let out. Emeric let the quiet sit. It felt, for the first time since arriving, like something he didn't have to solve.

The walk back to the castle happened at proper dusk, the kind of light that made every doorway look one shade darker than it should. The pull found Emeric one last time on the road up from the town, stronger than any of the earlier passes, close enough that he actually turned and looked, really looked, at the wall running along the road's edge. Nothing. A gap in the stonework, an old drainage cut, shadow doing what shadow does at that hour.

Then Ward stepped out of a side alley thirty feet ahead of them, waiting at the gatehouse like he'd been standing there the whole afternoon, forearm still wrapped, expression giving away nothing at all.

Emeric stopped walking again. He looked at Ward, then back at the wall he'd just been staring at, then at Ward again, and something in his engineer's brain quietly filed the three data points into the same column for the first time. The pull hadn't been random. It had a direction, and the direction had, every single time, pointed somewhere Ward could plausibly have been standing.

"You were on the roofline back there," Emeric said. It wasn't a question.

Ward didn't confirm it. He didn't deny it either. "You're back at the castle before full dark. That's the part that matters."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the answer you're getting tonight." A flicker of something crossed Ward's face, not quite a smile, closer to the specific satisfaction of a job done clean. "Rest, Your Highness. Tomorrow's going to want you sharper than today did."

Emeric let it go, for now, but he didn't stop turning it over on the walk up the last stretch of road. He had a mind that didn't leave a real, repeatable pattern alone once it noticed one, and this was already shaping into exactly that: not imagination, not exhaustion, something with a source and a direction that had tracked a man he now had every reason to believe could move through a town without being seen or heard. Ward's build alone didn't explain it; six and a half feet of muscle should have announced itself on a rooftop. Whatever the bracer's rune did for him under the wound, it was doing more than the fight at the campfire had shown, and Emeric added that to the list of things he intended to ask about once Ward ran out of ways to dodge the question. He didn't have a name for what he'd felt. He filed it next to the well positions and the burial ground and the shape of a plan that was finally starting to take one, and decided it could wait until it had more data behind it. Everything else tonight had earned his attention first.

It was the dead of night, and Emeric was ecstatic about the coming days. He walked back to the castle with Rylan.
