Chapter 56 - The Dead Air

Ward came down after him, which Emeric had known he would, because a man does not spend two years learning the shape of his guard's loyalty and then expect that loyalty to sit obediently on a rope at the lip of a hole while the thing it guards climbs alone into the dark. Emeric heard him on the ladder-pegs before he saw him, the deliberate unhurried descent of a large man who has decided a thing and is no longer arguing about it, and by the time Emeric had drawn breath to order him back up Ward was already level with him on the pegs, roped to the same line, his own lamp on his belt, his face set in the flat patient stubbornness of a man who has come to lose an argument in person rather than at a distance. "I know," Ward said, before Emeric could say any of it. "You're the only one who can read it. So read it out loud, and I'll do exactly what you tell me, and if you tell me to go up I'll go up, and if you go quiet I am hauling us both out whether you like it or not. That's the deal. It's the only deal. I'm not standing in the sun holding a string while you drown in something I can't see." And Emeric, who had planned to send him back and found that he did not want to, and distrusted the not-wanting and overrode it anyway, made the deal, because Ward tied to the line and obeying orders above the killing layer was in truth safer than Ward frantic at the surface, and because there was a part of him, the part the transfer had not reached, that did not want to go down alone into the thing that had killed better men than either of them.

So he did the thing he had never once been able to do for the villagers over the water, which was to make a man understand the danger before it touched him, because Ward, unlike a frightened town, had two years of watched results behind his trust and would take a strange truth on Emeric's word where a stranger would have taken it for madness. "The air down here isn't cursed," Emeric said, going down slow, watching the lamp at his belt more than the rock. "It's spent. Air isn't one thing, it's a mix, and there's a part of it your body burns the way the furnace burns charcoal, and when a deep place sits still long enough with no wind to stir it, that part gets used up and never replaced, by rot, by old timber, by the stone itself, and what's left is heavy and dead and it pools at the bottom because it's heavier than the good air above it. It sits there like water in the bottom of a cup. You can't see it and you can't smell it and it doesn't burn or bite. It just isn't air anymore, and a man who breathes it breathes nothing, and breathing nothing feels, right up until the end, exactly like being tired." He said it plainly and Ward took it plainly, and they went down, and Emeric watched the flame.

It changed forty feet lower, and he stopped them both with a hand. The lamp, which had burned clean yellow the whole way down, dimmed, not much, a guttering, a sag in the flame like a man's shoulders when the day has gone on too long, and Emeric held it out at arm's length below him and watched it sag further and drew it back up and watched it steady, and there it was, the shore of the thing, the surface of the invisible lake, marked out for him in the one language it could not hide from. "There," he said quietly. "You see the flame drop when I lower it and stand up when I raise it? That's the top of the dead air. Right there. There's a level in this shaft, like the surface of a pond, and below it is death and above it is fine, and it is the flatest, quietest, most patient death you will ever meet, because it doesn't announce itself. This is how they died. Renner's men. They came down here not knowing there was a line, and they crossed it, and the tiredness came on the way tiredness comes on after a long shift, honest and familiar, and they sat down to rest in it because resting is what you do when you're tired, and the resting killed them, and they never once knew they were being killed. They thought they were catching their breath." He looked at the sagging flame a moment longer. "The flame's a good warning here, but I don't trust it all the way, because a flame can die where a man could stand and a man can die where a flame would burn. So I've got the flame, and I've got the other thing, the sense, and I only move when they agree. Below this line, Ward, you do not go. Not for me. Not for anything. You hold here where the air's alive, and you keep the rope, and you count."

The deposit was below the line. He had known it would be, in the way he had known most of this before he arrived at it, because Renner had not paid a fortune for a survey and sunk the deepest shaft in the district to reach a level a man could stroll into, and the thing worth all that dying had settled, like the dead air itself, to the very bottom of the reach. Which meant that to see it, to know what he had, Emeric had to go into the lake. There was no fix yet, no draft, no way to drain the dead air before he touched it, because building that was days of work with a crew and he was here now with a rope and a held breath and a question he was not willing to carry back up unanswered. So he did the only thing the physics left him, and it was crude and old and it worked, and it was the exact opposite of the curse the town believed in, because it treated the killing air as a problem with a shape rather than a demon with a will. He breathed. He stood at the shore of the dead lake and filled his lungs with the living air above it, deep, again, again, flushing himself full of the good stuff the way a pearl-diver does before he goes under, and then he went under, down past the line into the dead dark holding the last good breath in his chest, working fast, his hands and his sense doing in the black what his eyes could not, and when the breath began to go he came back up over the line and hung on the rope and breathed until the world steadied, and then he went down again. He dove the dead air the way a man dives water, in measured descents with the whole of his life held in his lungs, and Ward stood at the shore above him and paid out rope and hauled it back and counted under his breath and watched his lord vanish into a nothing that had eaten a dozen men and come back up out of it, and vanish, and come back, until the counting was the only sound in the shaft and Ward's knuckles on the rope had gone white with a fear he was too disciplined to voice.

On the third dive Emeric found it, and knew it the instant his hand closed on it, and the knowing drove every held-breath discipline clean out of his head so that he nearly wasted the dive gaping in the dark at a thing he could not see and did not need to. Gravity crystal. He knew it by the sense the way you know a familiar weight in a familiar hand, the peculiar heaviness of the stone that was not heaviness at all but the faint constant leaning of the world toward it, the substance that did not merely have weight but bent weight, and there was not a seam of it but a body, a whole dense vein of it running down and away past even the sense into the deep foot of the hill. He surfaced with a fist-sized piece of it and hung on the rope and breathed and could not, for a moment, arrange his face into anything Ward would find reassuring, because his mind had already left the shaft entirely and gone racing off across every plan he had made in two years and every plan he had shelved as impossible for want of exactly this. He knew what gravity crystal was worth. Everyone knew, which was the whole point, it was rare and it sold like nothing else sold, a stone the great houses hoarded and the tower rationed and no barony in the district had ever owned a hand's weight of, and here was a vein of it deep enough to make Grayrock, alone, richer than the three baronies together were now. That was the number a merchant would see. Emeric saw the number and set it aside for a truth the market had never priced, which was that gravity crystal was not merely wealth, it was lift, it was the one substance that let a heavy thing be made to weigh what you chose, and every design he had ever drawn and buried because it could not fly on air alone had just come up out of a dead man's cursed hole in his own closed fist. He did not say any of that to Ward. He said, "It's here," and, "it's what he was after," and let the rest of it burn quietly behind his eyes on the long climb up.

It was on that climb, with the good air thickening around him again and the lamp standing tall, that the colder thought he had shelved in Bryda's hall finished itself and would not be shelved a second time. A worked-out shaft you abandon. You leave the timbers to rot and the story to fade and within a generation no one remembers there was a hole there at all. But this shaft had not been left to fade. It had been sealed, and storied, and the story kept alive, deliberately, the curse fed and tended like a fire someone wanted burning, and a man does not guard the memory of an empty grave. Renner had known what was down here. Renner had known it was worth all that dying and had kept it hidden not out of fear but out of possession, and a broke provincial baron does not survey a deep vein of the rarest stone in the kingdom on his own coin and his own knowledge, because he does not have the coin and he does not have the knowledge, and the survey Bryda had handed Emeric in a strapped bundle of a dead man's papers had been paid for by someone, and taught to Renner by someone, and that someone had wanted this vein controlled and quiet and had chosen a hungry baron on the right ground to keep it so. Emeric did not have a name. He had the shape of a hand, reaching from somewhere up and out of these hills, and he carried the shape of it up into the light and told himself, correctly, that it could wait for Ferra and the papers and a quiet evening, and told himself, incorrectly, that he had time.

They came up into a grey noon and a crowd, which Emeric had not expected and should have. Word that the strange prince had gone down the cursed shaft had run through Grayrock the way water runs downhill, and the miners had gathered at a careful distance from the head-frame to watch him not come back, because that was the only ending the shaft had ever given, and when he came back instead, filthy and alive and climbing out under his own power with his guard behind him, the distance closed. It closed the way the village over the water had closed once he had walked into the thing they feared and out the other side of it, because a town does not believe a lord's words and a town cannot argue with a lord's body, standing there in the sun, out of the hole that killed their sons. He had earned something in that shaft that no decree could have bought him, and he knew it, and he knew too, in the same cold accounting, what it cost. Every man in that crowd would tell the story, and the story would travel, and it would reach, eventually, the ears of men who were already telling themselves that Emeric of Kaldo could not be met on any ground and could not be caught by any law and would therefore have to be broken by other means, and every impossible thing he did to win a town added one more stone to the weight those men were gathering to drop on him. His competence kept purchasing him the exact danger it was meant to hold off. He had no answer to that. He was not sure there was one.

The fix he gave them before he left, plainly, in daylight, the way he gave everything he wanted to outlive him. He would not have Grayrock reopen the deep by sending brave men to breathe held air in the dark, because the whole of his standard, the thing he had crossed a world still carrying, was that nothing he built should need him standing in it to work. So he told them how to make the mine breathe. You did not fight the dead air. You moved it, and you moved it with the cheapest engine there was, which was fire. Cut a second shaft, or open the old winze, so the deep had two mouths and not one. Then light a fire at the foot of one of them, and keep it fed, and the hot air over the fire would rise and rush up its shaft and pull, and the pull would drag fresh living air down the other mouth to replace what left, and the whole dead lake would be set moving and drained and kept drained for as long as the fire burned. No mana in it. No stone in it. No prince in it. A fire, two holes, and a man to feed the fire, and any crew in the district could build it and any crew could run it and copy it into the next mine and the next, and the cursed shaft would become, within a season, the deepest working and the richest in Grayrock and no more haunted than a chimney. He drew it twice in the dirt for the mine captain and made him draw it back, because a thing a man could not draw was a thing a man did not yet have, and only when the captain drew it clean did Emeric let it go.

He went back to Bryda's hall to tell her what her curse had turned out to be worth, and found, watching her take the news, that he had been misreading her holding for two years the way he misread most rooms, and that the error this time was worth correcting. She did not gasp at the fortune or grasp at it. She sat with it, turned it, and inside a minute was three questions deep into the practicalities of it, who would hold the extraction rights and how the crown's share would be reckoned and whether a windfall this size would draw exactly the wrong attention to a town that had spent three years learning to want no attention at all, and every question was the right question, asked in the right order, by a mind that had clearly been asking such questions for years with no one on the far side of the table who thought a woman's questions worth answering. He had known she was competent. He had priced her, lazily, as a good caretaker, a widow minding a chair until a son grew into it, because that was the shape Grayrock presented and he had not looked past the shape. He looked past it now. Grayrock had not merely survived Renner's death and the loss of its war-band and the ruin of its treasury. It had been governed through all of that, quietly, competently, by someone, and the someone was not the dead man and was not a ghost, it was the woman sitting across from him asking better questions about her own fortune than his own stewards would have asked about it.

"Renner never let you work," he said. It was not a question. She went still in the particular way of a person hearing a true thing said aloud by someone who was not supposed to have seen it, and then, because she was not a woman who lied when a plain answer would do, she told him the shape of it. Renner had not been cruel. She was careful to say that, and Emeric believed her, because she had no reason left to defend him and said it anyway. He had given her a grand life, a good hall, fine things, a daughter he doted on in his rough way, everything a lord was supposed to give a wife and nothing a lord was supposed to withhold. He had simply believed, all the way down where a man's beliefs are load-bearing and cannot be argued out of him, that there were things a man handled and things a woman handled, and that the ledgers and the veins and the dealings of the barony were a man's to carry, and hers to be kept comfortable and clear of, and he had kept her comfortable and clear of them with a generosity that never once occurred to him was a wall. She had run the household flawlessly and been praised for it and had watched him mismanage the barony into the ground with a clarity she was never permitted to speak, and there is a specific starvation in that, Emeric thought, that no comfort touches, because a grand life given to a person built to do work is water poured over a thirst it cannot reach, and gold means nothing to a man dying of thirst in a desert, and it had meant nothing to her either, though she had never in her life said so to anyone and plainly did not know she was about to.

"Work for me," he said. "Not mind Grayrock. Govern it. In your own name, by my charter, Steward of Grayrock and answerable to me and to no man in this district, and when I trust the work, and I will, because I already trust the questions, the uplands beyond it too. I have three baronies and one of me, and the part of the job I am worst at, the part I get wrong and know I get wrong, is reading a holding and reading the people in it and governing them well while I go off and build. You do that. You have done it for years with your hands tied and no name on it and no thanks for it. I would take the rope off and put the name on and pay you what the name is worth." He watched her weigh it, and watched the one real weight come up in her face, and answered it before she raised it, because he had not forgotten the child even if Renner's world would have expected him to. "Your daughter stays where your daughter is. You govern from Grayrock, from this hall, in the town she was born in. I am not asking you to choose between the work and the child. Renner's world made you choose, and it chose for you, and it chose wrong. I am telling you that you can have both, because there is no reason on God's earth you cannot, and the only thing that ever said otherwise was a rule a dead man believed and I do not." She was quiet a long moment, the practical woman running the practical sum, and Emeric, who could not read her and had given up pretending he could, waited. Then she said yes, plainly, the way she said everything, and something in her that had been sitting folded up small for a very long time began, visibly, to stand.
