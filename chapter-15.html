Chapter 15 - The Shape of It

Ferra didn't scream. In the half-second she'd had to expect anything, she'd expected she might, and what came instead was quieter and more useful to her. Her hands went to Bram's wrist first, then Emeric's, feeling for a pulse the way her mother had taught her before anyone had taught her numbers. Both steady. Both breathing, slow and even, neither of them home behind his own eyes.

"Bram." Nothing. "Emeric." Nothing.

She'd watched a man go still like this once before. A dock worker who'd taken a blow to the head and spent three days only half inside himself before he came back wrong and stayed that way. She was already sorting through what she remembered of it, keep them upright, keep the airway clear, don't shake them too hard, when the both of them blinked at once, and the room came back into their eyes like a tide coming in.

"What," she said, "was that?"

Bram looked at his own hands as if to confirm they still answered to him. "I don't rightly know how to answer that."

"Try."

Emeric opened his mouth and did the thing she'd learned, over months of watching him talk to people who weren't ready for it, that he did when he mistook a clear explanation for a reassuring one. He started in on delving spaces and mental workrooms and the Hollow Crystal as some manner of address system, precise and technical and worth exactly nothing to a woman whose father had just gone unresponsive at his own kitchen table for reasons that sentence did nothing whatsoever to explain.

"Stop," Ferra said, and he stopped. "Is he hurt."

"No."

"Are you hurt?"

"No. Neither of us. I promise you that part first. I should have led with that part."

She sat down hard in the chair she'd been standing over for however long the whole thing had actually taken. Her hands weren't steady yet, and she let them not be, which was its own kind of tell in a woman who took pride in being steady about everything.

Emeric watched her a moment, gone still the way he did when he was reworking a sum that hadn't come out. He'd led with the mechanism because the mechanism was the part he understood, and understanding it was the thing that had made it safe for him. It hadn't crossed his mind, not until just now, watching her hands, that explaining the machine did nothing at all for the fear sitting under it. He put that away the way he put away most of his misses lately. Quietly. Without talking himself out of it.

"I want to show you," he said instead. "Rather than tell you. I think it'll make more sense that way, and I think you'll trust it more once you've seen it than you'd trust any version of me trying to describe it."

"Show me where. Show me what."

"The same place Bram just came back from." He hesitated, and let her see him hesitate instead of hiding it. "I don't fully know the rules yet. I've done this exactly once, an hour ago, with him. I won't pretend that makes me sure of anything. But I don't think it's dangerous, and I think you'd rather see the thing for yourself than be told it's safe."

Ferra looked at her father. Bram gave her a single nod, the nod of a man standing behind something he didn't fully understand and trusted anyway. That, more than a word Emeric had said, seemed to settle it in her.

"Fine," she said. "Show me."

He reached for her the way he'd reached for Bram without quite meaning to, thinking of her the way he'd thought of the furnace. Present. Invited. Wanted here. The room pulled back at the edges the way it had before, and then it wasn't pulled back at all. It was simply somewhere else.

She stood in the same half-built office, the furnace still standing where he and Bram had left it, solid and finished and strange enough that she circled it once, slowly, before she said anything. Bram had reacted to the mechanism, to the angle of the air line and the shape of the melt chamber. Ferra reacted to something else entirely, standing dead still in front of a machine that existed, whole, before a single part of it had gone into a fire.

"It's already done," she said. "All of it. Before anyone's touched metal."

"That's the idea. Bram gets to see what he's building before he builds it."

"That's not what I mean." She turned to look at him, and there was something sharper than wonder in her face, something closer to recognition. "I mean it's already organized. Every piece knows where it goes. Nothing's sitting there waiting to be sorted."

He hadn't thought of it in those words, but she wasn't wrong, and the thought that came behind hers arrived the way the furnace had, less like a flash of inspiration than a door he was realizing had been unlocked the whole time. If the space could hold a machine that didn't exist yet, laid out and complete, there was no clear reason it couldn't hold a stack of ledgers the same way.

"Your work," he said. "Do you want to try it?"

She didn't answer in words. She reached out toward nothing, the way he'd reached for a furnace that wasn't there, thinking of the census gap and the five years of tithe records she'd promised to pull and the figures that hadn't matched what Bram would have used to guess how many chairs to drag in.

The ledgers came the way the furnace had, resolving out of the space around her, except where his had come slow and taken effort, hers came fast, pages of figures hanging in ordered ranks like something built for exactly this kind of hand. She reached, and a column sorted itself by year without her touching a page. Another split itself by the size of the discrepancy, the largest gaps drifting to the front.

"This isn't reading it for me," she said, half to herself, drawing a finger down a column that shifted at her attention like it had been waiting on the question. "It's not telling me what it means. It's only, everything's finally where I can look at it at once, instead of one page at a time."

"That sounds right," Emeric said. "I don't think it can think for you. I think it can just get out of your way faster than paper ever could."

She went quiet after that, moving through the ranked columns, and for a while she seemed to forget entirely that any of this was strange. He left her to it, the way Bram had left him to the furnace, understanding that this particular silence wasn't distance. It was someone finally doing the thing she was best at with nothing in the road slowing her down.

"There's a pattern," she said at last, not looking up. "The gap isn't random. And it's not spread even across the years, either. It clusters. Worse in some seasons than others."

"Can you tell what's causing it?"

"Not yet." She said it without any heat, the flat report of someone giving an honest status and not confessing a failure. "This shows me the shape of it faster than I'd have found the shape in a month of paper. It doesn't hand me the reason. I still have to go find that the old way."

That was, he thought, exactly how it should be. He didn't say so. He only watched her keep at it, faster now, sharper now, still entirely herself.
