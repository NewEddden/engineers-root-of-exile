The Hollow Crystal hadn't left his side since he'd arrived in Kaldo. It sat under his pillow at night, in his coat pocket during the day, close enough that he'd stopped noticing the weight of it the way he'd stopped noticing his own heartbeat. His mother had given it to him. His brother had given him the other ones at the banquet, meant as an insult, dressed as nostalgia. Neither had ever done anything. Every mage who'd looked at it had called it worthless, and Emeric had believed them, the way you believe a doctor about a joint that's ached your whole life. Not because you checked. Because nobody's ever told you otherwise.

He didn't dream about his mother often. When he did, it was never the assassination. It was smaller than that: her hands over his on a practice rune-slate, the smell of fireplace in winter, her voice correcting his grip without any heat in it. Tonight was one of those nights. Not a nightmare. A memory his mind had decided to visit on its own, unprompted, the crystal warm against his palm where his hand had curled around it in his sleep the way it did most nights without him deciding to.

He didn't feel himself cross into anything. There was no threshold, no falling sensation, no announcement that something had started. One moment he was somewhere that felt like his mother's room. The next, underneath that memory, laid over it like a second exposure on the same photograph, there was structure. Lines. Logic sitting where logic had no business sitting inside a dream.

He came awake at some point without remembering the transition, and the crystal was still warm.

It took him a full minute lying in the dark to understand what had just happened wasn't a dream at all, not really. It was months of nights just like this one, the stone against his skin every single time, finally adding up to something. He hadn't done anything different tonight. That was the part that mattered. Whatever had just happened hadn't happened because of tonight. Tonight was just the night the total crossed whatever line it needed to cross.

He sat with that for a while, turning the stone over in the dark. It had a slight heat to it, like tech that had just been used. He decided he wasn't delving again, not yet, just thinking about what it meant that the door had been unlocked the entire time and he'd spent this long assuming it was a wall.

The next night was uneventful, the way every night in this cold room was uneventful. He lay there doing what he usually did before sleep took him, running through the day's problems and, when the problems ran out, missing things he'd stopped letting himself miss out loud. Televisions. Central heating. A comforter that actually held heat instead of just weighing him down. Fire crystals could solve the heating problem easily enough if he wanted to spend the effort keeping one active all night, which he didn't. The second he let his focus slip, it went cold again, no different from him personally holding a space heater switched on with his own hand. He'd turned over the idea of training something to cast on its own while he slept and dismissed it just as fast. That was a project for a version of him with time to burn, not this one.

Eventually his thoughts wore thin enough to let him sleep.

This time, he went looking for the door on purpose.

He didn't wait for a memory to carry him there. He held the stone the way he had every night for the month he'd been in this barony. Without thinking about it, instead of letting his mind wander, he pointed it deliberately, the way he'd point a flashlight down a hallway he already knew had something in it. Show me what you are.

Nothing happened, for what felt like a long time. Then the same layering sensation from before, structure underneath awareness, except this time nothing was covering it. No memory, no dream doing the work of getting him there. Just the stone, and whatever the stone actually was, sitting directly in front of him with nothing between them.

It didn't look like anything, not in any way his eyes would have recognized. It felt the way reading unfamiliar code used to feel, before the pattern clicked: a wall of syntax that resolved, slowly, into shapes he almost recognized. Not commands. Not exactly. Something underneath commands, structure that things could be built from, waiting for someone to actually build.

He found what felt like a branch. A fork, one path or another, depending on something, though he couldn't yet tell what that something was, what condition the fork was checking. He pressed against it anyway, carefully, the way you'd test a door you didn't know was locked, and felt it hold, unmoved, entirely unbothered by his pressing. Not resistance. Not rejection either. It simply didn't respond to force, the same way a variable doesn't respond to being shouted at, only to being addressed correctly.

Conditional logic, he thought, and the thought arrived with a kind of vertigo he hadn't expected, half excitement and half something closer to grief. This thing thinks in if-then. It's not a crystal. It's not even really a rune, not the way every rune he'd been shown so far was a rune. It was closer to something that had never had a right to exist in a world like this one at all, and every mage who'd ever held it and called it worthless had been telling the truth about their own instruments, not about the stone.

He found another shape near the first, something that repeated, looping back on itself in a way that felt deliberate rather than accidental, though he couldn't tell yet what it repeated for, what it was counting or waiting on. He didn't touch that one. He'd already learned, from the one branch he had touched, that understanding a shape and understanding what it was for were not the same thing, and he had exactly one of those tonight.

He stayed there, turning the two shapes over from a careful distance, the way he'd have stared at a schematic in a language he was only starting to learn to read, cataloguing what he recognized without yet trying to use any of it. Branch. Loop, probably. Somewhere underneath both of them, likely, whatever held state, whatever remembered anything between one use and the next. He didn't find that tonight. He didn't know yet that "tonight" was even the right way to think about how long he'd been in here.

Time, inside whatever this was, didn't announce itself the way it did outside. He didn't decide to leave. He simply became aware, at some point, that he was lying in his bed again, the crystal loose in his hand, morning light doing the same thing to the window frame it did every morning in this house that didn't quite fit anymore.

He lay there for a while before moving, running back through what he'd seen, or felt, or whatever the right verb was for a sense that had no name yet in either language he thought in. A branch. A loop. No idea yet what fed either one, no idea what the whole structure was actually for. But it was there. It was real, and it was his, and it had been sitting under his pillow every night since he'd arrived in this town, waiting on nothing but time.

He had, he realized, just found the one tool in this entire world built by someone who thought the way he did.

Hollow Crystal wasn't the right word anymore, and he was done pretending it was, even just in his own head. Hollow implied nothing was there. This had been full the entire time, waiting on someone who knew how to look. He didn't have anything close to a real name for it, not one earned by understanding rather than metaphor, but he needed something better than an insult wearing a description, if only for his own thinking. Control Rune. Not because it controlled anything he understood yet, but because everything he'd found so far, the branch, the shape that might be a loop, felt built to be directed rather than simply drawn from, the way every other rune in this world seemed to work. It was a working name, not a final one. It would do until it earned something better.

Morning came, and it came with a list already half-written in his head before his feet hit the floor.

He didn't tell Ward. He didn't tell Rylan. He told Bram, because Bram was the only person in Kaldo he trusted with something that sounded, out loud, like heresy.

Bram took it the way Bram took most things Emeric said to him now, with the stillness of a man deciding whether to be alarmed. "You're telling me the tower's been wrong. About the whole rune type."

"I'm telling you I don't know yet. I know something happened. I don't know what it means, or what it can do, or whether I can do it again on purpose." Emeric turned the Control Rune in his fingers, plain and gray and inert-looking, the same as it had looked every day since his mother handed it to him. "I need somewhere quiet to try it again, awake this time, and I need you to not repeat this to anyone until I know what I'm actually holding."

"Wasn't planning on repeating heresy to the priest, no." Bram's mouth didn't move much, but something in his eyes did. "You've got that same look you had over the ledgers."

"Feels the same. Bigger, maybe."

Bram didn't ask how much bigger. That was one of the things Emeric had come to rely on without meaning to: the man's refusal to ask questions he couldn't yet use the answer to.

The metal problem hadn't waited politely for the Control Rune to finish being strange.

"We buy our iron from Grayrock," Bram said, the two of them walking the smithy yard while his apprentices banked the forge for the night. "Have for years. A little copper too, when their smiths have surplus to spare. Nothing local worth digging for, not since before the war, so it comes down the same road the grain does."

"How reliable is that supply?"

"Reliable enough, if you're not particular about the price. Grayrock knows we've got nowhere else to buy from. Charges accordingly."

Emeric had expected some version of that answer before he asked the question, which didn't make it sit any better. A single supplier with no competition was its own kind of fragility, the sort of thing he'd have flagged in a production line without a second thought. "I don't like this depending on one town's goodwill. Not for something this size."

"Then you'd better hope Grayrock stays friendly, because there's nowhere else." Bram shrugged, the shrug of a man who'd made peace with a fact years before Emeric arrived to find it inconvenient. "For the farm work specifically, what am I actually going to need built?"

"Depends what you're building."

"Pipe. A lot of it. Fittings, joints, something better than what the town's using now, which is mostly nothing. And whatever a proper irrigation system needs once we're past hauling buckets by hand." He'd done the walk himself, twice now, along the floodplain the town's fields sat on, and the arithmetic wasn't complicated even without good instruments. Call it two thousand acres actually being worked right now, less than half of what the land could support if there were hands enough to work it, the rest sitting fallow for lack of people rather than lack of soil. Whatever pipe network got built had to run from a clean draw point upriver, past the contaminated stretch entirely, down through the worked fields in branches instead of one long spine, so a break in one line didn't take out the whole system. No single points of failure. He'd learned that lesson somewhere that wasn't this world, and it hadn't stopped being true just because the world had changed.

"That's a great deal of metal for one field."

"It's not for one field. I could grow one field myself; magic makes that easy, cheap, almost boring. It's not sustainable, and it's not what this barony needs from me. It needs a system somebody else can run after I've moved on to the next fire. Pipe. Fittings. Something that doesn't need a prince standing in the field for it to work."

Bram was quiet for a moment, the specific quiet Emeric had learned meant he was recalculating scope, not doubting the plan. "That's smith's work for months, not days. I can't do that and keep the shop running for the town's regular orders both."

"I know. That's why I'm not asking you as a favor."

Ferra was at the table when he said it, which he hadn't planned, but didn't mind either.

She had a ledger open in front of her, the household kind, not the town's, and she wasn't looking up from it when Emeric sat down across from Bram that evening. He'd learned by now that she was listening to everything said in that house, whether she looked up or not.

"Bram." Emeric didn't dress it up, the same way he hadn't dressed up the farming pitch weeks earlier. "I want to hire you exclusively, long-term, for the pipe and fitting work the farm's going to need. That's already on the table. What I'm asking now is separate. I want to hire Ferra too. Not to help you. On her own terms, doing her own work."

Bram's cup paused the way it had the first time Emeric had asked him for something large. "Doing what work, exactly?"

"Records. Contracts. Whatever this operation turns into, it's going to generate more paper than either of us can track by memory: who owes who, who delivered what and when. I've seen what she does with numbers already. That's not a smith's skill. It's its own skill, and it's one I need."

Ferra looked up from the ledger for the first time.

"You've seen what I do with numbers," she repeated, not quite a question.

"Your father mentioned the household accounts balance to the copper every month without you ever writing anything twice. That's not luck." Emeric nodded at the ledger in front of her. "What's the total on that page right now? Don't add it up first. Just look at it and tell me."

She glanced down, a beat, no longer than it took most people to read a single line. "Fourteen silver, six copper, assuming the fish-monger's third entry is a five and not a three. His hand's bad this week." She looked back up at him, something sharp behind the flatness of it, daring him to check her math instead of just believing it.

Emeric did check it. It took him longer than the beat she'd used, and it came out to fourteen silver, six copper.

"That's the skill," he said. "That's exactly the skill."

Bram set his cup down slower than he'd picked it up. "You're asking my permission first."

"You're her father. It matters to me that you're asked, not informed." Emeric meant it plainly, the way he'd meant most things since arriving here that hadn't landed the way he'd intended. This one, he thought, might land right. "But I'm not hiring you on her behalf either. If you say yes and she says no, that's still no."

Bram looked at his daughter for a long moment. Whatever passed between them, Emeric wasn't equipped to read fully, but he didn't need to read all of it to see the shape of a father deciding his daughter was old enough that his answer wasn't really the one that mattered.

"I've got no objection," Bram said finally. "Ask her, then."

Emeric turned to Ferra. "Same offer, direct. Records, contracts, order-tracking, for whatever this becomes. Paid work, your terms on how, exclusive if you want it that way or not if you don't."

Ferra closed the ledger, unhurried, the same unhurried she'd used pouring his tea the first time they'd met without a word. "Exclusive," she said. "I don't do half-measures on paperwork I actually respect." A pause, and something almost like amusement under it. "You checked my math just now. Nobody's ever bothered checking it before. They just believed me or didn't."

"I don't believe things I can check. No offense meant by it."

"None taken. I'd have thought less of you if you'd just nodded along."

Petram had said three real things since the fire, and two of them were about the weather. The third came the week after the crystal breakthrough, dragged loose more by boredom than by anything Emeric had done to earn it: a name half-swallowed, something about coin moving somewhere it shouldn't, and then nothing more no matter how the questions got angled after that. It wasn't a confession. It wasn't even really an accusation, not one that would hold up to anyone determined to argue with it. But it was a thread, and Emeric had learned by now that a thread was worth more than it looked like on the day he found it. Wexler's name hadn't come up specifically. It hadn't needed to. There weren't many hands in this barony with enough reach to move coin anywhere at all.

The letter went to the castle steward's office two mornings later, not to Wexler directly, though both of them knew exactly whose desk it would land on within the hour.

Emeric had written it three times before he was satisfied. The first draft had sounded like a request. The second had sounded like an accusation. The third, the one that went out under his seal, sounded like neither, a standard inspection of barony revenue and expenditure ledgers, the kind of oversight any crown-resident royal was entitled to as a matter of course, phrased with the flat procedural boredom of something that happened all the time and needed no justification because it had never needed one before. Nothing in it asked. Nothing in it accused. It informed, the way a tax notice informs, and that was deliberate. A baron could sit on a request. Sitting on an instruction with a prince's seal on it, in front of a steward who'd then have to explain the delay to someone, was a different kind of exposure entirely.

Wexler received him in the same room as before, and this time he didn't wait for Emeric to sit before he spoke.

"Ledgers," he said, turning the letter over once in his hand without reading it again, since he plainly already had. "You've decided you need my books now."

"Crown books. You administer them, they're not yours." Emeric kept his voice at the same register he'd used explaining the water survey, flat, unhurried, a fact stated rather than a position defended. "It's not a large request. Revenue in, expenditure out, the last two years. Standard practice."

"Standard practice." Wexler set the letter down on the table between them, precise, like he was placing a piece on a board rather than discarding a piece of paper. "For a prince who intends to stay resident. Is that what you're telling me you intend?"

It wasn't really a question about the ledgers anymore, and Emeric understood that even without being able to name exactly what it had become instead. "I'm telling you I'd like to understand the barony I'm living in. That's all this is."

"That's all a great many things are, right up until they aren't." Wexler studied him for a moment, the same recalculating stillness from two nights before, except sharper now, like a man checking a total he'd already run once and didn't like landing on the same number twice. "You'll have your ledgers, Your Highness. I've no grounds to refuse a request that isn't, in form, a request at all. You wrote it carefully. I noticed that."

"I try to."

"I'm sure you do." Wexler didn't stand this time, which Emeric registered without yet knowing what to do with it, whether it meant less concern or simply less need to perform concern in front of him now. "You'll find the barony's accounts in poorer shape than you might expect for its size. I'd ask that you keep whatever conclusions you draw from that to yourself, until you've understood why, rather than simply that."

"I don't draw conclusions from numbers I haven't seen yet."

"No," Wexler said, something unreadable moving behind the flatness for just a moment. "I don't imagine you do."

The ledgers arrived two days after that, carried up by a page who clearly wanted no part of whatever was in them, four bound volumes and a scattering of loose receipts that looked like they'd been gathered in a hurry rather than pulled from an organized archive.

Emeric spread them across his desk that night and started where he'd started with the parish records: sorting before reading, structure before content. It didn't take long to see the shape of the problem, only long enough to be sure he wasn't imagining it. A barony this size, this population, running the numbers Wexler's own stewards had recorded, should not have been sitting this close to the margin. The tithe income was there. The expenditure was accounted for, mostly. What wasn't there was the gap between the two, a difference that should have existed and didn't, not stolen exactly, nothing so clean as a single missing line he could point to, just a slow, distributed thinness spread across a hundred small entries that each looked defensible enough on their own.

He didn't have an answer yet. He had a shape, the same kind of shape the plague map had started as, dots without a cause attached to them.

He added a line to the list he'd started that first cold night at the writing desk. Six items had become nine now, and none of the earlier ones had gotten any smaller for it.

That was fine. That was how it always started.
