After the attempted assassination, time flew like a runaway train. Emeric and the remaining guards had made it to town. Emeric was never the type to care for a parade of arrival, but how did the baron of this place know that? Most nobles knew proper etiquette when someone higher on the hierarchy came around. Emeric wondered if the baron had decided not to come greet him out of sheer disrespect, or if he simply hadn't gotten the memo.

Four of them left now, and Petram besides, bound and silent in the back of the carriage where nobody had to look at him directly to know he was there. Rylan drove, quiet in a way that hadn't been there before the fire. Declan and Osric rode close flank instead of their usual loose formation, eyes on the treeline more than the road. Ward rode nearest the carriage door, forearm wrapped tight under his sleeve, jaw set the way it got when he wasn't going to mention the pain and wasn't going to be talked into resting it either. Nobody had said much of anything since the fire had burned down that night.

Emeric hadn't slept right since. He kept catching himself running the same diagnostic loop, the kind of thing his old engineer's brain did to problems it hadn't solved yet: input, failure point, root cause, and every time it came back to the same unsatisfying answer. He'd frozen. Not for long, not completely, but the freeze itself hadn't been the point. The point was that something in him still worked exactly the way it had at seven years old, and he hadn't known that until it nearly cost a guard his life, the same guard now riding one-armed a few feet from the carriage because Emeric hadn't moved fast enough.

Kaldo announced itself before he saw it, the smell arriving first, and there was nothing faint about it. Woodsmoke first, then under it something sweeter and worse, the kind of rot that clung to the back of the throat, the smell of a burial ground too close to a well and latrines that had never been dug anywhere sensible. Emeric had read about this once, medieval sanitation, disease vectors, and always as history, something solved a century before he was born. Standing downwind of it as a lived condition instead of a paragraph in a textbook was a different thing entirely.

Then the walls, low and old, patched in places with stone that didn't match, the kind of repair work done by people who couldn't afford to do it properly and had stopped waiting for someone else to. The gate guard who waved them through looked less like a soldier and more like a man who'd been handed a spear and told to stand somewhere.

After Ward confirmed who they were there to see, they were guided to the castle. Small was generous. It had been built for a barony twice this size, back when Kaldo still had a working port, and men who weren't dead or conscripted, and the gap between what it was built to hold and what it actually held now showed in every corner of it. The drawbridge chain had been replaced with rope at some point, thick enough to hold, ugly enough that nobody had planned on it being permanent. A section of the outer wall sagged where water had gotten in behind the stone and never dried out properly, the same patched-not-fixed logic as the town wall, just higher up and better hidden from anyone who wasn't looking for it.

Inside wasn't much better. The entry hall was sized for a household staff that didn't exist anymore, half the torch brackets empty, footsteps echoing in a way that told him more about how few people worked here than any account book would. A steward met them, thin, tired-looking, doing the job of what should have been three people. He didn't apologize for the state of things. He didn't need to. It was written into the walls already. A corridor that, at one point, held ornate art on both sides led to the office of the current baron.

"Baron Kaldo, I presume," Emeric said, with doubt already in it. Kaldo was a knight, and even generations down the line, his lineage should still have carried that knightly air. Knights raised the way Kaldo was would indirectly take that discipline home. No way this was one of his descendants.

The man behind the desk didn't stand, and didn't correct him quickly enough for it to read as anything but deliberate. Whatever he'd been about to say, he let the pause do the talking first, letting a prince stand there being wrong for a beat longer than manners required.

"Wexler," he said finally, not rising, not offering a hand. "Baron Wexler. Kaldo's the land, Your Highness, not my name." A pause, just long enough to correct, felt less like courtesy and more like a small, private win. "The line hasn't run blood-to-blood since old Kal himself. No children of his own, so he handed it to the man he trusted most instead of a son he never had. My family kept the name we came with. Kaldo kept ours." Something in the way he said it suggested he'd corrected other people the same way, more than once, and never once minded doing it.

The disdain wasn't in his face. It was in how little effort he spent hiding it, the specific laziness of a man who'd decided this visitor wasn't worth the performance of respect, only the minimum required not to be called insolent to his face. Emeric, being a man of two worlds, didn't take offense to this. Baron Wexler was senior, and for the longest time, Emeric had been known as a waste of a prince. It was only fair that people treated him like he was less than. Even if he'd made note of it, it would only have caused unnecessary friction. On the off chance he decided this baron had to die, he'd be seen as a tyrant. He had just arrived, and blood would be spilled already; Emeric didn't know what the town's reaction would be.

After exchanging wasted pleasantries, Emeric was escorted to his room by the steward he'd met before. The room was nothing special, but it would do. By the end of the day, everything in the carriage had been put into the room, and Emeric headed to sleep; he had big plans for the morning.

The morning came, sunlight peering through a window that didn't quite fit its frame anymore. "Damn, we're really not in the castle anymore," Emeric said with a sigh, and didn't waste any more time on it than that. He ate standing, whatever the steward's kitchen had managed on short notice, and went looking for Rylan before he'd finished the last of it.

"You said you hadn't seen your family in four months," Emeric said. "Take me with you."

Rylan blinked at him as he'd misheard. "Your Highness, I can go alone, there's no need---"

"There's every need. I haven't seen the town. You have a reason to walk through it that isn't me inspecting anything." It wasn't entirely honest, and Rylan's face said he knew it wasn't, but he didn't argue further.

Ward didn't like it. He said as much with a look rather than words, then said the rest with actual words when the look didn't land the way he wanted.

"You're not walking through an unmapped town with one guard and no title anyone here respects," Ward said.

"Rylan's enough."

"Rylan's a father going home to his family. He's not watching the street behind you while he does it, and he shouldn't have to be." Ward's jaw was doing the thing it did when he'd already decided and was only explaining it as a courtesy. "I'm not asking permission. I'm telling you how it's going to work."

"And how is it going to work?"

"You won't see me."

That got a raised eyebrow, but Ward didn't elaborate, and Emeric had learned by now that pushing on it wouldn't get him anywhere faster. Ward's forearm was still wrapped, still bad, and he came anyway, but not at Emeric's side. By the time they reached the gatehouse, he'd already dropped back and gone quiet in a way that had nothing to do with the wound, and when Emeric looked for him a second time, he wasn't where a guard was supposed to be.

The walk did more work than any report could have. The smell that had announced Kaldo from a distance only got worse up close, and Emeric started tracing it the way he'd trace any bad system, backward from the symptom. The burial ground sat too close to the water; that much he could tell without asking anyone. Where the latrines sat relative to the wells, he couldn't say yet, not from a single walk through streets he didn't know, but the shape of the problem was already there, sitting under the smell like something he'd seen before in a different life, on a different planet, in a textbook instead of a street. The people he passed had eyes that gave a clear view of their condition, caught somewhere between hopeful and given up.

Somewhere past the second street corner, Emeric felt it for the first time and had no name for what he'd felt. Not a sound, not quite a sensation he could point to on his own body. A pull, low and directionless, like a weight settling behind his eyes that hadn't been there a breath before. He stopped walking without deciding to.

"Your Highness?" Rylan asked.

"Nothing." It wasn't nothing. It was the same kind of nagging he got from a machine running one gear tooth off true, a wrongness too small to name and too specific to be imagination. He glanced back down the street they'd just come from. Nothing there but a woman airing out bedding from a window and a dog asleep in a doorway. He kept walking, and the weight faded the way a smell fades once you stop paying attention to it, not gone, just no longer the loudest thing in the room.

He didn't have a framework for this yet, and he knew better than to force one on too little data, so he filed it the way he filed everything he couldn't explain: input observed, no working theory, revisit if it repeats.

It repeated twice more before they reached Rylan's street. Once when they cut through an alley narrow enough that Emeric had to turn sideways past a stacked cart, the pull sharp enough that he actually looked up at the rooftops, at nothing, at empty tile and a gap of grey sky. Once when Rylan stopped to trade a nod with a neighbor and Emeric, half-listening, caught the same low hum settle in from somewhere behind them and vanish again a moment later, like a held breath let out. Both times, nothing was there. Both times, the feeling had a direction to it he couldn't have described to anyone, only that it sat closer to his spine than his ears, and that it thinned the second he tried to look straight at it, the way a star does when you stop looking at it sideways.

He was a mage now. He still hadn't gotten used to saying that word about himself, even in his own head. He knew, distantly, that mages were supposed to have some kind of sense for this sort of thing, a pull toward nearby mana the way a person feels heat off a stove without touching it. Nobody had explained to him what that was actually supposed to feel like, though, and he had nothing to calibrate it against; a decent-sized personal mana pool didn't hand him the experience of using it, only the raw capacity to. He decided, provisionally, that it was probably nothing. Stress. A bad night's sleep translating itself into phantom sensation. He'd revisit it if the data kept showing up.

Rylan's house was small and clean in the specific way poor houses stayed clean, like cleanliness was one of the only costs left that didn't require money. Nessa answered the door and went still for half a second too long before she remembered to bow. Finn hid behind her leg. Wren didn't, and stared at Emeric with the flat, unimpressed curiosity of a child who hadn't yet learned that princes were supposed to be interesting.

Emeric let the reunion happen without hovering over it, stepped back, and let Rylan be a husband and a father for a few minutes instead of a guard. It cost him nothing and told him plenty, watching how the neighbors on the street reacted to a guard's homecoming instead of a prince's arrival, wary in exactly the way people get wary of nobility who show up expecting gratitude.

He had a lot of walking still to do before any of this turned into a plan. After the lovebirds had a chance to get reacquainted, Emeric got the chance to ask what he'd actually come here for.

"What was the town like, before?" he asked. "Before the war."

Nessa considered the question longer than it seemed to need. "Busier. My parents talked about it more than I remember it myself; I was young when the men left. I was ten, maybe. One week the harbor had hands on every dock, the next it didn't, and nobody explained where they'd gone beyond 'the crown needs them.'"

"Nobody said which war."

"Would it have mattered? We weren't the ones deciding to fight it." She said it without heat, just fact. "Then the plague came a couple years after, once some of the men who came back were already trying to put things right. Took the ones the war didn't. Men, women, children, animals. It didn't ask who'd already paid."

"The census had this place at over four thousand once."

"It did. Ask anyone over fifty what it used to be, and they'll tell you like it happened last spring." She glanced toward the window, toward the street. "Half that now, maybe less. Feels cursed some days, if you let yourself think that way. The old folks talk about it like the town's still that place underneath, just resting. The rest of us don't have anywhere else to go, so I suppose we agree with them, whether we believe it or not."

Emeric took note of that. The town census had once read four thousand, and now it was half. A war that was over couldn't be helped, but a plague, based on the knowledge he carried, had a high chance of being curable. Plagues normally started because of bacteria, but how did you explain bacteria to commoners who didn't trust nobles and had been cheated with less than nothing to begin with? Are you telling me these microscopic creatures we can't see do things to our bodies and kill us? Are you telling me some of them help us and some of them hurt us? If he weren't a prince, he'd have been at genuine risk of the stake, or a healthy dose of a lobotomy to cure his mania.

Emeric decided he'd do some field research and collect his thoughts. Once Rylan was done with the pleasantries, he followed Emeric back out. They circumnavigated the whole town, and Emeric became sure of the plague's likely cause. The plague had a high chance of being waterborne. Everywhere he looked, the same pattern repeated: latrines dug downhill and too close to the bank, burial grounds sitting just upriver of the very wells the town drank from, and no clear sense that anyone had ever connected those two facts to each other. It wasn't ignorance in the way he'd have mocked back on Earth; nobody here had access to a framework that made the connection obvious. It was just bad geometry, decades old, never questioned because nobody had a reason to question it.

The pull came back twice more on that circuit, once sharp enough that he actually slowed his stride to listen for footsteps he never heard, and once so faint he almost convinced himself he'd invented it out of nothing. Both times he told himself the same thing: not enough data yet, keep walking. He didn't mention it to Rylan. He wasn't sure yet whether it was worth mentioning to anyone.

Near the well closest to the burial ground, a woman filling a bucket saw Rylan first and started to nod a greeting before she registered who was walking beside him. The nod died halfway. She finished filling the bucket without another look at either of them and left faster than the weight of a full bucket should have allowed, water sloshing over the rim with every hurried step.

"Was it me?" Emeric asked, once she was out of earshot.

"Wasn't you specifically." Rylan didn't slow down. "Her boy didn't come back from conscription. Crown took him, Crown never said what happened to him, Crown's the only reason she's hauling her own water instead of having someone else do it. You're the Crown today, whatever else you are on other days."

"That's not something Bram's going to fix by walking me somewhere."

"No," Rylan agreed. "That one you don't fix. You just don't make it worse."
