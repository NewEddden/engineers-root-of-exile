Book 2, Chapter 6: Sohra

The drum went quiet at nineteen minutes past five on the Sunday morning, with the sun not yet up and the whole convoy parked in the Band with its necks open, and Emeric felt it go the way you'd feel a clock stop in the next room.

He was on the K Discovery's forward deck with his back to the deckhouse and a mug going cold between his hands. They'd come up at four, all eight, a day short of the ring by Sorrel's reckoning, because the racks were due and Ferra had wanted them full for the last run in. The sky to the east had gone the colour of tin. The cloud was a long way under the keel, a grey floor with holes in it, and through the holes, five miles down, the sea was still dark. Everything was still. The racks pulled all down the line in their long even note, and under it, behind it, at the edge of his sense, the chain had been beating as it always beat: the drum they'd laid on the Wednesday afternoon a long way astern, and the wall water's beyond that, and the rest of them away east toward home, small and regular, each to its neighbour.

The nearest one stopped.

Not faded. Stopped, mid-beat. Behind it the wall water's drum beat once more into nothing, and waited, and beat again, and got no answer.

He was on his feet. "Sorrel."

"I've got nothing." She had the glass up. "Nothing behind. Nothing on the..." She lowered it. "What is it?"

"The last drum. It's gone quiet."

"Dead?"

"Dead or shut. They look the same from here." He'd built them to look the same. If anybody but his mother or himself tried a drum's decider, it went silent and held its pin and looked, from outside, exactly as if it had died. He'd thought that clever. "Get Linnet. I want to know if Kaldo can hear us."

Sorrel was already on the set. He turned round, astern, toward the drum, and opened his sense as wide as it went.

The Band was loud. It was always loud. It lay round the hulls a mile thick and he'd learned to hear past it, as you learn to hear one voice in a crowded room, and he heard past it now, as far as it would let him; and there was something in it, coming from astern and high, past the drum, swinging wide round the line's flank and out into the west, that was so much louder than the Band that the Band went quiet round it.

He'd felt something like it once before. At the ladder on Zale's ship, on the Tuesday past the wall, the old one coming through the sea toward them with the water leaning away. This was that, in the air. It had no edges he could find. It had a direction and a speed and a weight, and the weight was the most mana he'd ever felt in one place that wasn't a tree.

And it had a note. Under the weight, faint, for the space of a breath, before the loudness swamped it, he heard it. Gravity and fire. His own two, wound together, as they were wound in him.

"All hulls," he said into Sorrel's handset. "Something coming from the west, high and fast. Very big. I don't know what. Shells up if they're not. Nobody fires until Ward says. Ward, it's yours."

"Mine," said Ward, from the K Amara, at once. "Shells are up. Guns are manned. Nobody fires."

The sun came over the edge of the cloud behind them. The light went across the Band from east to west in one long stroke, and lit the hulls gold one after another, and went on, and lit the thing in the west.



She came down the line from the west at the height of the hulls, with the sun full on her, and he had time to see her before she reached them.

She was longer than escort one. That was the first thing, and he put her against the escort in his head as he'd put the old one against the K Amara, and then had to put her there again because the first time he'd thought he must have got it wrong. Her wings were wider than she was long, and they didn't beat; they were held out, still, and she came down the air on them as if the air were a slope she was sliding on. She was dark. Under the dark, between the plates, she was red, the deep banked red of a forge with the door shut, and it came and went along her as she moved. There were horns, two, swept back. And eyes. At that distance he couldn't have seen the eyes of a man. He saw hers. They were gold.

All down the line, he felt people see her. It went through the convoy as a dropped tray goes through a kitchen.

She didn't come at the K Discovery. She went past it, two hundred yards off the port side, so close that the wind of her going rocked Sorrel's ship on her stones, and she didn't turn her head. She went down the line. Past escort one. Past hull four. Toward the K Amara, which lay at the centre of the convoy with her stasis shell up round her like a soap bubble the size of a church, and her port racks open on the upper deck, a long bank of them with their necks raised.

She opened her mouth.

The fire came out of her low and flat, not at the hull, not at the deck, not at the aprons where the knights stood in their ranks: along the necks. One long line of it, laid down the port rack bank from one end to the other, as neat as a man drawing a pen down a column. It never got there. It hit the shell a yard out and spread on it, white and gold, a great sheet of fire flattened across the bubble's skin, so bright that he had to look away, and when he looked back she was past, and the shell was still up, and the racks were still open, and nothing on the K Amara was burning.

"Shell took it," said Ward's voice. Level. "Whole. Gauge down by a third on that quarter. Ferra says the stone's..." A pause. "Ferra says the stone's crazed. Two facets. Holding weaker on the port quarter. Holding."

She'd turned. Out past the K Endeavor, at the end of the line, she came round in a long climbing curve with the sun on her back, and he watched her come round, and he knew what he was looking at before he could have said how. She'd been told no. She'd put her fire on the thing she wanted stopped and something had put its hand up in her face, and now she was coming back down the line again, faster, lower, and she wasn't drawing a pen down anything this time. She went down the convoy in a sweep, wings tilted, and the wind of her went across every hull in turn, and she turned at the far end and came back up it, and turned again.

"Ward," he said.

"Holding fire." Ward's voice hadn't changed at all. "One contact. Nobody hit. Shells holding. I'm not firing on a thing that's only looking."

"It's not looking."

"Then tell me what it's doing and I'll decide."

He couldn't. He opened his mouth to, and found he had nothing but a feeling, and a feeling was what Ward had told him on Friday night to bring to the table before he acted on it.

Behind the K Amara, a mile down the line, on Zale's ship, somebody was shouting.

He heard it on the set. Not words he knew. Ilenne's voice, high, in her own tongue, fast, the same sounds over and over, and Zale's voice under it trying to catch hold of them, and a third voice, Linnet's, saying "...I've got the K Amara, I've got her, who do you want, Captain, who..." and then the set went to a hiss as Linnet keyed it wrong in her hurry.

"Zale," he said.

"She knows it." Zale was breathless. "She says she knows it. The first fire. The fire on the racks. She's got a word for it, she keeps saying it, it's a thing they do, it's... I've half the words, Emeric, I've..." Ilenne's voice, right at the handset, and then Zale again. "She says it's a saying. She says..."

"What does it do next?"

A gabble, and a pause, and Zale. "She says she doesn't know. She says she knows the saying. She doesn't know this one. She says ask her what it means and she'll tell you, ask her what it'll do and she can't."

The dragon came round at the far end of the line for the third time, and this time she didn't sweep.



Ferra had both hands on the gravity banks when it came through.

She'd been on them since the first pass, because the K Amara's bridge had four stations and the banks were the one nobody else could hold as well as she could, and Ward had looked at her once and she'd gone. Port forward, port aft, starboard forward, starboard aft: four brass wheels in a row under the bridge deck's forward glass, each with its gauge, each feeding a bank of stones in the keel that held the K Amara level on the air. She had the feel of the ship through them as she had the feel of a column through her pencil.

The dragon came in on the port quarter. She'd come in on the port quarter on purpose. Ferra saw it as she came: the quarter that was weak. And she came in close and slow, and this time the fire came out of her wide.

The shell took the first of it and went. She felt it go. The gauge on the shell's board by Ward's elbow went from a third to nothing in the time it took her to look at it, and through the bridge deck's port glass the sheet of fire on the bubble's skin tore down the middle and came through, and hit the rack bank.

It burned. The whole bank went up at once, every rack in it, their necks open and full of the Band, and the noise of it came through the deck into her feet like a train going over a bridge. The K Amara lurched. The port banks under Ferra's hands went light, all at once, both of them, because the racks that fed them were burning, and the ship went down by the port side, and everything on the bridge that wasn't fixed slid.

She put the starboard banks down. Not off. Down. She took them down a turn and a half each, both wheels at once, and felt the K Amara's keel come back toward level as the starboard side stopped lifting harder than the port, and she held it there. Three degrees down by the port. Two. She held it at two. It was the most she could do with half the ship's lift gone, and she held it, and didn't look up, and heard the bridge round her as a room you've stopped listening to.

"...in the trunk," somebody was shouting. "It's in the trunk, it's going down the trunk..."

"Where's the trunk go?" That was Ward.

"After store. Dry store. It's in the flour."

"Shut it. Every vent, every hatch on the after store, shut it and leave it shut. If you open it in this wind you'll feed it. Starve it." Ward's voice hadn't risen. It had got slower. "Knights on the racks. Cut the necks shut. By hand. Get the burning ones off the feeds. All civilians forward of the main mast. Planted deck, everybody, boots on, harness on. Go."

She heard them go. She heard the knights' boots going aft along the upper deck and the clang of the neck hatches as somebody began heaving them shut against the fire, and somebody screaming, short, and stopping. She held the ship at two degrees.

"Last vent," said a voice. "Last vent on the after store's open, the rigger's gone in for it..."

"Who's gone in?"

"A rigger. He's in. He's..." A noise she didn't want to understand. "He's down. A knight's gone in after him. He's got him. They're out. They're both..."

"Imani's deck," said Ward. "Both of them. Now. Who's on the rifles?"

The rifles. She'd known where they were all morning without thinking about it: six with the fleet, a crate-number and a serial each in her book, two of them aboard the K Amara. Arthur's at the forward port rack station, on its mount. The second in its case at the after port station by the rack bank, where its loader had been sitting with it since they came up. By the rack bank.

"The second's coming forward," said someone. "His loader's bringing it up..."

Through the port glass she saw him. A loader in his blues, with the long case in his arms, coming forward along the upper deck out of the smoke from the rack bank, bent over it, running. The fire came along the deck behind him. Not the dragon's: the racks', spilling out of a burst feed in a long sheet across the planking, fast, faster than a man. He looked over his shoulder and saw it. He went over the rail.

He went over with the case still in his arms. He hadn't had time to put it down. And there was a flight knight in the air off the port quarter, on station, where Ward had put six of them at the first pass, and the knight dived, and caught him by the harness ten yards below the rail. Caught him and held him. And the case came out of the man's arms with the jerk, and turned over once in the sunlight, and fell.

She watched it fall. Five miles. Into the cloud, and through it.

She took her right hand off the starboard aft wheel for exactly as long as it took to write in the book open on the shelf beside the banks, in pencil, without looking at what she wrote:

05.41. DR no. 2 (K Am., after port), with case. Over the side, port qtr, Band, loader saved by flight knight. Lost at sea. Unrecoverable. Position: Sorrel's 05.30 fix. Witnesses: me, the bridge.

And put her hand back.

"She's turning," said Ward. "She's turning for the K Endeavor."

Ferra didn't look up. She knew where the K Endeavor was without looking. The K Endeavor was half a mile down the line on the port quarter with the Challenger slung under her keel and her hospital deck open to the sky on the upper works, because Imani liked the light, and Imani was on it, and the two burned men were going to it, and so were the K Amara's other wounded, whatever was coming.

"Lives at stake," said Ward, to nobody, as if he were writing it down. Then, louder, on the apron set: "Arthur. Fire."

She heard it. Everybody on the K Amara heard it. It wasn't a sound so much as a blow, a hard flat crack from the forward port mount that went through the whole hull and rattled the glass in front of her face; and through the glass, out past the end of the burning rack bank, she saw the dragon twist.

She saw it twist at the flash, before the round got there, and not far enough. Something came off the dragon's head, spinning, catching the sun, and something red came out of its neck in a long spray across the air, and it stopped flying. It simply stopped, wings half shut, and dropped. Three hundred feet, near enough. It dropped through the Band, past the K Endeavor's keel, and below it, and then the wings opened with a crack like canvas and it was climbing again.

And then it wasn't there.

Where it had been, there was something small. Something the size of a man, dark, coming up through the air toward them fast, toward the K Amara. Toward the gun. Arthur's second round went past it into the sky.

"Check fire," said Ward. "Check fire. It's on the deck."

It was on the deck. It came over the apron rail at the forward port station so fast that Ferra didn't see it land, only arrive: a tall dark figure with something red and bright running down one side of its neck and a broken stump of horn above one temple, among the knights. She saw Mira come in with the spear, low, fast, the turn she'd been showing Maeve at the rail all week, and the dark figure did something with one arm and Mira went sideways across the apron and down. She saw Bren go in on Mira's heel, shouting, and go down too, on top of her. Neither of them stayed down. Both of them were trying to get up.

"Check fire!" Ward, louder. "Nobody fires on this deck!"

The ship tilted. Something had hit the port forward bank's feed, or the dragon's weight on the apron was enough on its own, and the deck went down by the head, and Ferra felt it go through the wheels into her hands, and leaned on them, and brought it back. Two degrees. One. She held it.

The figure on the apron had both hands on Arthur's rifle on its mount, and Arthur's hands on it too, and it was looking at Arthur. Just looking. As if it wanted to know who he was.

Then Emeric hit it.



He'd come off the K Discovery's forward deck at the crack of Arthur's shot, flat out, the length of the convoy in the time it took the dragon to fall and turn and change, and he came onto the K Amara's port apron from above and behind and hit her across the back with his shoulder and both arms and every ounce of gravity he could put behind it, and took her off the apron and over the rail and out into the air.

It was like hitting a wall with a body in it.

They went over the rail together and fell forty feet before he got his feet under him on nothing and threw her off. She turned in the air. She was a woman. She was tall, taller than him, dark-skinned and lean, in close scale from the throat down, dark bronze, heaviest at the throat and the wrists, with her hair cropped short and the stump of one horn standing up out of it above the left temple, broken off ragged at the root, and the second horn whole and swept back on the other side. Blood ran down the left side of her neck from a long open cut a hand's width from the throat. She didn't touch it. Her eyes were gold. They were wide open and fixed on him, and her nostrils were open too.

She breathed in.

He saw it happen to her. He saw it go through her face and down her whole body, and her head came up and round, toward him, and stayed, and something in her face that had been anger went to something else he couldn't read at all.

"All hulls," he said, into the set at his collar. "Stay on the K Amara. Everybody. The hull and the burned. Nobody comes after me."

He went backwards away from the convoy, fast, and she came after him.

She came as he'd known she would. Not at the K Amara now, not at the gun. At him. He pulled her a mile off, two, three, west and down, out over the cloud, until the convoy was eight bright beads strung across the Band behind her and there was nothing round the two of them but sky.

Then she put out her hand, and it began.

He'd been in fights. He'd been in fights since he was nineteen, in a gym over a tire shop with the tape on his hands, and he'd been in them in this world with knives and with mages and with a man who could put a wall of earth up faster than he could think. He'd never been in one where the other side was simply more. That was all it was. She did what he did. Gravity, fire. She did it with nothing he hadn't seen, no glyph he didn't know, and she did it at a size that had nothing to do with him, the way the sea had nothing to do with a bucket.

The first pull took him a hundred feet sideways before he'd felt it gather. The second he saw coming, a heartbeat before, a thickening of her where she was about to spend it, and he moved off its line and it went past him and tore a hole in the cloud below them the size of a field. The fire came after it. He got under the fire. He went in, close, inside her reach, where something big is always slowest, and hit her, twice, short, in the body, and it was like hitting the planted deck. She hit him back with her open hand.

He'd padded his insides before it landed. He'd felt it coming and done what he'd worked out after the cave golem cracked his hand, the Crumple, the soft give under the skin that spread the blow out and down through him instead of letting it break anything. It still broke something. He felt a rib go. He went backwards a quarter of a mile through the air, turning over, and got himself stopped, and she was already there.

So it went. He stayed small. He stayed fast. He read her as he'd read Ward in the rematch, by where her weight went a breath before she moved, and he wasn't in the place where she'd spent it when it arrived, mostly. When he was, he padded and took it. He hit her when there was an opening and it did nothing he could see. Twice she put fire round him in a ball and he got out the top of it with his hair burning. Once she put gravity on him from above, a weight like a building, and drove him down through the cloud, through the whole grey floor of it, into clear air on the other side with the sea miles under him and nothing between, and he stopped himself a thousand feet from where he'd started and came back up through the hole he'd made and she was waiting in it.

He didn't know how long. Minutes. His breath was gone. His left arm wasn't answering properly. The rib was two ribs. There was blood in his mouth.

From the convoy, far off now, he heard somebody on the set say, in a voice he didn't know, that the king was down, that the king had gone through the cloud and not come up; and somebody else say no, there, there he is; and then a long silence on the set, which was worse.

She came at him once more. Not fast. She'd learned how fast he was. She came straight at him with her hand out and the whole of that weight gathering in her palm, and he saw it gather, and knew he couldn't get off its line this time, because there was too much of it and he was too slow now, and he put his own hand up into her fire to meet it, because there was nothing else to put up.

His hand went into the fire and the fire didn't burn it.

His hand was fire. His arm. All of him. It came up over him from inside without his asking, the way it had in the Band trial and in the wall water with Ilenne's hand round his ankle, only all of him this time, his chest and his legs and his throat and his face, over his mouth, over his eyes, so that he looked out at her through fire, and his breath stopped. It just stopped. There was no air in him and nothing he could do about it and nothing in him that needed it, for now.

Her fire was round his hand. He knew it. He knew exactly the shape of it, as he'd have known his own; it was his own, only bigger. He turned it over. He didn't cancel it. He turned back the push she'd sent it with, end for end, and it came round in his hand and was his, all of it, and he threw it back at her.

It hit her full in the chest and took her backwards a hundred yards through the air.

He went after it. His eyes were burning gold; he could see the gold at the edges of what he saw. His left forearm, inside the fire, had gone hard, plated, scaled, a dark sheen like hers under the light, for the space of three blows, and then it was skin again. He didn't look at it. He hit her and hit her. He had her fire and his own and nothing to breathe and the clock was running in him, and he went at her as hard as he'd ever gone at anything in his life, and drove her down and back across the sky, and she gave, and gave, and he had her at the end of it with his right hand drawn back and the whole of both their fires in it.

She was looking at him.

She'd stopped. She hung in the air in front of him with the blood on her neck and her arms down at her sides, open, and her head back a little, and her throat bare. Her eyes were on his. He knew what it was. He couldn't have said how. A thing that has finished fighting.

He stopped.

He held the blow there, at the end of his arm, for one breath he didn't have, and then he opened his hand and let the fire go out of it into the air, and the skin went off him, all of it, all at once, and the air came back into his chest so hard it hurt more than the ribs.

She watched him do it. Her face did something.

It was not gratitude. He'd thought for a second that it would be. It was the face of somebody who's been handed back a coin she'd lost fairly, at cards, in front of people. She drew herself up in the air. When she spoke it was in the speech of the land, Kaldo's speech, or something under it, old, with every vowel a little out of its place and the words set down slowly, as if out of a book she'd learned by heart and never once heard said aloud.

"You stop," she said. "Why do you stop?"

"You'd finished."

"Yes. I am finished. You have it. It is yours." She said it as though he were stupid. "And you give it back. You stop with it in your hand and you give it back to me." Her lip went up from her teeth. "I did not ask."

He had nothing to say. He was trying to breathe.

"You drink the sky," she said. "Eight of you, open, hours and hours. I feel it from far. It is not yours." She flicked her hand, east, behind him, the way you'd flick away a fly. "The thing you leave in it, alone, drinking, I break. That is nothing. That is a thing. The ships, I tell. I tell them stop. That is the courtesy." Her eyes narrowed. "And you put a wall in my face."

She looked at him a long time, close, with her head on one side. Her nostrils were still open.

"I have never seen you," she said. "Never. And I know you." She came a yard nearer in the air and he didn't move away. "My elders tell of a man. They tell his shape. This shape." Her eyes went down him and up. "This face. They tell it to us when we are small, so that we know it."

"Why?"

She didn't answer that. She looked at his eyes, and then she looked past him, east, at the eight beads strung across the Band, and her head turned toward them and stayed turned, and she breathed in again.

He turned and went back to his ships, because he couldn't stay up much longer, and she followed him.



He came down on the K Endeavor because that was where the burned were going, and he didn't decide it. He was over the convoy, and his body went down to the hull where Imani was, as a horse goes to its own stable.

The K Amara was alongside her. Ward had brought her across while Emeric was gone, scorched black down the whole port side from the rack bank to the keel, still smoking from the after store's vents, and laid her against the K Endeavor in the air, deck to deck, a yard of nothing between their rails, so the burned could be passed across by hand. They were being passed across. He came down onto the K Endeavor's hospital deck among them: men on stretchers, men walking with their arms held out from their sides, a rigger with his face and hands wrapped wet, a knight beside him in the same state. Bettany. The cook from the K Amara, carrying somebody. Ferra, who'd left the banks to the K Amara's second when the ships touched, and come across the yard of nothing with her book in her hand, and was standing at the rail counting the stretchers aloud.

And Imani, coming toward him across the deck with her face gone still.

He landed on his feet and his left knee gave and he caught himself on a stanchion. Behind him, something else landed. Light, this time. Barely a sound.

Everybody on that deck turned round.

The dragon stood at the after end of the hospital deck in her woman's shape, with the blood still running down her neck in the sunlight and soaking the dark scale at her shoulder, and looked at all of them, at the stretchers and the burned and the people with their hands half up, without any particular interest. Then she looked at Emeric, holding on to the stanchion. Then she looked away from him, round the deck, as if he were settled and she were looking for the next thing.

Nobody moved. Nobody on the K Amara's rail a yard away fired anything, because Ward had said nobody fires on a deck, and Ward was at the K Amara's rail with his hand up flat, holding it.

Imani moved.

She came across the hospital deck past Emeric, past the stanchion, without looking at him, as though he were a thing she'd see to in a minute, and walked straight up to the dragon, and stopped a yard from her, and looked at her neck.

"That wants closing," she said.

The dragon looked down at her. She was a head taller than Imani. She looked at Imani's face for a while, and then at Imani's hands, which were empty, and held a little way out from her sides; and then she turned her head, a fraction, so that the wound was toward Imani and in the light.

Imani looked. Emeric felt her open her sight, narrow, as she opened it for a child: just at the cut, just at the edge of it. She didn't touch. Her hand came up an inch and the dragon's eyes went to the hand, and the hand went back down.

"It's clean," said Imani. "It's deep. It's a hand's width from the big vessel and it missed it. It'll close by itself, on you, by the look of you. It'll want keeping clean." She said it exactly as she'd have said it to a deckhand. "If you'd let me, I'd stitch it."

"No," said the dragon.

"Then keep it clean."

Imani went back up the deck to Ferra and the stretchers at the rail, and didn't look round.

The dragon opened her mouth to say something else. And then she stopped. Her head came up and round, sharp, into the wind that came across the deck from the K Amara's side, and her nostrils opened, and she went still all through, as she'd gone still in the air when she first had his scent.

And then she moved.

She went across the deck at a dragon's speed in a woman's body, which was no speed he had a word for. One moment she was by the after rail and the next she was halfway down the deck, straight at the rail where Ferra stood with her book, and Imani beside her; straight at the two of them, with her head down and her eyes on them and her face like nothing he'd seen on a face.

He was between them before he knew he'd moved.

He didn't decide that either. He was there, with the deck under his feet and her in front of him, and something had happened to his back. He felt it rather than saw it: a weight at the shoulders, and a spread, and the wind catching in something on either side of him that was his and that he'd never had; and a pressure above his temples, two points of it, hard. He saw Ferra's face over his shoulder go white. He saw the shadow on the deck in front of him, his own shadow in the low sun, and it had wings on it, half spread, the height of a man; and horns.

The dragon stopped. A stride short. She'd been going to stop there. He knew it the moment she did it, from the way she stopped: not checked, not pulled up. Arrived.

She didn't look at him. She looked past him, over his shoulder, at Ferra and Imani, side by side at the rail. She breathed in, long and slow, the whole of a breath, with her eyes half shut.

"Those two," she said.

Nobody answered her.

"Those two." She opened her eyes. She looked from Ferra to Imani and back, and her face had a frown in it, a small honest frown, the frown of somebody trying to remember a word. "I have not felt this. Since my mother. I don't..." She stopped. She shook her head, once. "Those two. Which? I can't tell. Both." She looked at Emeric then, at last, at his face and over his shoulder at the shadow on the deck, and her lip went up from her teeth again, not in anger. "You think I come to hurt them."

"You ran at them."

"I walk to them. You are very slow." She looked at the wings of his shadow on the planks. "Slow, and you have those. Hm."

The weight went off his back. He felt it go, the spread folding into nothing, the two points above his temples going soft and then gone, and with them went whatever had been holding him up. It had cost him something. It had cost him all of what the fight had left. He knew it as his knees went: the last of him, spent on two yards of deck.

The deck came up.

It didn't reach him. Imani had him under the arms before he hit it, and went down to her knees with him, and he heard her say his name, sharply, once, in the voice she used on people who were about to stop breathing; and then nothing at all.
