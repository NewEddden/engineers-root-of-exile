Chapter 35 - The Cure

The trouble came from the ford, which was the direction he had built for, and that was the first thing that made it easy to believe.

It came a little after full dark, a rider up the approach at a hard pace and men behind him on foot at the treeline, more than a few, moving the way men moved when they meant the wall to see them. The board caught them clean, three lamps waking in a row along the ford stretch, exactly the picture Emeric had built the thing to draw, and for a moment he felt the cold satisfaction of a system doing precisely the job it was made for. Bodies on the approach, counted and placed before they were in bowshot, and Ward already moving because Ward did not need to be told twice.

"Feint or not, I take it seriously," Ward said, pulling men to him in the yard, and Emeric agreed, because that was the correct call and Emeric knew a correct call when he heard one. You met the threat you could see. You did not stand at the gate theorizing about it while it walked up your road. Ward went out through the sally with the better part of the watch behind him to meet whatever was at the treeline, and Emeric stood at the board and watched the ford lamps and did not think, not once, that the wall had two sides and only one of them now had men on it.

The men at the treeline did not press. They probed, and drifted, and gave Ward a long grey shapeless nothing to chase in the dark, and Emeric read that as caution and not as design, because reading it as design would have meant reading the men who sent them, and the men who sent them had no faces he could find.

Then Rylan said, from the far end of the board, quiet, "My lord. Stretch nine's gone wrong."

It was on the opposite wall from the ford. The river side. The isolated run above the deep channel where the bank fell away and no one walked, the stretch a man put a node on precisely because no one walked it and so nothing but the node would ever watch it.

Emeric came down the board to look, and looked, and his own good work looked back at him. Stretch nine's lamp was not dark, which would have been a flat stone, an honest dead node, a thing that could wait for morning. It was flickering. Reading, losing the reading, reading again, the exact wrong-before-it-goes behavior he had described to Rylan four nights ago as the sign of a true fault in the rune-work and not a starved stone. The lamp did the thing he had said a faulting rune would do, and he read it as the thing he had said it meant, because it had been built to be read that way and he was the man who had built it.

"A real one," he said, and there was even a small unwelcome part of him that was interested, that wanted to see what a genuine rune-fault looked like out on the stone, because he had never actually caught one and here at last was the rare case in the flesh. "That's the rune, not the stone. I have to go to it."

"I'll come," Rylan said. "The men are all at the ford."

That was true. That was simply, flatly true, and it was the truth of it that walked Emeric out the door. The men were at the ford because the threat was at the ford, and the wall's one hand had to go put itself on a faulting rune on the empty river side, and there was no one to send instead because there was never anyone to send instead, because he had built a thing that answered to him and only him and had told himself for weeks that this was a problem for a quieter season. He took a lamp and his own two hands and he went, and Rylan came a pace behind him, and the wall watched them go along the parapet toward stretch nine and said nothing, because he had built it to say nothing, because the whole cleverness of it had always been that it woke no one it did not need to wake.

He crouched at the node with the lamp and put his hands on it and began, already, to be wrong about it in a new way, because the moment he actually touched the thing his hands started telling him something his eyes at the board had not.

The fault was wrong. Not wrong like a faulting rune. Wrong like a made thing. The flicker at the board had been perfect, too perfect, the textbook sign and only the sign, and here at the node itself the underlying rune-work was whole, untouched, humming along exactly as it should while something small and deliberate had been set against it only to make the lamp lie. He knew this rune. He had cut it. Nothing in it had failed. Someone had made it look like it had failed, made it do the one specific thing he had taught a man was the mark of a true fault, and there was only one man he had taught that to, standing now a pace behind him in the dark, on the empty side of the wall, with the watch three hundred yards away chasing shapes at the ford.

The understanding arrived whole, the way the best of his understandings always did, clean and complete and one full moment too late to be worth anything at all. He turned, and for the first time in his life he read Rylan's face correctly and without effort, because the thing on it was too big now to miss, the grief and the terror and the apology all at once, the face of a man doing the worst thing he had ever done and unable to not do it, and behind that face, in it, plain as a number in a ledger, a child. Milford. The aunt no one was staying with. Every tell Emeric had filed under nothing that week rearranged itself in an instant into the only shape it had ever had, and he understood all of it, the boy and the cover story and the careful questions and the too-perfect fault, and he understood that Ward had been right and that he had talked Ward out of it with reasons, and he opened his mouth to say something, he never afterward knew what, and it did not matter, because they were already on him.

They came from the dark past the node, from the fall of the bank where no one walked, three of them or four, fast and silent and unsurprised by anything he was, which was its own answer. He reached for the Pull the way a drowning man reaches for the surface, for the delve, for anything, for the mana in the dark that had never once failed to be there for him, and got a hand on the edge of it and nothing more, because a delve was a thing that took stillness and seconds and he was given neither.

And in the half-count it took him to lose it, Emeric discovered he had a use for the one thing he had never bothered to learn.

Handless casting.

Any mage with real training could have thrown fire from an empty hand. A bolt. A burst of blinding light. Some crude, directed effect—anything to buy the space he needed.

Emeric could not.

Casting a raw effect from nothing but personal mana and spatial positioning was the one discipline he had never engineered around. There was no rune to trigger. No stone to channel through. No prepared system waiting for him to activate. Nothing built beforehand to rely upon when the moment came.

It was a skill mages developed through years of repetition, casting the same basic effects again and again until the shapes lived beneath conscious thought. Instinct replacing calculation. Muscle memory replacing hesitation.

Emeric had spent those years doing the opposite.

He had suppressed the very talent that repetition was meant to sharpen. He had retreated into his room, abandoned magic entirely instead of mastering it.

Now those years had come due.

The boy who had spent most his life being reclusive, not practicing was now in a predicament that same practice could have alleiviated.

The man who was here now spent his time offloading every task onto a rune, a stone, or some clever standing system.

And all of it had culminated in today.

He had finally reached the moment when every one of those external items he relied on had been taken from him.

And he had nothing left to throw.

Only his mind and his two bound hands.

Neither of which was a weapon.

Then the dark took him.

A knee took him off balance. A hand had his wrist and then both wrists. Something dark came down over his sight and the world went to cloth and pressure and his own breath loud against it, and through it, close, he heard a voice he did not know, low and level and entirely without heat, a man who did not need to raise his voice because he had never once in his life been in a room he did not own. It said one word to Rylan, and the word was a name, and the name was the boy's.

"Carry him," the level voice said. "You want the boy to breathe, you carry your lord down to the water with your own two hands, and you do it gently, because damaged goods are a conversation and I am tired of conversations."

And Emeric felt the hands that took his weight then, and knew them, knew them the way he had come to know that ragged conscientious breathing at the board, because it was Rylan who lifted him, Rylan who bore him down the fall of the bank into the dark where no one walked, a father carrying the man he had sold toward the water because the men who had built the trap had built a smaller one first, around a child, and left him only the one door out of it, which was this. Rylan did not speak. His breath came in the caught, held, grieving rhythm Emeric had spent a week beside and called conscientious, and it was the only sound the man made, all the way down.

They took him along the river side, to the water, where the one route out of Kaldo that was not the ford had waited the whole time for anyone patient enough to use it. He did not shout. There was cloth in the way of it and no one to hear it if there had not been, because the men who would have come were at the ford chasing nothing, and Ward was with them, and the board at the empty gate showed one lamp still flickering on stretch nine, a fault that was not a fault, calling for a hand that was not coming, to a room with no one in it to read the call. The wall watched the black water take him the way it watched everything, silently, faithfully, waking no one, doing a guard's work exactly as it had been built to do it, which had never once included the one thing a guard was for, which was to see a friend in trouble and refuse to stay quiet about it.

Somewhere behind him, growing smaller, Kaldo slept inside its finished wall, whole and prosperous and asleep, not yet knowing it had lost the one hand that held it, and would not know until morning, when Ward came back from the ford with nothing to show and found the lord's bed cold and a lamp still lying on the parapet by a node that had never truly broken at all.
