Chapter 51 - Against the Current

The machine ran for three days before the men stopped coming to watch it and started coming to argue with it, which was exactly the turn Emeric had been hoping for and had not dared to schedule.

They had opinions now. The one they called Toller wanted the firebox door hung the other way, so a man feeding it didn't have to reach across the heat. Two others had been up half a night over the linkage that carried the piston's push to the shaft and were sure, both of them, that it wasted motion, and they were right, it did, a little, and Emeric let them chase it rather than tell them where the waste was, because a man who found the loss himself would never again build it in. He had spent a career watching good engineers be handed answers and forget them by the next job. He would rather these men be slow and sure than quick and borrowed.

So he let them improve it, and he set them to building a second one, and did not tell them that the second would not be the last. The works wanted more than one. The foundry alone could eat the output of three of these and ask for a fourth, and beyond the foundry there was the glasswork coming, and the kilns, and every hungry mouth of the district he'd put in the ground the season before. They would be turning these out for a good while yet, each one a little truer than the last, and that suited him. A thing you built once was a trick. A thing you built again and again until your least-clever hand could build it half-asleep was an industry.

He was watching the second engine take shape on the bench when the idea arrived, the way the good ones always did, sideways and unasked. He was looking at the push of the thing, the raw insistence of it, fire turned into force with no wheel and no current needed, and he thought, before he had decided to think it, of a hull.

Put this on a boat. Put this insistence under a hull and point it up the river, and it would climb the current the way nothing on that water had ever climbed it. That was the part that stopped his breath for a moment, because he had told himself, not a season ago, staring at roads he could not yet build, that the river was a one-way road and always would be. Things came down from Grayrock. Nothing went up. He had built the entire argument for a railway on that flat refusal, on the fast steep water off the Grayrock hills that no pole or oar or ox on a towpath had ever beaten going the wrong way. And here on the bench was the thing that beat it. Not muscle, which the current had always defeated, but fire, which did not tire and did not care which way the water wished to go.

He made himself slow down and price it before the wanting ran off with him, because a wonder he didn't price was a wonder that lied to him. The boat would climb the rapids, yes, but not always and not with anything heavy in her belly. It would take high water to do it; let the river drop in a dry month and the fast reaches would go too thin and too fierce at once, and the climb that a full spring current allowed would simply close. It would carry men and light freight up in a morning that a barge spent a hard week losing to, and it would not carry ore by the ton, not up, not ever, because tonnage against that current asked more than any hull he'd put a kettle in could give. So the boat did not retire the railway. It only embarrassed it for part of the year, on one leg, for light loads, and left the rail every job that mattered most: the heavy haul, the dry months when the river shut its road, and the whole dry country west of the water that no current reached at all and no boat would ever help. He caught himself almost sorry the rail still had work, and then was glad of it, because a barony that hung its whole spine on a river that ran only one way and only in season was a barony one dry summer from being cut in half.

It was still, by any measure he cared about, the fastest thing that would ever have touched that water. That was worth building, even seasonal, even light. He set the daydream down as a plan instead, which was the only place he trusted himself to keep one.

Which left the small matter of a boat, and he had none, and no season to spend building one.

He put it to Ferra that evening. He did not delve for it; there was nothing in it that couldn't be said aloud, only a thing he'd rather not have overheard, and her little room with the door shut served for that.

"I need a hull," he said. "Small. Something a fisherman would work off, shallow enough to sit in the river the whole way up. If there's a man near the water willing to give his up for a fair price and a better one after, or a spare sitting in someone's yard that never got sold, I'd rather buy than build."

She looked at him a moment. "You want a fishing boat."

"I want a fishing boat."

"Why does the prince who's building a foundry and a glasshouse and half a city want one old fisherman's boat, and want it quietly."

"It's a surprise."

She did not roll her eyes, quite. She had heard him say that twice in a month now, and had come to understand it meant the answer was interesting and he wasn't going to give it, and that she would find out when the rest of them found out, and that arguing cost more than waiting. "You'll want it to draw little enough for the fast water up top, and light enough that whatever you're not telling me can shove it where a fisherman never would. That narrows it."

"It does. Find me the one that fits."

She found it before the day was out, which did not surprise him, because there was very little near the water Ferra had not already written down. She came back with a name he'd expected and a wrinkle he had not.

"Fletcher has one," she said. "Not one he's selling to a fisherman. One he built on spec off the old wood-hull work, back when you had him reinforcing keels for the defense, and never had a buyer for once the fighting stopped and everyone who'd have bought it was poor again. It's been sitting in his yard under canvas ever since. Sound. Never wetted past the launch trial. Never had a fish within a mile of it, so it doesn't stink yet, which he made a point of telling me matters if you mean to do anything but fish, and I couldn't tell him why it did because you won't tell me." A dry beat. "He'd be glad to be rid of it. And gladder, I think, to know the prince came to him for a hull at all."

That last was the part worth more than the boat. Fletcher was already building him the real ship, the metal one, the keel of it laid before the snow, the hull that would not compromise on anything the way this borrowed wooden one compromised on everything. A man building you the future did not always love watching you find your stopgaps elsewhere. Buying Fletcher's own leftover for the rough first draft was two birds, a hull to hand and a shipwright who'd know his yard was where Kaldo came when it needed to float something, grand or humble. Emeric filed the wooden boat, honestly, as what it was. A prototype. A thing to learn the mistakes on, so the metal one launched already past them.

Then he looked at where Fletcher's yard sat, and where the foundry sat, and saw the next problem.

Fletcher built at deep water. He had to; a yard that meant to launch seagoing iron could not sit in the shallow, silting mouth of the river, which was the whole reason Kaldo had needed a Fletcher in the first place and the whole bottleneck the man existed to break. So his yard was out around the low headland that sheltered the harbor, on the deep coastal side, a good way by water from the river mouth and a good deal further by the winding river from there up to the works. To float the hull to the foundry the honest way meant taking it down the coast, in past the shallows of the mouth that a keeled boat hated, and then up the whole slow length of the river, in daylight, past every soul in the town, who would watch a fishing boat go up to the ironworks and wonder, aloud and at length, what the prince wanted with it.

Overland it was barely a mile. Up the cart track that climbed the back of the headland, along the ridge behind the harbor where no one lived, down the far slope to the river well above the town, and up the near bank to the foundry. A mile of land against a day of water. The trouble with a mile of land was that a boat weighed what a boat weighed, and no number of men on ropes was moving that up a headland in a night.

He had gravity stones. He had, better than the stones, the one man in the barony who understood them down to the theory Emeric only half-followed.

Armand heard the whole of it without interrupting, which was his way, and then asked the one question that mattered. "How light do you need it, and for how long."

"Light enough that eight men can walk it a mile over a ridge. However long that takes."

Armand turned one of the small stones over in his fingers, and Emeric watched him do the arithmetic that Emeric could set up but not, if he was honest, finish alone. "These won't lift it," Armand said. "Not off the ground and floating, whatever the boys' stories say. A stone this size, on a mass that size, spread the field wide enough to take the whole hull evenly and there's not the strength in it to take the weight to nothing. What it will do is take most of it. Enough that the weight stops being the problem." He looked up. "The weight was never going to be your problem past the first hour anyway."

"The mass," Emeric said, because he had read the same page and it was the page that frightened him. Lighten a thing and you took its weight, the down of it, the press of it against the earth. You did not take its mass, the what-of-it, the amount of stuff that had to be shoved to start moving and shoved again to stop. A hull lightened near to nothing still had every pound of its true self wanting to keep doing whatever it was already doing. Standing still, it fought you to start. Moving, it fought you to stop, and it did not care in the least that it now weighed like a feather while it did.

"Downhill," Armand agreed, "it'll want to go on being downhill. You'll be leading a thing that pushes like a barn and steers like a leaf, and the two facts won't warn each other. Keep men below it on the slopes and you'll bury one."

So they planned it as the mass demanded and not as the lightness tempted. Armand set the field himself, wide and even across the hull, holding it there with the close continuous attention the work wanted, which was his to give and no one else's; the shaping of a steady even field across a whole awkward shape was exactly the fine-handed rune work the tower had spent his life teaching him and the farm had never once asked of him. Emeric took the route and the ropes and the order of men, lines fore and aft and never a soul on the downhill side of the thing on any grade. They moved it the night after, under a thin moon, up the cart track and along the empty ridge, and it went well and quiet for the whole of the climb and nearly killed a man named Osgood on the drop to the river, when the slope took hold of all that unweighted mass at once and the hull surged for the water like it had somewhere to be. It was the fore lines that saved it, and the fact that Emeric had put his heaviest men on them and his lightest nowhere it could reach. They snubbed it around the last turn hard enough to groan the timbers, and set it in the still water of the race pool below the foundry, and stood breathing in the dark, and no one said the thing everyone was thinking, which was that Armand had told them exactly which way it would try to kill them and it had tried that exact way.

They cradled it there and let the field off it slow, and it settled into the water and sat, an ordinary wooden boat again, heavy and honest, giving no sign of the strange hour it had just spent being a ghost of itself.

The marine engine was the second steam device the works had built, and it went in over the following days with all of Bram's crew swarming it, because a thing that moved was more interesting than a thing that sat, and every man wanted his hands on it. Armand watched more than he worked. Magic was his field and there was almost none of his field in front of him, only iron and fire and water and a screw cut true, and Emeric caught him more than once studying the whole enterprise with an expression that took Emeric a day to place, because he had not expected to see it on a mage.

It was something near to bafflement. Armand had tutored a boy who took the tower's hardest theory the way other children took bread, a boy who could hold a rune's logic whole in his head at an age when his brothers would have still been tracing the strokes. That boy had grown into a man who could delve where the tower's masters could not follow, and who had chosen, with all of that in his hands, to spend himself on kettles. On drains. On the honest dumb push of hot water against a piston, a thing any smith in the empire could grasp and no mage would deign to.

"It's like watching a master of the sword insist on the stick," Armand said to him once, quietly, not unkindly, on an evening when the engine was nearly seated. "You've the finest hand for the high art I've taught in thirty years, and you keep putting it down to pick up a tool a farmhand could carry. I don't say it's wrong. I've stopped being sure enough of anything about you to say a thing's wrong. I only don't understand it."

Emeric could have told him. The stick did not run out of mana. The stick did not die in a dead zone. The stick worked in his absence and after his death and in the hands of a man who had never met him, which was the whole and only standard he built to, and the sword, for all its beauty, was a thing that leaned on him being there to swing it. He could have said all of that. He said none of it, because the true root of it ran down past the engineering into the fear the engineering was built to answer, and that door he kept shut even to Armand. He only said, "The stick's harder to take away from me," and let the old man make of it what he would, and Armand looked at him a moment longer than the answer wanted and let it go.

He named the hull's own cost to himself while they worked, because a wonder unpriced was a wonder that burned someone. Fire under a wooden deck was fire under a wooden deck; he built the firebox its own iron hearth and its own clearances and its own discipline, and knew none of it made a wooden boat a safe place to keep a furnace, only a less reckless one. The engine's shake worked at every joint in the hull the way it never worked at a foundry floor. The weight of the thing sat the boat lower and stiffer than she'd been drawn to sit, and she'd carry all of that into the rapids the first time he asked her to climb. Every one of those was a reason the true ship wanted metal, and every one of them Emeric wrote down, because that was what the prototype was for. To spend the mistakes on wood, where they cost a boat, so the iron launched already knowing them.

They took her out on a grey still morning, all of them who had a hand in her. Bram and his crew, who had built the heart of her. Ferra, who had found the body of her and had earned the watching and would in any case not have been kept away. Armand, who had carried her over a ridge without a rope. Emeric last aboard, at the small wheel, with the pressure watcher's eyes on the needle beside him and the leave to stop the whole thing standing between the needle and disaster.

She lit, and shook, and caught, and went.

She went up the river against the current at a pace that stood every man on the bank silent, because they had all of them spent their lives watching things go up that river slowly or not at all, and here was a boat with no oar out and no sail set and the water plainly against her, walking upstream like the current was a courtesy she'd decided to decline. Ferra laughed out loud, once, surprised out of her by the sheer wrongness of it. Bram said nothing and gripped the rail and grinned like a much younger man.

Then Emeric put her at the rapids, the fast steep water off the Grayrock hills that had turned back everything with a pole in it since before Kaldo had a name, and gave her the throttle, and she climbed. She fought for it. The hull shuddered under him in a way that had nothing to do with the current and everything to do with an engine wood was never meant to hold, and the water came white over her bow, and she slowed to a crawl and dug and held and climbed, up the one-way road, the wrong way, the way nothing on that river had ever gone under its own power. Even Armand, who had seen a boy pull rooms out of the dark that no living mage could reach, watched the ordinary kettle drive the ordinary hull up the extraordinary wrong way and shook his head.

Emeric held her in the climb long enough to know it was real and not long enough to break her doing it, and read, in the shudder and the white water and the way she sat low and strained, all three of the walls at once. That the wood would not take this for a season, which was Fletcher's iron keel answered before it was launched. That the river ran high today and would not always, and the road she'd just opened would close itself the first dry month and stay closed until the water rose again. And that she could carry him up this way, and a handful of men, and a light load, and never the tonnage the hills sent down, which meant the rail he was already grading was not embarrassed after all, only handed the half of the year and the whole of the weight the river would always refuse.

He was not disappointed. Boats had gone up the river before. The problem was that doing so meant slow progress against the current, with men straining at oars, poles, or whatever other means they could find to force a boat upstream. His boat had shown him something more useful. With steam doing the work, they could climb the current at speed without exhausting a crew to do it. And he had learned the exact shape of the machine that would have to be built to make that practical, reliably and again and again. Somewhere down the coast, under Fletcher's hands, that shape was already taking iron. This one had one job: prove that the current could be beaten without tiresome labor, and tell him, through wood and shudder and white water, precisely what the real one would have to be.

Nontheless, it had done its job. He turned her downstream, and let the current he had just beaten carry them home for free.
