Book 2, Chapter 12: The Dry Hall

The court came up to receive them at ten on the Monday morning, and Armand, who had sat through a great many councils in his life and knew what a room said before anyone in it opened his mouth, knew within the first minute that this room was furious at having to be a room at all.

It was a hard dome, like the guest rooms, but large: the largest Ilenne said there was. It lay under the Rim on the city's inner side, a little deeper than the rooms where they slept, and it was long, and high in the middle, and the ribs of its roof went over in a long double row like the bones of something big that had died on its back. The panes between them were dark. The sea was dark on the other side of them, a deep clear dark with the green glow coming up through it from far below, so that the whole hall lay in a light like the inside of a bottle. Stone benches ran in tiers down both long sides. At the far end, on a low step of black stone, a seat cut from the rock itself, with nothing on it. At the near end, by the bell door they'd come in through, a single bench for guests.

They had pumped it out for strangers. Ilenne had told them that on the stair. It had been flooded since the winter, as you kept a hard dome flooded when you didn't need it, and the Keepers had begun emptying it the morning after the door, slowly, a day and a night, so that the land people could stand in it on their legs and breathe. You could still smell the sea in it. The black floor was wet in the corners.

And the court had come up into it, out of its own water, from the floor of the caldera, on its legs.

They came in through a second bell door at the far end, behind the seat, two and three at a time, and took the benches without looking at the guests. Men, all of them, on the benches; older men mostly, in long coats with a dull shine to them and stones at the throat and the wrist, and their hair still wet from the shafts, running down their backs. They came in with their colours held still and their faces set. Several of them were breathing through their mouths. One, a heavy grey man with a great deal of pale stone about him, sat down on the lowest tier opposite and coughed, deliberately, for some time, and looked at the guests' bench while he did it, and Armand understood him perfectly. In their own water at the floor, near the Breath, they sat as they liked and swam as they didn't. Here, in a dry room under the Rim, among the Rim's craftsmen and their furnaces, they had to walk on a wet floor and breathe the land's thin stuff like fish in a bucket, for the land's sake.

"They don't like it," said Zale beside him, very low.

"No," said Armand. "I wondered why it was up here at all."

Ferra, on Zale's other side, already had her book open on her knee. She didn't look up. "Because it's dry," she said. "Nothing said in a dry room is for ever. They receive strangers where nothing's for ever, because they don't trust strangers with it."

Armand thought about that, and settled his cane between his knees. "A civilised arrangement," he said. "We could use one at home."

The Keepers came in last of the court and didn't sit. They stood together against the long wall on the left side, apart from the benches, six or seven of them in their plain grey coats, and Ressar stood at their front with a teaching stone held flat against his chest in both hands, like a man holding a tray he means nobody else to touch.

Taurre came in through the near door behind the guests, alone, with his slate. He went to a place on the right side, by the end of the lowest bench, where there seemed to be a mark on the floor, and stood on it.

And then the queen.

She came in at the far door on her legs, alone, without guards; the twelve stayed in the bell behind her, and Armand saw the captain's shape through the water sheet before it stilled. She walked the length of the floor between the tiers to the black step at the end and sat down on the seat cut from the rock, and put her hands on her knees, and looked down the hall at the guests' bench. She wore a coat today, dark, close, to the ankle, and the band of white stone across her brow. Her colour was as still as it had been at the door.

The court did nothing that Armand could see. It didn't rise and it didn't bow. But the coughing on the lowest tier stopped.

Emeric was sitting at the centre of the guests' bench in the king's coat, dry now, the dragon on his back hidden against the stone. He'd steamed it through the small hours on Friday and finished it that night, and had worn it down the stair this morning without a word, and he hadn't sent for the sword. He sat upright, and his hands were on his knees as the queen's were on hers, and Armand thought it was probably on purpose.

Ilenne sat at the end of the guests' bench, by the aisle, nearest the court. She would put the court into Kaldo for them as it went, she'd said, low; it was the only way, and the court knew it, and would let her because it would rather she did it than one of its own. And when she spoke for herself, Zale would put her into Kaldo for them, badly. Zale had said that was fine, he was used to being the worst at things in this city.

A heavy man on the right side stood up. Not the cougher. A tall one, younger, broad, with his hair bound back and a collar of red stone round his throat.

Armand had heard from Zale how he'd come up out of the sea on Thursday morning at the queen's door, and he'd seen him go back into it on Saturday, slowly, with the disgrace of it on him like a wet coat. There was nothing of Saturday on him now. He stood on the dry floor of the hall in a long red-brown coat with his colour perfectly level and spoke to the queen, clearly, not loud, and Ilenne put it into Kaldo for them under her breath a sentence behind.

"The Rim's house," she said. "Ushar speaks for it. He says what the notice says. The binding at the door is not made. He gives the three. A land man. The witnesses. The door." She listened. "He says the house asks the court to find it. He says nothing else."

He sat down. That was all. He'd given the grounds in about a minute and sat down, and let them lie there in the middle of the floor for everyone to look at. Armand, who'd seen a great many young men stand up in a great many halls and talk for half an hour because they liked the sound, thought better of him for it at once, and liked him less.

Ilenne stood up.

The hall moved. It wasn't much. A stirring along the benches, a turning of heads, colour going here and there and away like wind on a wheat field, and then one of the older men on the left, near the Keepers, said something short and hard without getting up.

Zale leaned in to Armand. "That's the throne word," he said. "And war. I know those two." He listened again, and shook his head. "She said on the stair somebody would. Women aren't heard about the throne or war. That'll be him saying it."

Ilenne didn't look at the old man. She looked at the queen. And she said, in a clear level voice, a short thing, and stopped.

"That's hers from the stair," said Zale, low. "It isn't the throne, or war. It's a binding. A binding's a home matter." He sat back. "That's clever. Is that clever?"

"It's very clever," said Ferra, without looking up.

The old man said something else, longer, and his colour moved at last: a darkening, slate coming up from the throat. Several heads on his tier went with him. The queen didn't speak. She looked down the hall, not at the old man, not at her daughter. At the wall on the left.

At Ressar.

Ressar took one step forward from the Keepers' wall, onto the floor of the hall, with his stone against his chest, and said four or five words in his flat voice that had no tune left in it.

The old man stopped.

Ilenne turned her head to the bench and gave it them, low and fast. "He says his office keeps the songs. All of them. And the songs of binding are home songs. Sung in the home. Always by the women of the house." Her colour didn't move. "So it's a home matter. So I'm heard. He says that's all."

Zale let out a breath beside him.

It was all. Ressar stepped back to his wall. The old man sat with his slate colour on him a little longer, and then let it go, not all at once, and looked at nothing. Nobody on the benches argued. Armand watched them not argue and understood that he'd just seen something he'd have given a good deal to have on the record at home: a man who'd been born a daughter of a small house and had given up his voice for its seat, standing up in front of the whole court to say that the songs were women's and so were their matters, and not one man on those benches able to call him soft for it.

The court had a precedent now, and it couldn't deny where it came from.

Ilenne turned round from the aisle and looked at Ferra.

Ferra closed her book on her finger and stood up, beside Ilenne, on the dry floor of the hall. She didn't go forward. She stood at Ilenne's shoulder, a little behind, as you'd stand beside someone reading out a contract you'd drafted; and she spoke in Kaldo, quietly, a sentence at a time, and Ilenne put each sentence into her own speech for the hall, word for word, in her own clear voice.

The hall noticed whose words they were within about a minute. Armand watched it notice. It looked at the land woman standing at the heir's shoulder in her plain coat with her book shut on her finger, and it understood what it was looking at, and it didn't like it. But Ressar had ruled, and a home matter was a home matter, and the land woman was in the heir's home now by the heir's word; and so the heir spoke, and the hall heard her.



It took them most of the morning, and Armand watched Ferra do it as she did the castle's accounts at the quarter-day, one line at a time, and never raised her voice.

The first ground. A land man can't be bound.

"Ask the Keepers," said Ferra, through Ilenne. "Ask them what the stones say a binding is."

The queen looked at Ressar again, and he stepped forward again, and this time he turned his stone outward from his chest and held it up in both hands so the hall could see its face, and read it. Not much. Armand counted the lines his finger went down: four. Ilenne gave them in Kaldo as he went, though there was no need; every merfolk in the hall had learned them at Ressar's knee and was saying them with him in silence, Armand could see it, lips moving.

"In the water," said Ilenne. "Before witnesses. Both say it. Once."

Ressar lowered the stone.

"And who," said Ferra, "may be bound?"

Ilenne asked it. Ressar looked at the stone, and then at the court, and said one thing.

"He says the stone doesn't say," said Ilenne.

"Then it doesn't say," said Ferra. "It says how. It doesn't say who. If the court wants a rule about who, it can make one. It can't find that the binding broke a rule that isn't on the stone." She looked down at her closed book as if it were open. "That's the first."

The second. The witnesses were the queen's.

"Two witnesses stood," said Ferra. "The captain of the twelve, who's the queen's man; the house is right about that. And Taurre of the watch." She looked across the hall at Taurre on his mark. "Ask him who the watch is."

Taurre answered without being asked twice. He answered in his clerk's voice, level, as if he were reading a tally off his slate, and he didn't look at the Rim's bench or the queen's seat while he did it. Ilenne put it over. The watch was drawn from the Rim and from Below both, by old custom, half and half. It answered to the crown as an office, not to any house in it. It kept the water. It kept the record. It had witnessed bindings before at the queen's door and at other doors. It had never been the queen's.

"And there was a third," said Ferra. "Who wasn't a witness. Who was there."

The hall waited.

"A pilot brought the land ship to the door on Thursday," said Ferra. "A Rim pilot, by the Rim's right. I paid him in iron. He was in his boat in the slip at the gate, in the water, a few yards from the steps, when the binding was said." She turned, slightly, toward the right-hand benches. "Ask the house whether its pilot is lying, if he says it was said."

Ilenne asked it. She asked it to Ushar.

Ushar didn't answer.

He didn't refuse. He didn't stand up. He sat on the right-hand bench with his colour level and his face pleasant and said nothing at all, and let the question go past him into the air of the hall, where it hung and then sank, with nobody to pick it up; and Armand, watching, thought that was well done too. A man who answered that question either called his own pilot a liar in front of the court or agreed with the land woman. A man who didn't answer it lost nothing but the point.

"The third," said Ferra.

The door is outside the city.

"It's a gate," said Ferra, "in the Rim's own breakwater. The Rim's pilot charged me for the walk to it. The Rim's water runs from the markers to the gate, he said." She paused. "If the door's outside the city, then the fee I paid him was for a road outside the city, and the Rim has no right to charge for that by its own custom, and I'd like my iron back."

Ilenne put it into her own speech with no colour at all in her voice. She did it perfectly.

Somebody on the deep benches laughed.

It was one man, on the left, two tiers up, a stout old merman with a gold-brown skin: a short bark of a laugh that got out of him before he could stop it. He stopped it at once. His colour went pale with mortification and he looked straight ahead at nothing. But it had been heard. Armand saw it go along the tiers, a thing nobody showed and everybody caught, the way a smile goes round a lecture room when a student has been very rude to a professor who deserved it.

Ferra sat down. She opened her book on her knee again.

"That's all three," she said to Armand, under her breath. "I'd have liked a fourth."

The court showed its colour.

Armand had been told how it went. Ilenne had told them all on the aft deck, and again on the stair this morning, because she wanted them to see it and know what they were seeing. One voice for each great house, its head or its heir. One voice for the Keepers, as an office. Warm for. Slate against. And a held colour was no voice at all: a refusal to be counted, rude, and allowed in this room for exactly that reason.

The queen said a single word, and the hall's colour changed.

It happened at once, all along the benches, like lamps turned up and down in a street. Armand had been watching the man nearest him and missed most of it, and had to look again. On the right, Ushar: slate, hard, all at once, as he'd been on Thursday, from the throat down. On the left, two of the old men on the second tier sat with their colours held perfectly still, grey and level, the plate Imani called it. Not for, not against. Held. And everywhere else on the benches, and along the Keepers' wall where Ressar stood at the front with his stone, the warm colour, coming up into faces and hands like light coming through a window.

The queen counted aloud.

She did it slowly, in her own speech, naming each house as she went, and Ilenne gave the numbers in Kaldo at the end for them, quietly. Seven great houses and the Keepers. For: four of the houses and the Keepers. Five. Against: the Rim's house. One. Held: two, and not counted.

Five to one.

The queen said a few more words. They had the shape of a thing that had been said in that hall, or in the hall at the floor, a great many times.

"She says the binding was made," said Ilenne. Her voice was perfectly steady. "She says the three objections fail. She says it is found."

Taurre wrote it on his slate. Armand watched him do it. Three lines, the same as Saturday, and a mark.

Zale, beside him, breathed out through his nose, slowly, all of it. His hand had gone to his chest, to the cord under his shirt. Armand didn't think he knew it had.



And then Ushar stood up again, and Armand saw that he'd been waiting for this all morning, and that the three grounds had never been the thing.

He spoke as he had before, clearly and not long. Ilenne began to put it into Kaldo, and stopped after the first sentence, and then went on, and her voice had gone very careful.

"He says: the court finds it made. Good. The house accepts the court." She listened. "He says: then the house asks for the other thing. That the court set the binding aside. Not because it wasn't made. Though it was made. For the throne's good. A land man, bound to the heir. While both live." She stopped. "He says: set it aside, notwithstanding."

The hall went still.

Armand had heard halls go quiet. He'd heard the Tower's great chamber go quiet when a vote went the way nobody had wanted, and he'd heard a lecture room go quiet when a boy in the back row asked the question everybody had been not asking for a week. This was not that. This was the stillness of a room in which every person knew exactly what had just been touched, and every person had decided, separately and at once, not to look at it.

The colours didn't move. Not one. All down both sides of the hall, every great house held its colour, and so did the Keepers, and so, on her black seat, did the queen.

Armand looked at the queen. She was looking at Ushar. Her hands hadn't moved on her knees.

Something was under this. He could feel the shape of it as you feel the shape of a stone under a rug: not what it was, but where it was, and how big, and that everybody in the room was walking round it. A binding was once. They'd all just said so, with Ressar, in silence. So how did you set one aside while both of them lived? And yet a man had stood up in the court and asked for exactly that, and nobody had laughed at him, and nobody had told him it was impossible. They'd gone still.

He looked at Ferra. Ferra was looking at Ushar too, with her pencil still on the page.

The queen spoke. Not long.

"She says," said Ilenne, "to the season." Her colour was held so hard it shone. "The binding stands as made. The motion waits. To the season. She says that's all today."

To the season. Not refused, Armand thought. Not granted. Put on a shelf, in front of everybody, with a date on it.

Ushar inclined his head to the queen, the right depth, exactly. He didn't look at the guests' bench. He didn't sit down at once, either. He stood a moment longer on the dry floor with his colour level and his hands at his sides, and Armand had the strongest impression that there was another door he could have tried, here, now, and that he was standing in front of it deciding not to open it; and then he sat down.

Ferra wrote something. Armand saw it upside down as she turned the page, because she wanted him to.

He didn't try the succession. He knew better than to ask that in here. Cleverer than his house.

He took out his own notebook, the small one, and wrote under the morning's date in his tower hand, small: The motion to set aside. Everyone in the hall knows what it touches. Nobody will say it. I don't know what it is. He looked at that for a while, because it was the most important thing he'd written all week, and closed the book.



The court came up again on the Tuesday, and it was worse, and Armand had known it would be from the way the queen said that's all today.

It began with a question that wasn't one. A deep lord on the left, one of the two who'd held their colour on the Monday, a lean old merman with a narrow face, stood up on the second tier and asked the guests, through Ilenne, with great courtesy, how eight ships stood in the air.

Ilenne put it over. Then she sat with her hands in her lap and didn't look round at Emeric.

Armand had a bad moment then. He could see Emeric deciding how to answer it, and the answer Emeric would give a class: plainly, with names, the stones and what they pushed against. He wanted to put a hand on his arm. He didn't have to. Emeric had seen Ressar.

The Keeper of the Stones had come forward again from the left-hand wall without being asked. He had a different stone today. It was larger, and older, darker, a slab as big as a cart's tailboard that two of the younger Keepers had carried in between them and stood on its edge on the floor, facing the hall, and he stood beside it with one hand on its upper edge. Its face was cut all over. Not lines, this one. Pictures.

Ressar didn't read it to them. He recited it. He stood beside the stone and spoke in his flat voice, slowly, and Armand understood after the first minute that it was a set piece, a thing every child in the hall had learned in that same voice from that same man, word for word; and Ilenne beside him gave it in Kaldo low, a sentence behind, and her voice changed while she did it into something Armand hadn't heard from her before. A child's voice saying a lesson.

There had been a city on the sea. The First City. The oldest of them and the greatest. It hadn't stood on rock, as the Rim stood. It had floated. It had floated on stones, a whole city on the open water, with its towers and its gardens and its people, and it had gone where it liked across the sea. And in the war, the dead ground had come over the water. Not only over the land. Over the sea. It had come over the water to the First City, and every stone under the city had gone dark at once, and the city had gone down, with everyone on it. All of them. On the stone, Ressar's hand went to a carving low on the slab's face that Armand could just make out from the guests' bench: towers, tilting, and lines of water closing over them, and small shapes falling.

That was all. Ressar took his hand off the stone.

The hall was quiet, the way a hall is quiet after a thing it knows by heart has been said again aloud.

Taurre had told them this at the ring, Armand thought. In three sentences, on the platform, as a fact about the city. This was the same fact in its own clothes, as the stones kept it, said in front of the land king by the man whose office was to say it. It was history. He'd have staked his name on it. A people didn't carve a lie into a slab that size and teach it to its children for two thousand years.

Ushar stood up.

"He says," said Ilenne, and her own voice came back, "the land's ships hang from stones. The same."

That was all he said. He said it and sat down again. And it was true. The K Amara hung from stones. So did the K Discovery and the K Endeavor and every hull of theirs that could lift. They hung from stones exactly as the First City had floated on them, and if the dead ground came, they'd go dark.

And it told the hall nothing whatever about what anyone meant to do. Armand saw that with great clarity. It was a true sentence put next to a true story so that the hall would hear a third thing that neither of them said. It was the oldest move in any council chamber he'd ever sat in, and it was well done, and he watched it go along the benches and settle.

He found that he had his notebook out again. He wrote, under Monday's line, in the small hand:

The First City: they learned not to float a city. They never learned why it sank.

Because it wasn't the stones that had drowned it. It was that there'd been nothing under them. They'd learned that for cities, and built the Rim on rock, and stopped there; they'd never taken it any further than cities. Kaldo had a rule for it that went into every hull and every pump, and nobody here had ever needed one. He thought, too, of a room of iron in the K Amara's hold, past the markers, and what was in it, and shut that thought very firmly.

He didn't say it. It wasn't the moment, and it wasn't his to say, and if he'd said it in this hall it would have sounded exactly like what it was: a land man telling the merfolk their own history.

He put the book away.

An old man spoke after Ressar.

He came forward from the end of the left-hand benches, from among a knot of people who sat together and apart from the rest, older, plainer, their coats without shine. The old families of the shallows, Ilenne said, under her breath. Houses whose people had died on the land's coast. He was very old. He walked with a hand on the shoulder of a boy, and his colour was grey-white, not held, just grey, like Ilenne's in the tank on South Sentinel, and he spoke without standing straight.

A party had gone back. Long ago, after the war; not long after, but after. A party of the shallows' families, going back to the coast where their people had died, to see it, and to see their graves. They'd gone in the old way, swimming, with stones for light and warmth and the long swim. And on that coast their stones had gone dark. All of them. Not slowly. One morning, dark. They'd come home without them, those who came home.

"He says it could have been the water," said Ilenne. "The thin water. That country is thin. It does that." She listened. "He says nobody can say. He says the old families say it was the promise. That they broke it, going back. That it nearly came due." Ilenne paused. "He says nobody has gone back since. Nobody tries."

The old man went back to his place on the boy's shoulder.

Armand looked along the bench at Emeric, and Emeric didn't look back. He was sitting exactly as he had been, upright, with his hands on his knees. But he'd gone very still, and Armand, who had watched him think for years now, knew that stillness. It was a man hearing the shape of a thing he would have to think about for months, and not liking the shape.



Ushar stood up a third time when the hall had settled, and this time he spoke for longer, and Armand didn't need to be told that this was what he'd come up from the floor for.

Ilenne gave it in Kaldo as he went. Her voice was flat with care.

"He says: there was a promise. After the war. The people of the dry land promised the old peoples. To leave them be." She swallowed. "He says: eight ships. Armed. Past the wall. Under a king with gold eyes." She didn't look at Emeric. "He says: is that leaving us be? He says: if that isn't the promise broken, he doesn't know what would be. He says: the queen asked us here. Good. The queen's word is the queen's. He says: it reaches as far as the queen's word reaches. No further."

It went along the benches. Armand watched it go. Not slate, mostly; something stiller and more uncomfortable, colours held a little too hard, people not looking at each other.

Ushar wasn't finished.

He turned, for the first time in two days, and looked straight down the hall at the guests' bench. At Emeric. At the coat.

"He says," said Ilenne, and her voice wavered for the first time, very slightly, and steadied, "and the land's king comes to the queen's hall. Wearing on his back the beast that burned his own ship."

The hall made no sound at all.

Armand knew where he'd had it. Ferra had told the watch herself, at the ring, when Taurre asked what had burned the ship: a dragon's fire, plainly, because a hazard was a hazard and she wouldn't lie on the watch's slate. Taurre had read the arrivals out at the door. And the winged people on the cliffs had watched the black ship come in, and traded the Rim its news for its fishing. Ushar had stood in the water at the door and seen the gold thread over Emeric's shoulder with his own eyes, and let it lie until now.

It wasn't even an accusation. It was a picture: a coat with a dragon on it, and a ship burned by one, side by side. There was no name in it, and the hall heard it for what it was; Armand could see the faint distaste on some of the faces on the deep benches. It worked anyway. He'd seen it work in every chamber he'd ever sat in. You could despise a man for it and still, afterwards, find you couldn't look at the coat without seeing the ship.

Emeric didn't answer it.

He didn't move. He sat in the king's coat with his back against the stone, and the dragon was against the stone too, where nobody could see it, and he looked at Ushar with no expression at all, as he'd looked at him in the water at the door. He let it go past him. Armand thought it was the hardest thing he'd watched him do since Saturday, and on Saturday he'd watched him stand at the top of a stair with one hand round his own wrist.

Emeric asked one question.

He asked it when Ushar had sat down, on the dry, in his own voice, plainly, and Ilenne put it over word for word. Armand had wondered whether he would, and had hoped he'd ask that one and only that one, and he did.

"What does it say?"

The hall looked at him.

"The promise," said Emeric. "Its words. What does it say?"

Nobody answered at once. Then the queen moved her hand on her knee, the smallest movement, toward the left-hand wall; and Ressar, who had been standing against it with his slab beside him and his hands folded, spoke.

"He says it was sealed," said Ilenne. "Long ago. Where all the peoples' seals went. All of them, all the old peoples, one place." She listened. "He says it isn't here. He says there's no copy here. He says no Keeper of Aumere has read it, in his life or his teacher's."

So that was it. There was a promise, and the house said Kaldo had broken it, and nobody in the hall could say what it said, and the one man whose office it was to know the old things had never read it either. Ushar didn't need the text. He only needed nobody else to have it either. Kaldo couldn't answer a charge whose words it had never seen, and the court couldn't judge it, and so it would sit there, unanswerable, true or not, and every hall in the city would carry it home tonight.

Armand took his notebook out a third time. He found the page from the ring, from the platform outside the gate on Wednesday night, where he'd written, after Ilenne said the old men would talk about a promise: The promise. Nobody alive has read it. Find out who did. Under it now he wrote: Sealed where all the peoples' seals went. Not in Aumere.

He underlined it. Then he sat with the pencil on the page and his cane between his knees and looked across the hall at the old man by the slab, who had taught the stones without his singing voice for as long as anyone here could remember, and had never read the promise either.

Not yet, Armand thought. It was a beginning.



Sorrel called Emeric on the set at dusk, in the guest rooms, while they were eating.

She called most evenings now, from the K Discovery outside the ring, with the day's sky. Armand had come to like the calls. Sorrel reported weather the way she reported everything, as if the sky were a ledger and she was the only clerk on it who could add.

"Birds," said Sorrel. "On the outer cliffs on the north island. You'll say I keep reporting birds."

"You keep reporting birds," said Emeric.

"They're too big to be birds. I'll stop when they get smaller." A pause on the set. "Two of them went off the cliff at dusk. Twenty minutes ago. They didn't hunt. They didn't come back. One went east by north, low and fast. One went south. Different ways. I've marked both bearings in the book. I thought you'd want to know before the light went."

Emeric was quiet.

"Thank you, Sorrel," he said.

"It's my sky," said Sorrel, and was gone.

Armand put his cup down.

The winged people traded storm warnings and messages with the Rim, Ilenne had said, for fishing rights on its cliffs. The Rim's news was what they carried. And today the Rim had news: a fleet of the dry land had been charged in open court with breaking the old peoples' promise, and couldn't answer, because it had never read it.

Two of them. Different ways.

"Well," said Armand. "That's every door between here and wherever we're going."

Ferra was writing. She didn't look up.

"Before we get to it," she said.
