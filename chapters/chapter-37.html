Chapter 37 - Dead Man's Float

The man above them did not shout, and Emeric understood, even then, even bound and gasping in the bottom of a boat with a dead guard sliding off his leg, that the not-shouting was the most dangerous thing about him.

A furious man would have come down the ladder. A furious man would have wanted to see it, to make it cost, to be the hand that closed the account himself, and a furious man could be counted on to make the mistake that anger makes, which is to come within reach. The level voice did none of that. Emeric felt him move across the deck overhead, unhurried, placed, a man reading the sounds through the boards the way Emeric read the hold through the mana, two knots gone quiet, the wrong two, and doing what a professional does at the exact moment a plan stops being a plan, which is not to salvage it. Salvage was for men who loved their plans. This one did the arithmetic once, coldly, and got the only answer the new numbers allowed.

The job had been the prince, alive or dead, it had never mattered which, and that was the thing that damned all of them, because a man who is worth the same to you cooling as breathing is a man you will not risk your neck to keep breathing. The prince had turned out to have a hidden hand and a loose father and two guards were down and the quiet delivery to the deep channel had become a fight in a hull, and a fight in a hull was a mess, and a mess was a thing you did not carry home. So he did not come down. He took the one action that turned every remaining problem, the prince, the father, the boy, the two men he had spent, the whole embarrassing wreck of a clean job gone wrong, into a single problem with a single answer, and he made that answer the river.

Emeric smelled it before he understood it, the sharp animal smell of oil going onto old dry wood, and then the light came, not lamplight, the wrong color and the wrong hunger, blooming up between the boards from the deck above and running down the pitch in the seams faster than a thing that size had any right to move. He had built a barony on the study of how effects behave once they exist, and he needed no study for this one. A tarred hull over deep water with a fire set in the middle of it was not a fight anymore. It was a clock.

And there was nothing in him to fight it with. A mage with a Water crystal in his fist and the training to drive it might have bought the hull a minute, might have smothered a smaller fire in a larger boat, but Emeric had no crystal, had never once carried one out to a faulting rune, and the crystal was the whole of it, the step from mana to a made effect he could not take in his own head, mage country, not his, written into none of the books he had come with. His magic lived in his hands and his stones and his standing systems, and his hands were bound, and there was no stone, and the systems were three men and a river away waking lamps for no one. The one faculty that answered a bound man in the dark was the delve, and the delve could feel the fire blooming in the ambient mana as a swelling absence where the wood had been, and could do nothing to it at all, because a delve moved knowledge and read a room and had never once laid a hand on a physical thing. He knew fire. He knew exactly how it behaved once it existed, the pitch running in the seams, the draw of air up through the open hatch, the heat stacking under the low beam, all of it his to the last detail, the downstream kind of knowing he could always trust, and every scrap of it told him the one same thing, which was that this fire could not be fought and could only be outlived. Knowing how a fire works and being able to put one out were two different things, and the whole width of the gap between them was the ground he had spent his second life pretending he did not stand on.

"He's burning it," Emeric said, and got his knees under him in the heat and the smoke starting to stack under the low beams. "He's burning all of it. Rylan, the boy, now, the water, now."

He heard the man he had named to himself go over the side. It was the cleanest sound of the whole night, barely a sound at all, no splash worth the name, a man who had a way out planned for every night stepping off a burning boat into black water as calmly as off a stair, and gone, upriver and away, unhurried even in leaving, giving nothing away even to the dark. He had lost the job. He had not lost himself, and he had not, Emeric understood with a cold that had nothing to do with the water, been beaten. He had cut. There was a difference, and the difference was going to matter for a long time, and Emeric filed it, even there, even then, because the machine did not stop for a fire any more than it had stopped for anything else.

Then there was no more time for filing. Rylan had Finn up out of the corner and against his chest, the boy's face pressed into his neck, and the fire was between them and the ladder and the ladder led only up into more of it, and the hull was old and the seams were already weeping flame and the one door left was the one the level voice had chosen for all of them, the low breach where the boards had begun to burn through toward the waterline. Rylan went at it with his shoulder and the burning wood gave, and the river came in cold and black and Emeric had never in his life been so glad of anything so terrible.

"The boy first," Emeric said. "Go. I'm behind you. Go."

Rylan's freed hands had gone to Emeric's wrists first, to the wet knot at his back, because that was the shape of the man even now, to set right the thing in front of him before he saw to himself, and Emeric twisted out of the reach of it. There was no time for the knot. It was tight and it was soaked and it was behind him where neither of them could work it quickly, and every second spent on it was a second the fire was not giving and the boy did not have, and a child could not be carried across a burning channel on a father whose hands were full of another man's rope. "Leave it," Emeric said. "I'll come bound. I know how. Just go."

Rylan went, the child clamped to him, out through the breach into the channel, and Emeric put his own bound body after them into the water the boat had been set over, and the cold took the breath he had.

It took everything. It closed over him like a fist and his hands were still bound behind him and his first animal instinct was to fight it, to thrash, to do the one thing that drowns a bound man faster than the water ever could. He did not. He made his body do a drill he had watched other men's bodies learn, on a screen, in a life that was not his, the training they gave men who might go into black water with their hands tied and no bottom under them. There was a name for it and a rhythm to it and the rhythm was the opposite of everything panic wanted. You did not fight the water. You went limp in it and let it carry you up, and you broke the surface for the one breath and no more, and you sank again, unresisting, a dead man's float, a corpse's patience, and you rose and took the one breath and sank, and you did not spend a single motion the water did not give you back. He sank and rose and took his one breath of smoke and cold night and sank again, and the burning boat lit the underside of the water orange above him, and he did not drown, because for once in his clever life the right answer was to stop trying to be clever and let a thing already known do the work.

He rose for a breath and there were shapes on the water that did not belong to any of it, the wrong direction for enemies and the only direction for Ward, and a voice he knew coming across the channel low and hard, and light, and hands.

He learned the rest later, in pieces, the way he learned everything, but the shape of it he had at once. Ward had not committed to the ford. A thing that will not commit is a thing that wants you looking at it, and the men at the treeline had probed and drifted and pressed nowhere and cost nothing, and somewhere in the second hour of the nothing the wrongness of it had met, in Ward, the wrongness of a man named Rylan he had spent a week not trusting and been talked out of, and the two wrongnesses had turned him around. He had come off the ford at a dead run with the better part of the watch behind him, back to a wall that answered to one hand and a board that showed him one lamp lying to an empty room on stretch nine, and he had not needed to read runes to read that. He had needed only to know that the empty side of the wall had a lamp crying wolf on it and the one man who answered such cries was not in his bed, and that a wall with two sides and a fire now on the river had exactly one route out that was not the ford. He had put a boat on the water without waiting for it to be a good idea, and he had come down the river the enemy came up, and he arrived now the way he arrived at everything, without ceremony and without doubt.

They got Finn out first, and then Rylan, and then Ward's own hands closed on the collar of a bound man going under for the third patient time and hauled him over the gunwale and into the bottom of the boat, and Emeric lay there and breathed and shook and could not stop shaking, and behind them the boat that had been his jail burned down to the waterline and hissed out and took with it two men the level voice had spent and left, burned to nothing along with the hull, because a professional who leaves no witnesses does not draw the line at his own. That was the last accounting of the night and the coldest, and Emeric made it lying in the bilge with a child crying somewhere near his head and did not have the strength to file it anywhere at all. Marrow had burned his own men to erase a job. Emeric had spent his whole second life sorting men by their uses without once feeling the cruelty in the habit, and here at the end of the water was the habit shown back to him at its full and final size, a fire on a river and two names no one would ever say, and he found that he had seen enough of it, permanently, to last him the rest of his life.

They turned the boat and took him back up the river he had gone down, toward the wall he had built to watch one direction and the barony that had slept whole inside it while its one hand was carried out the back. Ward cut the rope off his wrists with a knife and did not say anything about the state of them, and did not say anything about the fit or the fire or the fact that the lord of Kaldo had been fished out of the deep channel by the one man he had spent a week talking Ward out of distrusting. That conversation was coming. Emeric could feel it waiting behind Ward's silence the way he could feel the mana in a stone. But not yet. Not on the water. For now Ward wrapped something dry around him and put the boy's father at an oar and turned them for home, and the wall stood above them on its ridge as they came, whole and finished and faithful, having watched the black river take Emeric and having watched it give him back, and having done, all night long, a guard's work exactly as it was built to do, which had never once included the only thing a guard was ever for.
