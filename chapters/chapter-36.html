Chapter 36 - The Living Stone

They took the hood off in the hold, and the first thing he saw, before he saw the men or the lamps or the low black beams a hand above his head, was Finn.

The boy was in the far corner with his knees to his chest and his back to the hull, and he was not hurt in any way Emeric could see, and that was the whole cruelty of it, that they had kept him whole, because a whole boy was worth more than a hurt one to a father doing sums at an oar. Finn looked at his own father and his father could not look back, and Emeric watched Rylan set him down against the boards where they told him to be set, and understood that every question he had left was answered. The aunt in Milford who had never been asked for. The cover story. The week of careful faults. It had all been true and it had all been here, in this hold, on this water, the entire time, a ledger kept in plain sight and read by everyone but him.

There were more of them than there would be later. He counted by the lamps and the shifting weight of the hull, five, six, working men and one who did not work, the level voice from the bank, near the ladder, giving nothing away. Emeric had heard that voice for the length of one order and had already filed the sound of it under a heading he did not have a name for, and would not until a bright banquet hall months and a lifetime ago swam up out of nowhere, a man standing wrong at the edge of a room full of people who belonged, breathing the same placed, overfull, giving-nothing breath. He filed the resonance with it, a low frequency in the mana around a body, specific, memorable, worth nothing he could ever bring to a hall, and he set it down and turned his attention to the thing that mattered, which was that they were making a jail of the hold and putting all of them in it.

Him. The boy. And Rylan.

That was the datum that turned the whole night over. He watched them bind Rylan's wrists, loosely, carelessly, a knot for a man no one expected to fight, and he understood the carelessness was itself a reading, the level voice's reading, that Rylan was a spent thing, a father wrung dry, compliant to the bone, a door that had already been walked through and did not need locking. They were right about the man they had made. They were wrong about the man underneath, Emeric thought, and set that aside to be sure of later.

The lamps went with the men. The level voice went last, up the ladder without a word, the way a man leaves a task he considers finished, and the hatch came down, and the hold went to the kind of dark that has weight. When it settled there were two left. Two guards, by the sound of them, sat near the ladder with the bored heaviness of men set to watch cargo, and beyond the hull, faint, the long slow patience of deep water, the deep channel above stretch nine where no one walked and nothing was ever heard.

And in the dark, with nothing left for his body to do, Emeric did his accounting, because it was the only comfort he had ever really trusted, and the account came out wrong in a way that stopped his breath.

Two guards. Deep water. A boat that had gone quiet and stopped rowing hard. You did not leave two men to watch cargo you meant to deliver. You did not sit a boat over the deepest channel in the district and put out your lamps and send everyone but two men up top. You did that when the cargo was not going anywhere but down, when the two men left were not guards but the hands that would do the putting-down, and the deep water was not on the way to a destination, the deep water was the destination. Rylan had carried his lord aboard to buy his son's life, and the deal had never been a deal. There was no aunt in Milford at the far end of this, no reunion, no boy handed back to a father who had done what he was told. There was this channel, and two men, and the clean arithmetic of a professional who did not leave witnesses and had never once intended to leave three.

He heard the exact moment Rylan understood it too. The caught, ragged, grieving breath at the far end of the hold changed, went still, went to something colder and more level than Emeric had ever heard from him, the breath of a man who has just been shown that the worst thing he ever did bought nothing at all, that he sold his lord and it was not enough, that they were going to take the boy anyway, and now had no reason left in the world to keep him alive. A man will do a great deal to save his son. There is a particular and terrible freedom that comes to him at the instant he learns he cannot, and Emeric heard it arrive.

Under the account, the thing he could not make himself hate. A boy in a corner with his knees to his chest. He had tried, on the bank, when his eyes were covered, to find the anger a betrayed man is owed, and it would not come, and now he understood why it would not come, because the anger had nowhere to land. Finn was not a cause. Finn was a consequence. The boy was in this hold, over this water, for exactly one reason, and it was not Rylan's love and it was not Rylan's weakness, it was that Emeric was worth taking, and everything downstream of that worth, the leash and the lie and a father made into a door, was only the price the world had set on him. If he followed the fault to its root the fault did not stop at the man who had carried him aboard. It ran back past Rylan, all the way back, and came to rest on the plain fact of what he himself was worth to men like the one who had just climbed the ladder without a word. A man cannot hate the guard whose son sits in the dark because of what the guard's lord is worth. He can only get the boy out of the dark.

Which meant the two men by the ladder, and no delve would move them.

He had reached for the Pull already, out of habit, the moment the hold went black, and it was there, and it would even answer him, and it was worthless in exactly the way it had been worthless all night. It would take him into the clean quiet room of his own knowledge, and it would let him reach the far cold edge of the network and wake a lamp in an empty guardroom, and it would do faithful silent work for no one, three men and a river away. It moved knowledge between minds and it built things in a false space and it could not put a hand on a man in this hold and stop his heart. He was not going to delve his way off this boat. He knew that with the flat certainty he brought to all his best and most useless understandings.

And then, holding the Pull, sitting in it, blind in the weighted dark with nothing to build and no stone under his hand, he did the thing by accident that he would spend the rest of his life doing on purpose.

He had always delved into a stone. That was the whole of what he knew delving to be, a hand laid on rock long enough for the mana in it to open a door. But there was no stone in reach, only the dark and the boat and the men in it, and his sense, cast out for a stone and finding none, kept going, and touched the mana that was simply there. It was everywhere. It had always been everywhere, the ambient stuff of the world, thin as air and just as unregarded, filling the hold the way water fills a jar, and he had never once thought to read it because a man does not think to read the water he is standing in. He sank his sense into it now the way he sank it into a stone, not reaching for a door this time, reaching only to feel the shape of the room by the way the room disturbed the medium, and the hold resolved around him, not in light, he had no light, but in the returns.

"The living were denser than the dark. That was the first thing, the thing that made it work at all. A body sat in the ambient mana as a knot sits in grain, heavier, and a living body did more than sit: it gave off a low resonance of its own, a hum in the medium, the same specific frequency he had learned to hear around a caster and never thought to hear around a man. Two knots by the ladder, humming, breathing. A small knot in the far corner, humming faster, a boy's quick frightened pulse laid into the mana like a tremor. A knot beside him, Rylan, gone very steady.

He could not see their faces. The ability was too new and too crude for that, and for now it always would be, mass and motion and the pulse of the living and nothing finer. And it cost him. He could feel it costing him: the ambient mana was thin, and holding the read of it drank from a tank that was not large. Past the walls of the hold it thinned to nothing, a finite bubble of knowing with him at its center and blindness a stone's throw out. It was not sight. It was a blind man's palm laid flat on the surface of a pond, reading the shapes by the way they bent the ripple home. It was enough. It was, for the first time all night, a thing his enemies did not know he had."

He turned his head a fraction toward the steady knot that was Rylan, and spoke under the sound of the water, so low it was nearly only breath.

"When I fall," he said, "you take the near one. I'll give you the far."

A pause. Then, just as low, the level cold voice of a man with nothing left to lose: "You can't reach the far one. You're bound."

"I don't have to reach him," Emeric said. "I have to make him reach me."

He waited until the two knots by the ladder had settled into the slack inattention of men on a long dull watch over cargo that could not run, and then he gave them the one thing a man watching a bound and beaten prince is trained to fear above all others, which was the prince dying on his watch before the men above were done with him. He let his breath go ragged and then wrong, and then he threw his whole body into it, a seizure, a hard convulsing arch and a strangled sound and his bound hands thrashing behind him, damaged goods, a conversation, the exact thing the level voice had said he was tired of. In the mana he felt both knots come off the wall at once, both of them, because a dead prince was a failure they would both have to answer for, and that was the whole trick, that fear made them careless together instead of watchful apart.

They came to him in the dark and he was already reading them by the ripple, where their weight was, where their knees would land, and he did not need his hands, he had never needed his hands for this, he had a body and the floor and two years of never once training it and a single clear memory of how men on a distant world took another man down with the legs when the hands were gone. The near guard bent to him. Emeric hooked a leg up and over and behind, caught the man's neck in the vee behind his knee and his own shin, the way he had seen it done and never done it, and clamped, and fell, dragging the guard down off his balance and across his own body toward the far side, toward Rylan, reeling him in like a line, his thighs locking the man's throat against his shin while the guard's hands scrabbled at legs that would not open. He held. It was ugly and it was not clean and the man bucked and he held, squeezing the dark into the guard's head, and he had bought, with a fit and a leg and a memory, the one thing the whole hold had turned on, which was one guard down and out of the fight and the other one alone.

And Rylan, loosely bound, spent, compliant, a door already walked through, took the far man in the dark the way a father takes the thing between him and his son. Emeric did not see it. He felt it in the mana, the second standing knot driven hard into the boards and going still, and heard the short wet finish of it, and then felt Rylan's hands, free now, on his own locked legs, and a low voice saying "I have him, my lord, you can let go, I have him," and Emeric let go, and the near guard slid off his shin and did not get up, and the hold had two men in it who could still move and both of them were the ones the level voice had been wrong about.

Above them, through the boards, the man who did not shout had heard the whole of it, the fit and the fall and the two knots going out, and was already, being a professional, three steps into what came next.
