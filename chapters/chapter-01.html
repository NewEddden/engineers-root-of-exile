Chapter 1 - Session Fourteen

The rain hadn't started yet. Marcus noticed that much and not much else, which was going to matter later.

He'd signed the consent forms three weeks earlier, in a waiting room that smelled like new carpet and old coffee, six months to the day after the layoffs. The savings he'd spent a decade stacking were draining fast, and he'd needed money yesterday. Direct skill induction, the recruiter called it. Cognitive scaffolding transfer, according to the paperwork. In practice it was a headset, a chair that reclined further than any chair needed to, and ninety minutes a week of someone else's decade of engineering intuition getting pressed into the grooves of his brain like a foot into wet sand.

Human lab rat, if you wanted to put it kindly. He mostly did.

He'd gone in skeptical, though not for the reason everyone else was. Friends kept reaching for the horror-movie version, the one where the recipient loses his mind or starts leaking out of his ears. Marcus's doubt ran a layer under that, down where an engineer looks at what a system implies instead of what it's sold as. If skill could be pressed into a man from outside, then some working assembly of a person was portable. Nobody at the company had said that part out loud. Nobody was ever going to. He sat with it longer than the consent form deserved and signed anyway, because there wasn't a real decision left in it by then.

He'd put applications in the week the layoffs landed. Denholm. Metalworks. Furna. A dozen others he'd stopped keeping track of. All of them were taking their sweet time about calling him back, and rent didn't care about anyone's hiring timeline. Then the trial invitation came through, and it came laid out flat: publicly traded company, clean standing, the study announced to the world with the recipients listed by number, personal details stripped. They had made it close to impossible to quietly bury a body, and that, more than the money, was what got him to say yes.

They were testing several things at once and needed the sample wide, so they'd taken a mix, people deep in a discipline and people who'd never studied anything at all. Scientists had speculated about it for as long as there'd been scientists to speculate. The brain was a marvel nobody had ever put a name on the deed for. If god is real, then he, she, or it operates on a different level entirely. Two and a half petabytes of storage, give or take, running an absurd load of passive computation, and doing all of it on twenty watts. Twenty watts ran a desk fan.

Marcus did that arithmetic more than once in the recliner while the headset warmed up, because it beat the other arithmetic, the one that ended with him hollowed out and racked in a server room as somebody's macabre cold storage. Papers were signed either way.

The trial team emailed him after the first session and told him to live normally. They'd monitor for changes.

Within the month, the small local manufacturing firm he'd interviewed with called. It paid well. The trial covered what it didn't. For a while the two schedules ran alongside each other without touching. One day a week he drove out to the facility, put the headset on, let the chair recline past anything structurally reasonable, and let a stranger's career get pressed into him in ninety-minute doses. The other days he was the new guy at Denholm, learning where the good coffee was hidden and which drawings were trustworthy and which were aspirational.

Nobody at the firm knew what he did on the mornings he came in late. Nobody at the trial knew what he was doing with what they gave him. The division held.

What nobody had warned him about was how well it worked.

Halfway through session four he caught himself sketching a load path on a napkin at a diner counter, tracing a force down through a stack of components, member to member, all the way to the ground. That was a thing good engineers took years to do without effort, following the chain instead of reading one part in isolation. He'd done it in the margin of a napkin while waiting on his eggs.

By session six, Denholm Fabrication's numbers had stopped agreeing with themselves.

It was a Tuesday call, nothing on the invite marked urgent, the usual half hour with Denholm's applications engineer walking Procurement and Quality through the machined brackets for the new housing assembly. Marcus was half listening, drawing in the margin of his notepad, and then his eye caught on a tolerance callout for the mounting boss and wouldn't let go. He pulled the mating part up on the second monitor. Then the fastener spec behind that. Three components. Three drawings. Three engineers who had each done their own job correctly and never once talked to each other. Every tolerance was fine on its own. Stacked worst case on worst case, the assembly wouldn't close.

He waited for the doubt. It usually showed up right about here, the second-guessing, the surely somebody already checked this. It didn't come. What came instead was certainty, quiet and ordinary, the same certainty you have that a door you just walked through was a door.

"Hey, can we back up?" He cut into the applications engineer mid-sentence without quite meaning to. "The boss tolerance on the bracket, the shaft OD on the sub-frame, the fastener's minimum engagement length. Run those worst-case together and there's no clearance left. It's going to bind."

The silence on the line came in two parts. The first was the newcomer speaking at all. New hires didn't see a problem like that, and on the rare occasion they did, they didn't have the nerve to say it out loud on a call with three departments listening. The second part was eight people quietly pulling up drawings they'd been pretending to have memorized.

"...Let me check that," the applications engineer said, in the voice of a man who was going to be checking it for the rest of the afternoon. A newcomer breaking that hard against convention was worth the double check on its own.

He was right. Denholm took two days to confirm it and revise the drawing. It cost the company nothing but the mild indignity of Procurement asking Marcus three separate times how he'd caught it, like the answer might come out different if they kept asking.

He didn't have a good answer. Two months ago he wouldn't have known to look, and if he'd looked he wouldn't have trusted himself. No way these experts made a mistake an amateur like me could find. Now the looking just happened on its own, unbidden, the way you notice a crooked picture frame in someone else's house. He shrugged it off and went back to his sketch.

It wasn't until the drive home that it occurred to him to wonder what session ten was going to feel like, if six felt like this.

By session ten, it had stopped staying in his field entirely.

It was a Saturday. A coworker's kid's birthday, of all places, and Marcus was standing at the edge of a backyard holding a beer he wasn't really drinking when the coworker's father-in-law started coughing. Wet, effortful, the kind everyone at the table politely agreed not to hear. The old man waved it off. Heart medication, a new one, doctor still sorting out the dose.

He said the name of the drug and the number in the same breath, and something in Marcus clicked into place that had no business clicking. A whole shelf slid out where a second earlier there had been nothing at all. Contraindications. Interaction profiles. The specific mechanism by which that drug, at that dose, in a man that age with that cough, could be doing more damage than the thing it was prescribed for.

He'd never studied medicine. Sitting there with a warm beer sweating through his palm, he wasn't sure he could have told you the difference between systolic and diastolic a year ago. The knowledge arrived whole regardless, confident, uninvited. Same delivery as the tolerance stack-up in session six. Except this time there was no napkin, no CAD file, nothing anywhere in his life that explained why he'd know it.

"You should mention that cough to whoever adjusted the dose," he said, working to make it sound like a guess. "Sooner than your next appointment."

The old man's eyes narrowed. More amused than offended, but not by much. "You a doctor now?"

"No." Too fast. He heard it land wrong on the way out.

"Mechanical and software, right? Kevin said you build brackets and mess around with code." He wasn't letting go of it the way he was supposed to. "So how do you know what my heart medication does?"

"Read about it somewhere. Had a relative on something similar."

The lie came out smoother than the truth would have, and that bothered him more than the lie did.

Two seats down, Kevin had stopped mid-conversation to watch it happen, gone still in the specific way of a host recalculating what he actually knew about a guest he'd invited. Marcus caught the look and read it clean. Not suspicion yet. A data point Kevin would sit with later, the same way Marcus was learning to sit with his own.

The old man laughed it off eventually. The laugh came a beat too late to undo the pause in front of it, and he didn't bring the cough up again for the rest of the afternoon. Marcus let it go too. Pushing meant explaining how he knew, and he had no explanation that didn't end in a very different kind of appointment.

On the drive home he did the math nobody at the trial had asked him to do out loud. Session four, structural, adjacent to his own work, explainable as an unusually good teacher. Session six, still mechanical, still his lane. Session ten, a domain he had no connection to whatsoever, arriving with the same unearned fluency as everything else.

Whatever the trial was doing to him had stopped respecting the fences around what he knew. It had stepped into somebody else's yard.

He made a note to raise it at his next monitoring session.

By twelve, the focus started. Hours at a stretch on any problem at all, the world dropping away around the edges until only the thing in front of him stayed lit. It reminded him, oddly, of the way his college friends used to describe Adderall.

It didn't stay at his desk either. At Denholm one afternoon he watched a coworker walking a line across the design floor and saw it coming the way you see the end of a sentence before somebody finishes saying it. Somebody had left a length of small-diameter pipe on the floor. Given the man's stride, he'd roll his foot on it, lose his balance backward, and catch his head on the edge of a desk on the way down.

Part of Marcus wanted to watch. The part that liked confirming a prediction. It wanted the data, and the data cost one concussion.

He let that part lose.

"Hey," he said, already moving, "pipe on the floor, about three steps ahead of you. Watch it."

That was the shape of it now. Knowledge on hand the moment he decided he needed it, and sometimes arriving whether or not he'd decided. It should have been crushing. It wasn't. No pressure behind the eyes, no overload, nothing to point at. Take away the weekly drive and the fact that he could answer damn near any question put to him, and he'd have had no way to tell anything had changed at all.

By session fourteen he'd stopped worrying about the company's secret and started worrying about his own. Structural and software fit inside his own field. Medicine didn't, and neither did a pipe he'd seen three steps out. If the fences came down that easily, he had no idea what was still standing on the other side of the one that used to be him.

He signed the log for the day, same as always, and wrote none of that down.

He was pensive the rest of the afternoon. The session ended, and he walked back the same route he always walked. By now it was automatic. He could have done it blindfolded, could have done it asleep.

Today wasn't going to be one of those days.

He was crossing the street, still turning the morning over, when his mind snapped all the way back to the surface at the sight of a little girl stepping into the road.

After that, nothing arrived in an order he could file.

A crash. Then the ground, and no pain in it, and no feeling attached to any of it, like a room going quiet around an alarm he hadn't registered yet. Realization hadn't caught up.

A woman on her phone. A hand holding a smaller hand and then not holding it. A kid in a yellow jacket taking two quick delighted steps off a curb, because a curb is the most interesting geography a four-year-old knows. Marcus had been moving before he decided to move, some borrowed reflex getting there ahead of him. He got the kid clear.

He didn't get clear himself.

Then came the strange unhurried part, the part nobody's supposed to get. He seemed to be standing a few feet above the street looking down at the shape of himself, a dented bumper, a woman screaming into a phone at a 911 operator. If there was a later, he wanted to know how long that had actually lasted. It felt like minutes. Four seconds, probably less. That gap alone was worth a whole session's thinking, assuming he ever got to think again.

He noted, without much feeling, that a couple of seconds before the crash the woman had been far enough into whatever was on that phone to let go of her daughter's hand. He noted the driver's face through the windshield, the particular blankness of a man dissociating at forty miles an hour. He found the irony funnier than he probably should have. He'd been paying attention. If he'd kept paying attention, he could have had the girl clear and been clear himself.

Then the shout came.

"Emeric!"

A man on a high ledge, calling a name that wasn't his.

One moment there'd been a street, a car, a woman, a child, bystanders arriving from three directions at once. The next he was standing in a room with ceilings too high for the space and gold-threaded banners hanging dead still in air that didn't move. The man shouting wore a crown. His face had gone rigid with something that could have been rage or could have been disappointment.

Who in the flying fuck is this man yelling at me, and why.

That was the last clear thought that belonged entirely to Marcus.

Inside the same breath, a dam went.

Twenty-two years arrived at once, not as a story but as a texture, the way a smell arrives before you've decided to notice it. He knew the banners were his father's colors. He knew the shape of this room the way you know your childhood kitchen with your eyes shut, hip-checking the same drawer for twenty years without once looking at it. He knew his mother was dead, and that one came with a name attached and no face to hang it on. Amara. He knew, distantly, that his hands were wrong. Too small. The whole frame was smaller than it should have been.

The disconnect sat underneath everything else, and he couldn't get a measurement on it. Was he the man from Earth or the man from here? Who got whose memory?

Why the king was shouting came half a second later, in fragments. A woman's face, a diplomat, going cold at a party he couldn't quite picture. A room full of people turning at once. The held-breath quality of a scandal that hadn't finished happening yet. It came the way the medicine had come at the barbecue, whole and unearned.

All of it added up to a young man who had not been listening to a word his father said. By the time his attention actually landed in the room, the sentence was already halfway out.

"-have made your choice for you, since you haven't made any for yourself in twenty-two years."

Marcus, who had been dead four seconds ago by his own best accounting, was now apparently twenty-two years old and possibly a prince. He opened his mouth. Nothing came.

The walk to his chambers took longer than it should have, mostly because Marcus, no, Emeric, kept stopping to look at things he had no business remembering. A tapestry. A crack running through one particular column, the same crack he'd traced with a fingernail at nine. The memories gave every ordinary object in the castle a doubled quality, new and old at once, and he still couldn't settle which of them was doing the looking. Marcus of Earth or Emeric of Ostenne. His feet knew where to turn before he did, and he let them. Arguing with his own legs was a fight he'd lose.

The guard walked half a step behind him, silent, hands loose at his sides in the way of a man who'd spent fifteen years making sure his hands were never far from something. He was enormous. Six and a half feet, built like a wall that had been given a grudge.

The name surfaced on its own. Ward.

Fifteen years. He'd known this man since he was a child.

"Do you have any thoughts on today's events?" Emeric asked, mostly to hear what his own voice did in this body.

"Nothing comes to mind."

That was the whole answer. Flat, complete, delivered without a flicker, and so exactly the shape of fifteen years of this same dynamic that Emeric nearly laughed.

Nearly.

"Fuck," he said instead, quiet, to himself. "This can't be. An isekai?"

Ward didn't ask what the word meant. He kept walking, half a step back, a man who had stopped being surprised by this prince a long time ago.

The room, his room, apparently, was bigger than his old apartment and colder than he expected. A trunk already sat open at the foot of the bed, which meant someone had known how the conversation with his father would end before it started. He worked through it on autopilot, hands folding while the rest of him sat somewhere behind his own eyes, still catching up.

He was nearly done when his hand closed on something small at the back of a drawer he hadn't consciously decided to open.

A crystal. Dull, greyish white, the kind of thing that belonged in a kid's rock collection instead of a prince's chambers. He knew, in the same effortless way he now seemed to know everything, that the household considered it worthless. Decorative. A trinket he'd stopped bothering with years ago. There was a reason he'd kept it. That part stayed just out of reach, sliding off whenever he turned toward it.

He almost put it back.

His fingers closed instead, and something in his chest went loose that he hadn't noticed was tight. Not relief. Quieter than relief. Closer to recognition, with comfort somewhere underneath it.

He had no idea why. He pocketed it anyway.

His departure was early, so he went to sleep. Maybe the whole thing was a dream and he'd wake up on Earth carrying the memories of a man from somewhere else.

He didn't.
