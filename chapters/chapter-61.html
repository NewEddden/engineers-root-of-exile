Book 2, Chapter 22: Guessed

The watch's runner found Bram at a quarter to six on the Tuesday morning, on Zale's deck at the gate, eating a cold potato out of his hand and arguing with Dagny about a hinge.

It was a good hinge. That was the trouble. It was the after hatch's, and it had been squealing since the wall, and Dagny had taken it off at five and found nothing wrong with it at all, and Bram had said there must be, because a hinge with nothing wrong with it didn't squeal, and Dagny had said this one did, and they'd got about that far when the boy came over the gangway in the watch's dark coat with a slate in his hand and stood at the top of it, out of breath, looking at both of them.

Linnet read the slate for them, slowly, with her finger on the marks and her lips moving, because Ilenne had taught her the watch's twenty commonest and she'd been practising.

"The salvage quay," she said. "A man. Under. The watch asks the land to come." She looked up. "There's a mark here I don't know. It's next to man."

The boy said something and touched his own back, high on the right, and then touched his nose, and made a face.

"Ah," said Linnet. "Blue. And he smells."



The salvage quay was the last of the Rim's working quays before the breakwater turned north, a long black pier on stone piles over deep water, with the salvagers' sheds along it and their gear stacked on the planks: lines, floats, nets with weed dried into them, a row of big iron hooks. At its far end, where the piles went down into the dark green, there was a small crowd of the Rim's people on their legs, and two of the watch in their dark coats with slates, and Oddrin.

Bram knew Oddrin. He'd seen him the first week, at this quay, standing up in his boat talking a bronze up out of the sea on the end of the animal; and he'd thought about the animal a good deal since, at night, more than he'd tell anybody. Oddrin was a short wide man with a beard that didn't seem to belong to his face and hands like a cooper's, and he was sitting on the edge of the pier with his legs over the water and his face the colour of putty.

The animal was below him in the water. Bram could see the shape of it: long and dark and as thick through as a barrel, holding itself against the piles in the green with a slow push of its flukes, nosing at something under the pier that Bram couldn't see. Oddrin had a line to a harness on it. He'd been trying to call it off, the watch officer said in his careful Kaldo, since before light, and it wouldn't come.

"He went down," said the officer, nodding at Oddrin, "to see what it wants."

"And?"

"And then he came up."

Oddrin didn't look round.

The watch wanted Kaldo's hands on it, the officer said; he said it twice, carefully, so that Bram would have it right. The man under the quay was marked with the land's mark. So the land would bring him up, before the watch, and nobody on the Rim could say afterwards that a Rim hand had done anything to him on the way.

Bram said that was fair. He said they'd want the Challenger's lights and a diver, and somebody to talk to the water, and that two of them were at the bottom of the sea.

The officer had already sent a runner down the stages.



Zale and Ilenne came up in a little under the hour, and the Challenger came round from the gate with her floods on before they'd reached the quay.

Zale was in the Mark II with his helm under his arm and his side strapped under it, grey round the mouth from the stages. Ilenne came out of the water at the foot of the pier on her legs, with the sea running off her, and stood at the edge looking down at the animal and not at Zale, and her colour was the pale polite green it had been for a week.

Finn was on the Challenger's casing. He was going down as dive lead; that was settled before anyone asked. He had the line reel and a knife and his own helm, and he looked at Zale's ribs, and at Zale, and said, "You're going on the line behind me, and if I pull twice you come up, and you come up even if you think you've a better idea."

"I always have a better idea," said Zale.

"That's why I said it."

Dagny did Zale's harness.

She did it every time he went in. She'd done it since hull four patched the suit's side where the beast had torn it, to Abloh's pattern, stitch for stitch, because Abloh had once told her that if anything of his ever tore she was to bring it home to him in a bag, and she'd believed him. She went round him on the pier with her quick hands, the shoulder buckles, the chest strap, the pack's two hoses, the weight belt, and then she knelt behind him and ran her fingers down the back strap where it lay along his spine under the gill pack's shell, feeling for the stitching.

She stopped.

Bram saw her stop. He'd worked beside Dagny long enough to know the way her hands stopped when they'd found something they didn't like; they didn't jerk. They went still and stayed on it.

"Bram," she said.

He came round. She had the back strap's lower edge folded back with one thumb, where it met the belt, and under it, pushed in between the rubber and the strap's backing so that it would lie flat against a man's spine, was a sliver of stone. Grey. Dull. Thin as a fingernail and as long as the first joint of his thumb.

He didn't touch it. He didn't need to. He could feel it from where he knelt, the way you can feel a draught from a door before you see it's open: a small cold pulling, a little hole in the air.

"Drain," he said.

Zale had gone very still, with his back to them.

"It's in your strap," said Bram. "At your spine. Where the pack holds it on you." He spoke slowly, because Zale was going to want every word of it later and it was better to have them in order. "In the air, a sliver that size you'd not notice. A bit tired. Off your food. In the deep, with the water pressing it into you and your bracing pulling on everything you've got, it'd take you a little at a time, every dive. Until one day you'd be at the bottom of a shaft on a line and not have the strength to swim up it."

"And then I'd drown," said Zale.

"And then you'd drown."

"In deep water," said Zale. "Where land men drown." He was quiet a moment. "Nobody'd call it anything."

Ilenne had turned round at the edge of the pier. She was looking at the sliver in Dagny's thumb, and her colour had gone from the pale green to nothing at all, held flat and blank, which Bram had learned was worse.

"The suit," she said. "At night. It hangs in the bell."

"Yes," said Zale.

"In my mother's house." She didn't say anything else. She didn't need to.

Dagny worked the sliver out with the point of her knife into a fold of oilcloth and wrapped it, without once touching it with her skin, and gave it to Bram, and he put it in his inside pocket and felt it there against his ribs like a cold coin. Then she went over the rest of the harness inch by inch, every strap and every seam, twice, and found nothing else; and Zale stood and let her, with his helm under his arm and his face turned out to the sea.

"Go on, then," he said, when she'd done. "There's a man under there."



Bram watched it from the pier. There wasn't anything else for him to do.

The Challenger's floods went down through the green under the quay and lit it from the gate side, white, hard, the arc lamps laying long shadows of the piles across the water like the bars of a cage. He'd seen merfolk turn their faces away from those lamps. They didn't now. The crowd on the pier stood along the edge in the glare and looked down into it, and a woman near him put her hand over her mouth.

The animal moved off when Ilenne went in. She went down the pier's ladder into the water on her legs and was a tail before she was under, and she spoke to it, Bram supposed; he couldn't hear anything. It turned its long head to her, and backed away from the piles in a slow heavy curl, and hung off in the green at the edge of the light with Oddrin's line slack on it, watching.

Then Finn went down the line, and Zale behind him.

Two of Voll's knights had come down the pier with their rifles while the Challenger came round. They stood at the edge with them slung and their hands nowhere near them. Nobody had told them to. A rifle stopped being any use about three feet under the sea; the water took a round in a yard or two and dropped it, and everyone on the pier knew it, and nobody pretended otherwise.

It took twenty minutes. Bram timed it on Dagny's watch. Twice Finn's line went taut and stopped, and Ilenne came up and spoke to the watch officer and went down again. Once a stream of bubbles came up off the piles that Bram didn't like the look of, and Dagny put her hand on his arm and said it was the pack venting, and he knew that, and was glad she'd said it.

They brought him up alive.

Finn came up first with the line, and then Zale with one arm round the man's chest, slowly, kicking badly with his strapped side, and Ilenne under them both holding them up. They'd cut him out of a tangle of old net and line lashed round a pile ten feet down, where somebody had tied him with his arms behind it so that he couldn't swim and couldn't drown, and left him.

The watch took him onto the pier. He lay on the planks in the floods' glare with the water running off him, and the crowd drew back, and Bram looked at him and understood what Oddrin's face had been about.

He was a merman, tall and narrow, on his legs; and he was grey. Not the grey of the Rim's working people, warm and brown under it. Grey all through, like ash, like a fish that's been in a bucket too long. His eyes were open and he was breathing, short and shallow, and his lips moved, and nothing came out of them that was a word. On his left wrist, tight, was a cuff of dull dark stone in a band of plaited cord. And down his back, over his right shoulder blade and down to his waist, the dye: Kaldo's dark blue, a great dried splash of it, and the smell coming off him even in the sea air, fennel, sharp and green.

"That's Mira's," said Dagny, quietly.

"That's Mira's."

The officer knelt by him and spoke to him. The man's lips moved again. His throat moved. There was nothing on it; no colour at all, not even the flat held blank that Ilenne could do. Just the grey.

Ilenne was on the pier beside them, kneeling, with the water running off her hair.

"He can't," she said. "Drained. Like this, he can't hold a colour. Without colour he can't make half our words." She looked at the cuff. "Off. Get it off him."

Dagny got it off him, with the knife, by the cord, without touching the stone. It went into a second fold of oilcloth, and into Bram's other pocket.

The station took him, under the watch's guard, on a board between four men, up the quay to the clinic with the two watch officers walking beside him and a slate going ahead. Before they'd got him to the end of the pier one of the officers had cut a name on it. Bram asked what it said, and the officer told him.

"Lusse," he said. "The salvagers know him. He's off a boat at the far islands. They say." He looked at Oddrin, still sitting on the edge of the pier with his legs over the water. "They say a lot of things this morning."

Oddrin got up then. He looked at the watch officer and at the slate, and at the board going up the quay, and at Bram; and then he went along the pier to his shed without saying anything to anyone, and went in, and shut the door.

The animal was still hanging off in the green at the edge of the light, watching.

"I'd still have one," said Bram.

"Of course you would," said Dagny.



He read them in hull four's workshop that afternoon, on the bench under the big lamp, with the doors shut and the knights outside them and Ferra on the other side of the bench with her book.

Two of them. The sliver from the strap, and the cuff off Lusse's wrist. He laid them out on clean cloth side by side and looked at them through his glass, a long time, before he said anything, and Ferra didn't hurry him.

They were both drain stone; he'd known that on the pier. But it wasn't the stone he was looking at. It was the cutting.

"There's a mark," he said.

It was on both. On the sliver, on its flat face, near one end; on the cuff, inside the band, where the cord would hide it. Small. Three short cuts, one across the other two, and a dot. Somebody's sign, cut with a fine point and a steady hand, the way a smith stamps his bars.

"That's a yard mark," said Bram. "A maker's." He looked up. "Taurre's clerk gave us the list. The marks the bench knows."

Ferra had it in the back of her book, where she kept the things she'd been given and didn't trust. She found the page and ran her pencil down it and stopped.

"The old families," she said. "The shallows. A cutting yard of the old families of the shallows. That's their mark."

Bram sat back.

So that was what they said. A drain stone in the queen's son-in-law's back strap, and another on the wrist of a man drained grey and tied under a quay, and both of them signed by Dessaun's people. The shallows, who'd gone slate in front of the whole court. Who'd lost people on the land's coast. Who were the court's swing vote, Ferra had said, with two houses held. It fitted. That was the trouble with it. It fitted the way a key fits that's been filed for the lock.

Ferra was looking at the sliver.

"A cutter who means to kill," she said, "doesn't sign the knife."

Bram looked at her.

"A man who leaves his name on it," said Ferra, "wants it read." She didn't write it down. She looked at it a while longer, and then at the cuff. "This was meant to be found. Not on Zale; on Zale it was meant to drown him. But afterwards. When they pulled his body out of a shaft and somebody went over his suit." She turned a page. "And this one was meant to be found this morning. On a man nobody wanted to talk."

They had Saban on the relay at four, from the harbour set at home, a long way off and faint, and Ferra read him the mark and the two stones and what she'd said about the knife.

Saban was quiet for a bit.

"She's right," he said. "But she's being kind about it. A man who signs a knife isn't just wanting it read. He's wanting it read by somebody he's chosen, who'll go where it points and look stupid in front of everybody." A pause. "What do drain stones cost there?"

"We don't know."

"I'll tell you what they cost everywhere I've ever heard of them. A great deal. They're rare and they're dear and you can't make them do anything useful except take, so nobody buys one who isn't rich enough to waste it." The set hissed. "That's a house with money. Two of them, wasted. One in a strap and one on a wrist. Your shallows haven't got money. I'd be surprised if they've a coat each."

"They've one coat each," said Ferra. "Grey."

"Well, then," said Saban, and rang off.



Tess came on at six, from the Works, because Bram had asked for her by name and Ambrose had sent a boy running.

"I've got it out," she said.

"Both locks?"

"Both locks. It's on the bench. I've got the big glass on it." Her voice was flat and close; she'd be leaning over it with the set at her collar and her hair tied up. "The bident tip. South Sentinel. The one that came out of Ilenne's side."

It had been in the two-lock box under the mill since South Sentinel. Bram had seen it once, the day it went in, and hadn't wanted to see it again.

"Tell me the cleave," he said.

"You tell me yours first. I don't want to see what I want to see."

So he told her. He had the sliver under his glass. He told her where the drain stone had been split from its parent, the face of the break under the mark: the way the grain ran across it at a slant, not along; the little stepped ridges down one edge where the cutter's chisel had gone in twice and not once; the way the whole face had been worked afterwards, faintly, with something fine, to take the edge off, so that it would lie flat. Shaped on purpose. He gave her the angles as near as he could read them, and the count of the ridges.

The set was quiet.

"Again," said Tess. "The ridges."

He gave her the ridges again.

"Bram," said Tess, after a while. "It's the same."

He'd known. He'd known since he'd seen the stepped edge. He'd seen it before, once, in the lamplight in the mill cellar, on a piece of stone that had come out of Ilenne.

"The grain across the face, at a slant," said Tess. "Two strokes in, not one. Worked flat after. It's the same hand. Or the same yard, the same way, a man taught by the man." Her voice didn't change. "It's not the shallows' mark on mine. Mine hasn't got a mark. But it's cut the same."

"Years apart," said Bram.

"Years apart."

Ferra wrote something in her book and drew a line under it.

The house that had chased Ilenne to the land. The house whose people had put that tip in her, in the water off South Sentinel, and lost her, and gone home. Not the shallows. Somebody who'd had the shallows' mark cut on two blanks so that they'd point at the shallows. And the cut under the mark, the part you couldn't see unless you took a glass to it and had the other one to hold against it, belonged to a different hand altogether, the one Ushar's house used.

"Put it back," said Bram. "In the box. Both locks."

"I'm putting it back," said Tess. "Don't tell me what to do with my own box."



On the Wednesday morning Arthur and Mira went to the old families' cutting yard, with a watch officer to speak for them, and Bram went too, because they were going to be talking about cleaves and neither of them knew a cleave from a crumb.

It was at the shallows' end of the Rim, on the last island but one, where the ground went down to the water in shelves and the houses were low and old and patched with ash cement of four or five different colours. The yard was a roofed shed with its front open to the sea and three benches in it and a grindstone turned by a boy with a crank. Two cutters at the benches, both old. Stone dust on everything, grey and fine. It smelled of wet rock.

The older of the two cutters got up when they came in, and looked at the land knights with their rifles on their backs, and at the watch officer, and at Bram; and when the officer showed him the mark, drawn on a slate, he didn't pretend not to know it.

"His," said the watch officer. "He says it's his."

Bram took the sliver out of his pocket in its oilcloth and laid it open on the cutter's own bench, and gave the old man his glass.

The cutter looked through it at the sliver without moving. Then he turned it over with the point of a pin, and looked at the cleave, and then he put the glass down and said something to the watch officer, short, in a voice like dry gravel.

"He says the mark is his," said the officer. "The cut is not."

"Ask him how."

The officer asked. The old man picked up a blank off his bench, a little flat lozenge of grey stone with the mark already on it, rough, unfinished, and held it up, and talked for a while with the blank in his hand.

"He sells blanks," said the officer. "Unfinished. Marked, so a buyer knows where it came from, that it's good stone, the shallows' stone. Not cut to anything. A buyer takes the blank to his own man to finish." He listened. "He says this," and the old man tapped the sliver with his pin, "was finished by a man who cuts across the grain and goes in twice. He says nobody in the shallows cuts like that. He says it's an ugly way to cut. A furnace way."

"Who bought the blanks?" said Arthur.

It was the first thing he'd said since they'd come in. The officer put it over. The old man looked at Arthur for a while before he answered, and when he did it was one word and a name.

"A steward," said the watch officer. "Of the furnaces. A Rim house's furnace steward." He paused. "Maudrec."

Mira had her notebook out. She wrote the name. The old man watched her write it.

Then he said one thing more, to the watch officer, not to them, and the officer didn't put it over until Arthur asked.

"He says he's old," said the officer, "and he sold blanks to whoever came, the way his father did. He says he didn't know. He says if the court wants to drown him for it, he's at his bench."



Maudrec had paid in iron.

The old cutter brought it out from under his bench in a cloth, at the end, when Ferra had come along from the pilots' hall and asked him through the officer how he'd been paid; and he unrolled the cloth on the bench in the stone dust, and there they were. Three bars, a foot long and two fingers thick, flat, dark grey, with the marks of the roll on them and the Works' stamp at one end.

Ferra looked at them.

Bram watched her look. He knew those bars. His own mill had rolled them at home, plain flat bar for the yard's smiths, twenty to the crate. And Ferra had weighed eight of them on her scale in the first week, being Ferra, and written each weight in her book, and counted them into the pilot's boat herself, one at a time, for the fee. They'd been the first thing Kaldo had paid anyone at Aumere.

She took a bar up and turned it to the light and read the stamp. Then she took her small scale out of her bag, the one she carried everywhere, and set it on the old man's bench in the dust, and weighed it. Then the next. Then the next. Then she opened her book at the page from the first week, and ran her pencil down it, and stopped three times.

"Ours," she said. "The pilot's fee. These three, out of the eight." She closed the book. "Our iron. Paid to a pilot for safe passage, and gone from the pilot to somebody, and from somebody to Maudrec, and from Maudrec to here, for two blanks to drown Zale with."

Bram watched her write it. Her face did nothing at all while she did it. Not a line. She wrote the three weights and the stamp and the date and the old man's yard and Maudrec's name in her ordinary small hand, and closed the book on her pencil, and asked the officer to tell the cutter that Kaldo would want the bars kept, by the watch, as they were, in their cloth.

"Nobody's accusing anybody," she said to Arthur, on the way back along the causeway, before Arthur could say it. "It all goes to Taurre."

"I know," said Arthur.

"I'm saying it so it's said."



Bram went down the yard stair that afternoon because he wanted to see how the furnace hands got Below.

That was all it was. He'd been meaning to since the first week. He'd watched them on the Rim for a fortnight, the furnace people on their legs coming up out of a low door at the back of the big kiln yard by the geyser in the morning with their hair wet, and going down it again at dusk, and nobody had ever told him where it went. He'd spent the forenoon at the bench's long hall with Ferra over the steel test, which the bench still hadn't agreed to and still hadn't refused, and he was due at Neris's yard behind the kilns at four, and there was an hour in between with nothing in it. So he took Dagny and went to the low door.

It was at the landward end of the yard, beside the bench's own office and its record room. A bronze hatch in the floor, heavy, standing open, with a bar in brackets beside it to bar it from above. Below it, a stair cut in the rock, going down steeply, with lamp stones in niches. He counted it on the way down. Forty-one steps, a turn, and a landing.

On the landing there was a door that wasn't a door.

It was a shutter: a great slab of bronze as wide as the stair and taller than a man, held up overhead in two deep slots cut in the rock on either side, so that it hung there above the landing like a blade in a guillotine, and you walked under it to go on. Bram stopped dead under it and looked up. He couldn't help it. A thing that size, hanging over your head, held up by what?

The stair went on past the shutter and down a few more steps and came out into a chamber cut in the rock: a long low room, dry, at ordinary air, with benches along one wall and pegs with furnace hands' coats on them; and at the far end of it, where the room met the outer face of the Rim's wall, the sea.

It stood across the end of the chamber in a sheet. A wall of water, upright, smooth as glass, filling a great round opening in the rock from edge to edge, with the green light coming through it from outside and lying on the chamber floor. Held there. Bram walked up to it and stood a yard off and looked at it. He'd seen the water doors at the hard rooms, and this was the same thing and twice the size; he could feel the stone that held it, somewhere in the rock above the opening, humming, steady.

The chamber had a keeper. An old woman on her legs in a leather apron with a slate at her belt, who'd come out of a cubby beside the stair when she heard them and watched them come in with her arms folded. She didn't speak any Kaldo. She didn't need to.

Bram pointed at the water. Then up the stair, at the shutter.

The keeper looked at him the way Maren looked at a man who'd asked what an oven was for. Then she unfolded her arms and showed him.

The seal first. She walked to the sheet of water and put her hand flat into it, to the wrist, and drew it out wet, and pointed up at the rock over the opening, where the stone was. Held. That was how the furnace hands went down, and the merfolk came up out of the sea: through it, the way you'd go through a curtain.

Then she took him back up to the landing and showed him the shutter.

It was held up by a catch. A bronze tongue, thick as his wrist, set into the rock on the chamber side of the slot and hooked under the shutter's lower edge. And the catch was set into a block of dark stone let into the rock: the same note, faint, that he'd felt from the seal. The seal's own stone, or one tied to it. While it held, the catch held, and the shutter hung. If it stopped, the catch would let go, and the shutter would come down its two slots by its own weight and close the stair.

The keeper showed him the rest with her hands. The great counterweight on its chain in a slot of its own at the side, to wind it back up. And the winch, a big bronze drum with a crank on it, set into the wall of the landing: on the chamber side. Only on the chamber side. On the stair side of the slot there was nothing at all, only smooth rock.

"So you can't open it from the stair," said Bram.

The keeper couldn't understand him. She seemed to know what he'd said anyway. She nodded, and pointed down at the chamber, at the sheet of water standing across the end of it, and then she made a motion with both hands, a rushing, upward, the sea coming in; and then brought one flat hand down hard on the other. The shutter. Shut. The sea stopped on the landing and never reached the stair, or the yard, or the town.

Bram stood under it on the landing with his head back.

"Dagny," he said.

"I see it."

"Magic first," said Bram. "The seal holds the sea. That's the stone. And under it, the mundane: if the stone goes, a lump of bronze falls down a hole by its own weight and nothing on earth can stop it, and the stair's dry." He was talking louder than he'd meant to. The keeper was watching him. "It's the layer rule. It's Kaldo's own rule. Somebody cut it into this rock before Emeric's great-grandmother was born." He shook his head. "And nobody here even knows it's a rule. They just do it here, at this door, because a seal failed once and drowned a stair, I'd bet you anything. And then they go home and build the rest of their lives on stones."

"You admire it," said Dagny.

"Of course I admire it. Look at it." He put his hand flat on the cold bronze over his head. "It's beautiful."

The keeper said something then, the only thing she said, and laughed, short; and Bram didn't know what it was, and decided it was probably about him.



Arthur came to the guest rooms on the Wednesday night with Zale behind him, and said what he'd come to say before he'd sat down.

"Take him tonight," he said.

They were round the table in the outer room under the ribbed glass, with the lamp and the black water over them: Ferra with her book, Imani, Bram with his drawing board on his knees, Dagny. Zale had sat down on the bench by the door with his arm held in against his ribs and his face grey from the stages. He'd come up for the post, and then come here instead of going down, because Arthur had asked him.

"Maudrec," said Arthur. "We've his name. We've the old man's word. We've our own iron, with Ferra's stamp on it, sold to him for the blanks. He's a steward of the furnaces. He lives above his yard, the officer says. He'll know by tomorrow that we've been to the shallows, if he doesn't know tonight." He stood at the end of the table with his hands at his sides. "He'll run. Or he'll burn something. Or somebody'll tie him under a quay. Take him tonight, quietly, the three of us and two of Voll's, and hand him to the watch in the morning with everything we've got."

"No," said Ferra.

Arthur looked at her.

"It goes to Taurre," said Ferra. "All of it. The sliver, the cuff, the mark, the old man, the iron. It's gone already; I sent it at six. It's his water and his court and his law. Taurre moves at the court's pace."

"The court's pace is a week."

"Sometimes."

"In a week," said Arthur, "Maudrec's somewhere we can't find him. Or dead."

"Yes."

Bram had been watching Ferra. He'd been at the table in the first week, the evening after the finding, when she'd argued the other way: when she'd wanted a charge laid against the house, said out loud, in front of the court, and Emeric had said no, because a land king's charge against a great house turned a record into an attack. She'd lost. She'd written it down. And here she was a fortnight later holding Emeric's line for him, word for word, against Arthur, because Emeric was at the bottom of the sea and somebody had to.

"If a land knight goes into a Rim house's yard at night with a rifle," said Ferra, "and takes a man out of it, it doesn't matter what the man did. It's the land, in the Rim, at night, with guns. That's what Ushar wants. He's been wanting it since the steps." She didn't look up from the book. "Emeric said: we accuse nobody. Everything goes to the watch. That's the order. I didn't like it when he gave it. I'm keeping it."

"And if Maudrec runs."

"Then he runs, and it's on the watch's record that we gave them his name on Wednesday at six."

Zale shifted on the bench by the door.

"It's my back the sliver was in," he said.

Everybody looked at him.

"I'm not saying which way," said Zale. "I'm saying it's my back. I'd like it said." He leaned his head against the wall. "Arthur's right that he'll run. Ferra's right that if we go and get him, we've done Ushar's job for him." He shut his eyes. "I'd like both of those to be wrong, and they aren't."

Arthur stood at the end of the table for a while.

"It's close," he said at last. "It's close, and I think you're wrong. I'll say that." He looked at Ferra. "But it's your call while he's down, and you've made it. We'll not go."

"Thank you," said Ferra.

"Don't," said Arthur, and sat down.



Bram drew the chamber that night, at the table, after the others had gone to bed, from memory, with the lamp pulled close and the black water pressing on the glass above him.

He drew the yard first, at the top of the sheet: the bench's office and record room on its landward side, the hatch with its bar. Then the stair going down, forty-one steps, a turn. The landing. The shutter in its slots over the landing, with the catch under its lower edge and the dark stone the catch was set in. The counterweight in its slot, the chain. The winch, on the chamber side. The chamber, with its benches and its pegs. And at the far end of it, the seal, standing across the opening in the outer wall, and the sea beyond it, and a little way off down the outer face of the rock the first of the bells.

It was a good drawing. He knew it was. He'd been drawing machines since before he could spell.

Ferra came out from behind the curtain in her nightgown with her book.

"Mark it," she said.

"It's marked. That's the stair. That's the..."

"Not the names. The words." She sat down at the end of the table and opened her book at a clean page and turned it round so that he could see what she'd written at the top of it, five words in a column in her small square hand. "Every line on it. Which of these it is. I'm going to copy it into the book, and when somebody reads it in a year, they'll want to know which lines you saw and which you made up."

Bram looked at the five words.

Seen. Measured. Remembered. Guessed. Blank.

"I didn't make any of it up," he said.

"Then it'll be quick."

It wasn't quick. She went down the drawing with him line by line, with her pencil, and asked. The hatch and its bar: seen. He wrote seen beside it, small. The stair: he'd paced it, forty-one, and the turn; measured. The landing, the slots: measured, roughly, by his own height. The catch, the stone it was set in: seen. The counterweight and its chain: seen, though only the bottom of the chain. The winch on the chamber side: seen. And nothing on the stair side: seen, because he'd put his hand on the bare rock there and felt for it.

The seal: seen. The bells beyond it: remembered, from the keeper pointing, which wasn't the same.

Then Ferra's pencil came to the shutter itself, hanging in its slots over the landing, and the long dotted line he'd drawn under it, down to the landing floor, showing where it went when it fell, and stopped there.

"And this," said Ferra.

"That's the drop."

"Did you see it drop?"

Bram looked at the drawing.

He'd stood under that slab of bronze with his hand on it and admired it out loud to a woman who couldn't understand a word he said. He'd been sure of it. He'd seen the catch, and the stone, and the slots, and the counterweight, and he'd known what it would do. He'd have bet his tools on it. He'd have stood under it.

He'd never seen it fall.

"No," he said.

Ferra waited, with the pencil.

Bram picked up his own pencil, and beside the long dotted line under the shutter, in letters a good deal smaller than he'd used for any of the others, he wrote it.

Guessed.

"I hate this," he said.

"I know," said Ferra, and copied it into the book.
