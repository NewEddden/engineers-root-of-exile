Book 2, Chapter 35: No Mark

The certifying bench tested Kaldo's steel on the Friday morning at ten, in front of the crown's iron buyer, and Bram stood at the end of the long stone table with his arms folded and watched them do it properly.

It was the crown's bench now. Nothing in the room had changed but the seal on the door, a new one cut in the night with the queen's sign on it, and the two clerks at the side table, who'd been the house's and were the crown's and looked as if they were still getting used to it. The same old woman ran the tests. The same press, a big screw press of black iron with a dial on it and a bar across the top that two men turned, older than anything in Kaldo's Works; the same notched stone for the bend test, the same file for the scratch, the same slate with the columns ruled on it that the bench had been filling in for longer than anyone could remember.

She'd had Kaldo's bars since the second week. The house's bench had sat on them. The crown's bench had sent for them on the Tuesday, and here they were.

They did the dwarven iron first, because it was the measure everything on the Rim was held against. Three bars, out of the crown's own store, that had come in by the iron trade from the dwarves' coast over the winter: dark, dense, close-grained stuff, very good. Bram had seen it in the market and liked it. The old woman put the first bar in the press, and the two men turned, and the dial went round, and the bar held and held and then went, with a sound like a bone; and the clerk wrote the dial's figure in the column.

Then Kaldo's.

The Works' rolled bar, the same stuff Ferra had paid the pilots with, and two lengths of the spring steel from hull four's spares, and one of the forged bars Bram had brought for exactly this, with the Works' stamp on it. The press took the first. The men turned. The dial went round past where the dwarven bar had gone, and on, and on, and the men looked at each other and turned harder; and the bar bent, slowly, the whole length of it bowing in the press like a reed in a wind, and didn't break. The old woman stopped them. She looked at the dial, and at the bent bar, and wrote the figure herself.

The bend test, the scratch, the press again. The spring steel. The forged bar. She did every one of them twice, and the clerks wrote every figure in its column, and nobody in the room said anything at all for the best part of an hour except the old woman, who said the numbers.

The crown's iron buyer sat on a stool by the door through all of it. He was a lean grey merman with ink on his fingers who'd been the house's iron buyer until last Friday and was the crown's now, and he had a slate on his knee, and when the clerks had finished he got up and came to the long table and read the figures in the columns. All of them, from the top. Then he went back to the top and read them again.

He went back to his stool and sat on it and looked at his slate.

The old woman spoke, and Dagny, who'd learned more of the Rim's tongue in two months than Bram had in his life, put it into Kaldo at his elbow.

"She says the figures will be published. On the board at the pilots' hall, under the crown's seal. Anyone can read them." Dagny listened. "She says the bench gives no mark."

Bram nodded. He'd known that before he came. The bench's mark was a little sign cut into a maker's work that said the bench had tested it and it was what it said it was, and it was for the Rim's makers, for Aumere's own iron and glass and stone, and it had never in the bench's history been cut into anything made on the land.

"Tell her that's right," he said. "Tell her the mark's for her makers. We're not hers. The figures are all we came for."

Dagny told her. The old woman considered him over the bent bar on her table, and let her colour come up, a small dry warm colour at the throat that Bram didn't know.

"She says," said Dagny, "the iron buyer will be reading those figures in his sleep."



Lirrae's divers took them down to the old grove that afternoon, Bram and Dagny in their Mark IIs, braced, with Emeric behind them, to do the pressure studies the queen had said yes to.

Lirrae was a Keeper. A short square merwoman in the Keepers' grey with her hair cropped close and a slate on a cord round her neck, who'd been given the keeping of the old grove's access ledger by the crown on the Tuesday and had already, Dagny said, found three things wrong with how the house had kept it. She didn't like land people much. She'd said so to Dagny, plainly, on the first morning, and then shown her where the pressure gauges ought to go anyway.

The old grove was below the Rim's inner face, at the third bell down, where the warm seam came up out of the rock: a long low dome of held glass on black stone, old, green-tinged, with its beds of moss and weed inside under a slow drift of warm water that came in at one end from the seam's vents and went out at the other. The water in it was thick. Bram felt it the moment they came through the door; even in the suit, even braced, it pressed on his chest like a hand. The Rim's people breathed it in turns. The rest of the time they breathed the Rim's ordinary water, which was thinner every year, Lirrae said, in the districts furthest from the seam.

And they waited for it. There was a line outside the grove's gate when they came down, and it was there when they went in and there when they came out four hours later. A long line of people, standing in the water on their legs along the wall of the stage beside the gate, with their children. Waiting their turn.

Bram and Dagny put the gauges where Lirrae showed them. They took the pressures at the vents and the beds and the outlet, and the flow, and the warmth, every reading twice, and Dagny wrote them on her slate in the Rim's numbers and Kaldo's both. It was good, careful, dull work, and Bram liked it.

Emeric had his idea about an hour in.

Bram saw him have it. He was standing at the outlet end of the dome with his hand flat on the glass, feeling the thick water go out past it into the thin, and his face did the thing it did when the pieces went together; and he turned round and told Lirrae, through Dagny, what he was thinking. A pump. Kaldo's kind, on the dynamo, at the surface. Thick water drawn off the seam here, where it was thick, and pushed up the stages in a pipe to the Rim's thinning districts, so that they could breathe it at home and not stand in a line at the third bell. One pump, one pipe. It could be built in a month.

Lirrae listened to all of it. Then she held up one finger, which meant wait, and swam off out of the outlet door.

She came back with two glass jars, stoppered, and two stones.

She filled both jars with the thick water from the bed in front of them, carefully, right to the top, and stoppered them. She set one of the stones on the dome's floor, a grey one, and put her hand on it, and Bram felt it go cold through the water, a little cold pocket spreading round it. She set the other beside it and put her hand on that, and it went warm, a fire stone, faint, the warmth of a cup of tea.

She put a jar on each stone and folded her arms and waited.

Emeric was looking at the jars. Bram watched him look. He couldn't feel it himself, not through glass, not much; but Emeric could, and Bram saw his face change after about a minute, and then change again.

The jar on the warm stone was going thin. That was what Emeric was feeling, Bram understood afterwards, when he asked. The thick water in it was letting its mana go, out through the glass and the stopper and into the sea round it, the way warm water lets go of a gas; and the jar on the cold stone beside it was holding, nearly all of it, as thick as when it was filled.

Lirrae said something short.

"She says warm water that moves doesn't keep it," said Dagny. "She says a pipe up the stages is warm water that moves, all the way. She says by the top it would be the Rim's water again." Dagny listened. "She says that's why the grove is here. It has to be grown where it's breathed."

Emeric stood looking at the two jars.

"She's right," he said. "Tell her she's right. I didn't know. I'd have built that pump and pushed sea water up a pipe for a month."

Dagny told her. Lirrae unfolded her arms and studied the land king with her colour doing nothing, and then took the jars off the stones and handed the warm one to Bram, who held it up to the lamp and couldn't see any difference, and the cold one to Dagny, and swam off to the next bed.

When they came out at five, the line was still there. Near the front of it there was a woman in a net-maker's apron with a small boy beside her, standing with his hand in hers, looking at the land people coming out of the grove's door in their glass helms. He should have been at the stones; Lirrae said so, sharply, in passing, and the woman said something sharp back. Dagny didn't put it over. Bram asked her afterwards on the stages.

"Her name's Jorra," said Dagny. "Her district's terrace closed last month. Too thin. They come down here now, every day, three bells each way." She was quiet a landing. "She said if Lirrae wanted him at the stones, Lirrae could stand in the line for her."



Bram asked Oddrin about the animal on the Saturday morning, on the salvage quay, because he'd been wanting to since the second week and the fleet would be gone in a week.

Oddrin had lost his licence. The court had given him a season without it, for selling a posted hazard without reporting the raising, and he sat on an upturned crate outside his shed with his feet apart and his hands on his knees and his gear all round him on the planks, not working, because he wasn't allowed. The animal was in the water under the quay. Bram could see it, a long grey shape down among the piles, nosing at the weed, the way he'd seen it in the second week and on the morning of the dive: something between a seal and nothing he had a name for, with a long blunt head and too many flippers.

He asked through Dagny whether Oddrin would sell one. Not this one; a young one, if there were young ones. He'd keep it in a tank at the Works. He'd look after it.

Oddrin was slow to answer from the crate. Then he talked, for longer than Bram had ever heard him talk, low and slow, with his eyes on the water under the quay.

"He says it's a deep thing," said Dagny. "From down past the city, near the Breath, where the water's thickest. He says out there they're small, like the little grey ones off our coast, and they've been small for as long as anyone knows. He says these ones that live near the caldera, in the thick water, they get big. Bigger every year they live. Stranger." She listened. "He says it's the water that makes them what they are. He says if you took one to the land's thin water, it would sicken in a month. He says he's seen one taken out past the fetch line once, by a fool, and it went grey and stopped eating."

Bram looked down at the shape in the water. It had stopped nosing the weed and was looking up at him, he thought, though he couldn't see its eyes.

"Ask him if I can draw it," he said.

Oddrin could. Bram sat on the edge of the quay with his legs over the water and a board on his knee for an hour and drew it, while Oddrin called it up to the surface for him twice with a sound in his throat and it put its long blunt head up out of the water beside the piles and looked at Bram's board, and blew, and went down again. It was a good drawing. Bram knew it was. He put the date on it and Oddrin's name and the animal's, as near as he could write it, and when he'd finished he held it out to show him, and Oddrin studied it and nodded, and went into his shed and shut the door.



He found the gravel on the way back along the quay.

It was in a heap by the last shed, where the salvagers kept their ballast: black lumps, each about the size of a fist or a potato, rough and knobbly and dull, like burnt bread. The Rim used them for everything. Ballast in the boats, and path gravel on the spine's steps, and fill behind the breakwater's stones; Bram had been walking on them for two months. The salvagers dredged them up off the deep floor round the caldera in their nets by the hundredweight when they were after something else, and tipped them on the heap.

He picked one up because it was heavy. Heavier than it looked. He turned it over in his hand, and then took his knife out and scratched it, across one knobbly face, and looked at the scratch.

Under the black crust it was layered. Rings, like a tree's, or an onion's, fine and dark, round some small hard thing in the middle; and in the bright line his knife had made, the layers had a dull metal sheen that went from almost black to a sort of brown and back.

He stood on the quay with it in his hand.

Emeric came along the quay from the gate with Dagny, and Bram held it out to him without saying anything. Emeric took it. He weighed it in his hand, the way Bram had, and turned it, and looked at the scratch; and then he looked up at the heap by the shed, the whole black hundredweights of it, and back at the stone in his hand, and his face went quite still.

"What do the dwarves pay for these?" he said.

Dagny asked the nearest salvager, who was mending a net on the next crate. The salvager laughed and said something.

"Almost nothing," said Dagny. "He says the dwarves take them with the iron, by the boatload, for next to nothing, and smelt them for the iron, and the Rim's glad to be rid of them. He says the paths are full of them."

Emeric stood there with the stone in his hand.

Bram knew that look. He'd seen it over the mother lathe, and over the first dynamo, and on the morning in the Works yard when Emeric had seen what a ten-foot length of good steel pipe would do to a boiler. It was the look of a man who'd just seen a great deal more than he was going to say.

"Wait," said Emeric.

"Wait for what?"

"Just wait. Don't say anything to anybody. Not yet." He put the stone in his coat pocket. "I want to think."

He walked off down the quay toward the gate. Bram watched him go. Then he picked up another of the black lumps off the heap and scratched it, and looked at the sheen under the crust, and put it in his own pocket, and went to see about the rifle.



The rifle was on hull four's bench in the workshop under her afterdeck, where Bram had put it a week ago, the night the court gave it back.

He'd stripped it that night. Every part, laid out on a clean cloth in the order it came out, the way the Works taught: the bolt, its springs, the extractor, the pins, the trigger group, the barrel off its receiver. Salt in every joint. Salt crusted in the bolt's lugs and the spring's coils and down the bore, and under the salt the first brown bloom of rust in the places the house's smith hadn't oiled because he hadn't known where to oil it. Bram had soaked every part in fresh water for a day and a night, changing it every watch, and then in oil, and then gone over every surface with a stiff brush and a glass, and found the fresh tool marks on the pins again and drawn them, all three, for Ferra's book.

Now he put it back together. It went together the way it was meant to. He worked the bolt and it ran smooth and locked; he dry-fired it on an empty chamber and the striker fell clean; he looked down the bore at the lamp and the pitting was there, faint, a frosting near the muzzle that he'd tell the armourer about at home, but nothing that would throw a round.

He signed it back into service in hull four's book in his own hand.

The eight rounds in its box he didn't. He'd laid them out on the cloth beside it and looked at every one with the glass, and every one had salt under the lip of the case where the bullet sat, and a week in the sea and a month in the house's hands since. A round that had been wet might go and might not. A round that might not, in a rifle that could kill a dragon, was worse than no round. He put them back in their box and wrote across the lid in grease pencil, CONDEMNED, and took the box across to the K Discovery for Ferra to write off.



The Rim's smiths came to find them on the Saturday afternoon to ask about coal.

Four of them, together, in their leather aprons, while Bram and Dagny were at the pilots' hall reading the published figures on the board for the third time because Dagny wanted to copy them out properly. The oldest of them did the talking. Bram had seen him at his forge on the spine in the second week, a broad bald merman with forearms like hawsers and burn scars up both of them.

The house's iron trade was the crown's now, he said. The house had held it all their lives: iron in from the dwarves, by the boatload, and the Rim's smiths buying it at the house's price and working it, and never smelting their own, because there was nothing to smelt with. The dwarves sold iron out and never sold coal. It was their rule. Everyone knew it. And the Rim had the black stones on every path, which the smiths knew had iron in them because the dwarves took them for it; and now the house was gone and the crown brokered the trade, and the smiths had been thinking.

The land had coal. Everyone had seen the land's ships burn it.

Would the land sell them coal?

Emeric was there. He'd come up from the stages for the figures. Bram watched him hear it, and watched him not answer straight away, and knew why before he did answer: the stone in his coat pocket.

"Not yet," said Emeric, through Dagny. "I'll tell you why. The dwarves don't sell coal, and I don't know why they don't. There's always a reason for a rule like that, and I don't know what theirs is, and I haven't met them. If I sell you coal and I'm wrong about why they keep it from you, I've done something to you and to them that I can't take back." He stopped. "I won't sell what I don't understand. Ask me again when I've been to their coast."

The old smith heard it out. He didn't like it. He said so, through Dagny, with his colour gone a dull red at the throat. Then he said that it was a better answer than the house had ever given them, and went.



Neris came to the pilots' hall later that afternoon to see the figures, and Tolliver showed her the drawing.

It was Tolliver's. He was a deckhand off the K Amara who'd come in on the delivery boats for the last week of the stay by the watch's leave, and he'd been drawing in his book all the way out since April, as all of them had, because Emeric had given every one of them a book on the quay at home and told them to write and draw what they saw. He'd drawn Neris. Bram didn't know when. He'd drawn her at her cellar door with her arms grey to the elbow and her sleeves rolled, from across the quay, in pencil, very well; and he'd coloured it with the coloured pencils from the tin in his kit, and he'd got her colour wrong. A plain green, where hers was a green with a grey in it and a warmer thing at the throat when she was working.

He held it out to her, a bit red in the face, and Dagny explained what it was.

Neris took it from him and held it to the light.

Then she held out her hand for the tin of pencils. Tolliver gave it to her. She sat down on the hall's step with his book on her knee and went through the tin, pencil by pencil, holding each one to her own wrist; and she chose three, and went over the drawing's throat and arms with them, carefully, layering, the way Bram had seen her lay a glaze; and when she'd finished she held the book out at arm's length and looked at it and at her own wrist, and nodded.

Then she asked what happened to the page.

Dagny asked Tolliver. Tolliver said he didn't know. It was his book. He supposed it went home with him.

Neris thought about that. Then she said, through Dagny, that it was her face. That he could keep the page, and take it home, and show it to whoever he liked, because he'd drawn it well and she'd made it right. But that if anyone ever put it up on a wall for strangers to look at, they'd write her name under it, and that she'd made the colour, and they'd ask her first. And if she said no, it came down.

Tolliver said yes. He put it on the back of the page, under the drawing, in Kaldo, there and then, and Neris watched him do it, and had Dagny read it back to her.

Emeric had come along the hall's front while she was colouring it. He stood behind Tolliver and read what Tolliver had written on the back, twice, and Bram saw him take his own notebook out and copy it, word for word, the whole of Neris's terms, and put the notebook away without saying anything about it.



Imani found Emeric on the hard rooms' step that night with a black stone in his hand, and sat down beside him, and asked him what it was.

She'd been watching him since the afternoon. He'd come back from the bench quiet and stayed quiet through supper, and turned the thing over and over in his fingers under the table, and Ferra had looked at it twice and not asked. Imani asked.

He told her. Not all of it at once; he never told her all of anything at once when he hadn't finished thinking it. A thing off the deep floor that the Rim used for gravel. Layered inside, round a seed, like a pearl. He'd scratched one and seen the sheen. He thought, from what he remembered of his own world, which wasn't much on this, that things like it on the floor of the sea there held iron, and manganese, and nickel, and copper and cobalt, a little of each. Nickel, mostly, was the one he thought about. Nickel in steel made a steel that didn't rust in salt and held its strength hot. Nickel was half of what he'd need for an engine he hadn't built yet and wanted to.

"And the dwarves buy them for iron," he said. "For almost nothing. And the Rim's paths are full of them."

"And you said nothing."

"I said wait. To Bram."

"You said nothing," said Imani, "to anybody whose stones they are." She turned on the step to look at him. "You stood on their quay and saw a thing their water made and worked out what it was worth, and put it in your pocket, and came home and thought about it. Alone. All afternoon." She let that sit. "Item one."

He looked at her.

"Start with the people whose water we put that stone in," said Imani. "You remember. On the floor of the bell. Ferra wrote it at the top of the board, above everything." She held out her hand, and after a moment he put the black stone in it. It was heavy. Heavier than it looked. "This is their water too. Their floor. Their paths. And here's the king of the land with one in his coat, deciding what to do about it before he's told them it might be worth anything."

"I don't know that it is. A scratch isn't..."

"Then tell her that." Imani gave it back to him. "Tell her you don't know. Tell her what you think and that you don't know. Tell her before the fleet sails, not from a thousand miles off in a letter when you've done the sums at home." She stood up. "You don't get to keep it in your pocket because it's interesting."

He sat on the step with the stone in his hand.

"No," he said. "I don't."



Neris signed on the K Endeavor's deck on the Sunday at noon, in her own name, and Ferra read her terms aloud first, so that nobody could ever say afterwards they hadn't been heard.

Imani stood at the rail with the rest of them to hear it. The whole of the K Endeavor's watch on deck, and Fletcher, and two of the watch in their dark coats with their slates because a berth in a land ship was a thing the watch recorded, and Neris in the middle of the deck in her best coat, which was the one without ash on it.

Ferra read them in Kaldo, and Dagny put each one into the Rim's tongue after her, standing beside her. That Neris's beads were hers. That nobody in the fleet or in Kaldo would copy them or the way she made them without her leave, and if she gave it, she'd have her share of whatever came of it, in writing. That she could leave the fleet at any port, on any day, and be put ashore with her things and her pay to that day. That she'd write to Tess at home by the relay, as often as Ferra's rationing of the relay allowed, and Tess would write back.

Ferra read the last one, and looked up.

"Is that all of them?" she said, and Dagny asked.

Neris thought. Then she said one more thing, and Dagny put it over.

"She says the bunk's to be on the side away from the boiler."

Ferra added it. Neris signed, in the Rim's script, in her own name, on the K Endeavor's book on the capstan head, and the watch officer cut it on his slate, and that was that.

That evening, at the K Discovery's radio cabin, there was a slip for her. The first. Off the relay from home, in Tess's square hand as taken down by Ambrose's operator, held for her by Sorrel's: Neris. Bram says you make clay do what I can't make steel do. Tell me how. T.

Dagny read it to her on the quay. Neris listened, and asked for it twice more, and then took the slip and put it in her coat, and didn't say what she'd answer.



The trade was done on the Monday, at the queen's trade hall on the Rim, and Imani spent most of it saying no.

Not to the trade. To the crates. Ferra did the buying, with Dagny and the crown's buyers, at the long table in the trade hall's front, and paid in Kaldo iron by weight, bar by bar, with the crown's scale beside her own; and every crate that was bought came past Imani at the back door on its way to the boats, and she looked in every one.

The cane first. The Rim's sugar cane, a strain of it they grew in the warm terraces by the seam, that the plant notes Emeric had given Ferra at home said was better than anything Randillia had: thick, sweet, short-jointed, quick. Bundles of cuttings, wrapped in wet weed. Imani went through them stalk by stalk with her glass, and found two with something white at the joints, and took those two out and had them burned, and let the rest through, and put it on her board.

Then the pearl meal. Ground shell, from the pearl beds off the outer reef, that the Rim fed its terraces with. She wouldn't have it aboard as it came. Shell meal had things living in it; it always did. She had it heated in the furnace yard's drying kiln on iron trays, the whole lot, until it was too hot to touch, a full bell, and then stowed in sealed tins, and every tin marked with the day, and only then would she let it go to the boats.

"It's ground shell," said Ferra, at the table, when the kiln's man came to say it would be a full bell.

"It's ground shell that's been on the sea floor," said Imani. "It goes in tins or it goes in the sea."

Ferra didn't argue.

The sea silk. Abloh's. He'd seen it in the second week on a stall on the spine and stood in front of the stall for a quarter of an hour without a word and then gone and found Ferra. A cloth the Rim wove from the fine threads a kind of shell spun to hold itself to the rocks, gold-brown, very fine, very light, that shone when it moved like a cat's coat. Six bolts of it. Abloh came to the trade hall himself to take them, and stood at the back door while Imani looked them over, and when she'd finished and nodded he took the first bolt in his arms and stood there holding it, and didn't move, and Imani understood after a moment that he was waiting for her to go so that he could put his face in it.

She went.

The ash cement. Sacks of it, grey and gritty, the ground ash from the caldera's old falls that the Rim mixed with lime for its sea walls and that set under the sea and got harder every year it was there. She looked in three sacks and found nothing living, because there was nothing in it to live, and let them all go.

There was one thing Ferra asked for that she didn't get.

A seed. A grove seed, from the old grove's beds, the kind the sea groves grew from, that Emeric had wanted since the first week and had told her to ask for properly, through the crown, at the end, when everything else was signed. She asked. The crown's buyer sent to the Keepers. Lirrae came up from the third bell herself, in her grey, with her slate on its cord, and stood at the end of the trade hall's long table and said no.

Not rudely. She gave the reason, and Dagny put it over. The Keepers didn't give a grove's seed to anyone the season that person's stone had crossed their markers. It wasn't a law. It was the Keepers', and older than the court.

Ferra sent to the queen. She thought, afterwards, that she'd known what the answer would be before the runner was out of the door.

It came back on a slate in the queen's own hand, in Kaldo. The Keepers say no. I don't overrule the Keepers. Ask me when you come back.

Ferra copied it into the book exactly, and let it be.

At the end of the day, when the hall was nearly empty and Ferra was squaring her book, Taurre came in from the watch house with his slate.

He gave Ferra a sheet. She read it at the table. Imani read it over her shoulder.

It was the watch's finding. On the land's crossing of the markers, and on the boy Nimo and his ribs: what the watch had found, in its own words, under its own seal, adverse, every line of it. And at the top, in Taurre's officer's careful Kaldo, a note: that the watch understood the land kept a book of what it gave and took at each place it came to, and asked that this be written in it. As the host's entry. In the host's words. Without the land's certifying it.

Ferra read it twice.

Then she opened her book to the gift ledger's page for Aumere, the one with the board-building tools and the pressure studies on it, and copied the watch's finding into it, under them, every word, without changing one; and at the head of it she wrote Host's entry, the caldera watch, Mon 29 Jun, and nothing else.

Taurre watched her do it.

"First," said Ferra, to him, and the officer put it over. "First host's entry in the book."

Taurre's colour moved at the throat. Imani had never seen it move before. It went a very slightly darker grey, and came back.



The iron contract was signed that night in a hard room under the Rim, at the queen's table, and when it was signed, Emeric asked her for a road.

Imani was there because Ilenne had asked for her. Not for anything; the queen had said, through Ferra, that she'd like the land healer at the table, and Ferra had said that meant Ilenne wanted someone in the room who'd tell her if the land king was being clever, and Imani had come.

The contract was the crown's first with the land. Kaldo iron, the Works' bar and plate, by the boatload, at a price Ferra and the crown's buyer had argued over all afternoon and both disliked, which Ferra said meant it was right. And a quay, on the Rim's outer face, for the iron boats to come to. The queen signed it in her own hand, and Emeric in his, and Ferra and Taurre as witnesses; and then the queen made to rise, and Emeric said, "One more thing."

She sat down again.

He explained it to her. Imani watched him do it and thought she'd never seen him explain anything harder.

A machine that flew. Not on stones, like the hulls; on wings, like a bird, a long stiff wing that the air held up if the machine went fast enough. Kaldo didn't have one yet. It would. And a machine like that couldn't come down on the sea or a hillside or a dome roof; it needed a road. A long, flat, hard road, straight, with nothing at either end of it, that it could run along on wheels until it was fast enough to lift, and come down on, and run along again until it stopped. He drew it on the back of the contract's cover, with Ferra's pencil: a line, long, and a little shape at one end of it.

He wanted to build one here. On the ring.

Ilenne looked at the line on the paper.

Imani watched her get it. She got it faster than Imani had, the first time he'd drawn one for her. She looked at the line and her colour moved, a quick bright flicker at the throat; and she said something in her own tongue, and then in Kaldo.

"A fetch line," said Ilenne. "In the air."

Emeric stopped.

"A fetch line," she said again. "Where ships come in. Wait. Get a pilot. Go in, one at a time, by the line." She put her finger on the long line he'd drawn. "In the air. The same."

"Yes," said Emeric. "That's exactly what it is."

She didn't say yes at once. She sat back and thought about it, and Imani watched her think, and saw her come to her reasons one at a time, and saw that they were hers. The iron needed a quay anyway. So the land would be on the Rim's outer face whatever she decided about any road. The dry people's flying machines would come, sooner or later, whether she built a road for them or not; and whoever owned the ground they came down on would say who came down on it, and how, and when. And Aumere sat between Randillia and everything past it. If the machines came, they'd come here first. The crossroads would be hers.

"The domes," she said. "On Sunday, no. Now, yes. You ask why."

"I wasn't going to."

"I tell you anyway." She looked at Bram, who was at the end of the table and had gone a little red. "The domes, your people build. Your people keep. Your seals. Your pumps. When your ship goes, who fixes them?" She let her colour come up, warm, briefly, at Bram. "The road, my people build. My people keep. Under my law. And if I want, I close it."

Bram nodded, slowly, at the table.

And then she said the other thing, and her colour went out of her to say it.

The last time dry people had ruled the air over the sea, she said, they'd made dead zones in it. Their ships had. And where those zones came down on the water, her people's floating cities had gone into the sea, and the people in them. It was on the stones. Every child at court learned it.

She said it once, flat, without any colour at all. Nobody at the table answered it. Imani didn't think there was an answer, and from Emeric's face, neither did he.

He said the catch himself, before she could find it.

"You should know this before you say yes," he said. "There won't be regular flights past the wall. Not for a long time. There's a law about the air past the wall, the dragons', the Leaving Terms, and the Rim's house laid a charge against us under it in the dry hall, and nobody can answer that charge here, and until it's answered I won't fly Kaldo's machines past the wall as a matter of course." He put his hand flat on the drawing. "You'd be building a road for a day that hasn't come. I can't tell you when it will."

Ilenne was quiet across the table.

"You tell me the bad part," she said. "Before."

"Yes."

"Good," said Ilenne, and turned to Ferra. "Write."

Ferra wrote. Imani watched her do it, and watched the terms come out of Ilenne one at a time, flat and plain and every one of them a fence: the ground stayed Aumere's, under Aumere's law; Kaldo leased it and didn't own it; every landing by the crown's permit and no other way; the watch kept it; a Rim crew maintained it, trained by Kaldo and paid by Kaldo; nothing armed stored on it, ever; and the queen could close it on any day she chose, and it stayed closed until she opened it.

The build, then. Bram did most of that, with Dagny, and the crown's builder, and a map of the ring on the table between them. The ring's outer reef flat on the north-east side, where the trade wind came in steady: a sea wall of the ash cement first, because ash cement set in seawater and got stronger there, and behind it the flat filled with the caldera's own lava rock and black sand, rammed, and paved over with more ash cement. Long. The line he'd drawn wanted to be about three kilometres, said Bram, the whole of it; but the first one and a half would do for a start, and could be built while the fleet was away, by the Rim's crafters, under the crown, on his drawings, paid in Kaldo iron and coin. And beside it the iron quay, and the Rim's ground relay for the sets, so that a machine coming in would have somebody to talk to.

Emeric leaned over and said something to Imani under his breath while Bram and the builder were arguing about the rammer.

"Where I come from," he said, "they built them on coral. In the middle of the sea. Out of nothing but what was there."

"Did they work?"

"Better than the ones on land, some of them."

At the far end of the table, a deep lord who'd come in with the queen's council to witness the iron contract and had sat through the whole of the rest of it without moving let his colour come up, slowly, in front of all of them: a dull hard slate, from the throat down, and kept his mouth shut. He didn't need to open it. The land had ground on the ring now. Every house in the court would know it by morning.

Ilenne saw it. She didn't look at him.

When it was done and signed she stood up, and they all stood up; and at the door she turned round.

"What I want for it," she said, in Kaldo, to Emeric, "ask me when you come back."

Ferra wrote it down exactly, word for word, before the door had shut.

And Emeric, at the door, didn't let her go.

"There's one other thing," he said. "I should have told you on Saturday, and I didn't. I'm telling you now."

He took the black stone out of his coat and put it on the table where the contract had been.

Imani watched Ilenne look at it, and then at him, and then, for a moment, at her.

He told her. All of it, and the parts he didn't know. That the Rim used these for gravel and the dwarves bought them for iron. That he'd scratched one, and that a scratch could suggest a great deal and prove nothing; that he thought there might be other metals in them than iron, worth more than iron, and that he didn't know, and nobody could know without an assay, done properly, in a furnace, by someone who knew how. That he'd like her leave to take three home and have them tested. Three. And that whatever the assay said, the trade in them was the dwarves' business and the Rim's, and he wouldn't set a price on them or go round the dwarves to buy them until Kaldo had been to the dwarves' coast and met the people who bought them now.

Ilenne picked the stone up. She weighed it in her hand.

"You find this Saturday," she said.

"Saturday."

"You tell me Monday."

"Yes." He didn't look at Imani. "I was told I should have told you on Saturday night."

Ilenne turned her head and looked at Imani, and something came up at her throat that Imani didn't know, and went.

"Take three," said Ilenne. "Tell me what is in them. And the dwarves, first. You meet them first." She put the stone down. "I agree."



He made the boards on the Tuesday, on the Rim's quay, and Imani sat on a crate with Ferra and watched him do it, because there was nothing else for either of them to do and she wanted to see what it would cost.

A plank of the Rim's pale light pine across two trestles, a long one, as long as he was tall and a half again. Children all round, a ring of them, three deep, on their legs and in the water off the quay's end, with their chins on the edge. Zale on a bollard with his side strapped, because Imani had let him come and sit if he didn't stand.

Emeric took his coat off and rolled his sleeves to the shoulder. Then he looked at Imani.

"Session," he said.

"Go on."

He held his hands out over the plank and they changed.

Both of them, to the forearm, on purpose this time: the near-black scale coming up out of the skin from the fingertips to just below the elbow and stopping there, where he'd told it to, and the claws coming down long and hooked and the colour of old horn. The children went silent all at once. Then one of them said something, and they all started talking, and Zale said something short to them in their own tongue and they stopped.

He put the claws to the plank and drew them along it.

The wood came up in curls. Long, clean, pale curls, like the shavings off a plane in a good shop, a yard long, falling off the plank's edge onto the quay round the trestles' feet; and the plank under them went down, a little at a time, into a shape. A long shallow shape, rounded at the nose, flatter at the tail. He worked it from end to end and back, slowly, with his head bent over it, and every so often he stopped and his eyes went gold, the whole of them, iris and white, for a breath at a time, and he looked along the grain from the plank's end as if he could see into it; and then they went back, and he drew the claws along the next line.

"A cleaver," said Imani, beside Ferra, "doing a scalpel's job."

"Use only what the job needs," said Ferra. "He says. In his own words."

"Exposure therapy," said Emeric, without looking up.

Imani looked at him.

"That isn't what it means," she said.

"It's exactly what it means. You do the thing that frightens you, a little at a time, until it doesn't." He drew a long curl off the rail. "It came on its own at breakfast and I didn't like it. So I'm calling it on purpose, over and over, for something that doesn't matter, until it comes when I ask and goes when I tell it."

She opened her mouth.

She closed it. She didn't argue, because it was.

A child in the water off the quay's end, a small girl with her chin on the planks, said something to Zale, quite loudly, pointing at the claws. Zale answered her. The girl looked at Emeric again and said something else, scornful, and the children round her laughed.

"What did she say?" said Ferra.

"She asked whether all the dry king's people can do that," said Zale. "I told her no. Just the one. And that he's showing off."

"What did she say after?"

"That she could tell."

The sun got bad at about eleven. It came round the end of the spine and down onto the quay, white, hard, the kind that burned in a quarter of an hour, and Imani felt it on her neck and saw Ferra's freckles start to come up on the backs of her hands; and then the light went off them, both of them, all at once, and it was shade.

Imani didn't look up straight away. She thought it was a cloud.

It was a wing. One of his. It had come up out of his back through his shirt, which had a split in it now, and opened out to the side over the crate, black and leathery, scaled along the bones, as tall as he was; and he'd angled it to keep the sun off the two of them, and gone on working the plank with his other hand without looking round.

Ferra noticed it only when he folded it.

She'd been watching the plank. The sun came back onto her hands all at once and she looked up, and there was the wing going back, folding in on itself along the bones and down into his back, into the split shirt, gone; and she stared at the split in his shirt, and then at Imani.

"Was that there the whole time?"

"Half an hour."

Ferra looked at the back of his shirt.

"I'll mend that," she said, in a voice Imani knew, the voice that meant she'd been looked after without being asked and didn't know where to put it.

It cost him. Imani had been waiting to see where, and the wing was where. The hands he'd held for an hour and more and they'd cost him something steady, a hunger he'd mentioned twice; the wing, in half an hour, took more than the hands had all morning. She saw it go out of him. He finished the first board and stood back from it, and swayed, a little, and put his hand on the trestle.

Then he came and sat down on the crate between them, heavily, without asking, and let his scaled hands hang between his knees.

"You do the next one," he said. "Hold it. I'll tell you where."

So they held the second plank for him, Ferra at the nose and Imani at the tail, while he sat between them on the crate and worked it with his hands; and Imani wrote on her board, in the gap between one curl and the next: Both hands, forearm, an hour and a half; steady. One wing, half an hour: more than the hands together. Sat down before the second board.

He smoothed both boards with a piece of sharkskin a cutter on the quay sold him, and rubbed them with oil from the K Discovery's stores, and stood them up against the trestles in the sun, and the children came and touched them.



He did it properly the next day, at the reef station, with a spokeshave and a block plane and no claws at all, and said so.

The reef apprentices had asked him to. Six of them, the station's boys and girls, the oldest not yet grown, who'd been out on the reef with Lirrae's divers and had come to the quay to watch on the Tuesday and gone home and thought about it. They wanted boards. They wanted to make them. So on the Wednesday morning he took the tools off hull four, the spokeshave and two block planes and a rasp, and went out to the reef station with three planks on a cart, and showed them, on the station's work floor, how a man made a board with his own two hands and the tools for it.

The apprentices were disappointed. Imani could see it. They'd wanted the claws. They stood round the planks with the land tools in their hands, scowling, while he showed them how to set the plane's iron and which way to take the spokeshave along the grain; and then one of them, a thin quick girl with a scar on her chin, got the hang of the spokeshave before the others and went along the rail of her plank in one long sweep, and the curl came off it as clean as anything his claws had done.

He came and looked at her rail. He ran his hand along it.

"That's better than mine," he said. "Look. Mine's got a hollow there, where I leaned. Yours hasn't." He showed the others. "Do it like hers."

The girl went a colour Imani didn't know, at the throat, and looked very hard at her plank.

At noon a woman came along the station's walk with a pot. A cutter's wife, she said through the station's officer, from the shallows, who'd heard on the stair that the land king was making boards for the sea and wanted to know what he meant to coat them with. He showed her: the oil, rubbed in. She looked at it and laughed, not unkindly, and opened her pot.

It was holdfast. The stuff the Rim brushed on its boats' planking and its pier piles, thick and brown and smelling of pine and the sea; she thinned it with something from a flask, and brushed a coat on the end of one of the apprentices' boards, and he watched it go in, and touched it when it had dried, and bent close to look at it.

"That's better than what I had at home," he said. "Better than anything I had. It goes into the grain." He looked up at her. "Can I buy some?"

Bram, who'd come out with the cart, had already asked her for a barrel.

The tools stayed. Emeric gave them to the apprentices before they left, the spokeshave and the planes and the rasp, all of them, in front of Lirrae and the station's officer; and Ferra, when they got back, wrote them in the gift ledger at their value, with the board-building, under the host's entry, and the date.



Ressar came up to the Rim on the last morning, which he never did.

Imani saw him come. She was on the hard rooms' step at eight with her tea and her board, and the old Keeper came up out of the stage head behind a pilot, slowly, on his legs, blinking in the daylight, with something rolled under his arm in oilcloth; and he stood on the quay and looked round him at the Rim's streets in the sun as if he hadn't seen them in years, which, Ilenne said afterwards, he hadn't.

He'd come for Armand.

Armand had been at the Keepers' bell every sitting day since the first, on time, on Ressar's terms, and come back blue with cold every evening and said he'd got it wrong four times, then three, then once. He came out of the hard rooms when Imani sent for him, in his coat, and stood in front of the old Keeper on the quay, and Ressar unrolled the oilcloth.

It was a rubbing. Charcoal on a long sheet of the Rim's fine weed paper, taken off a stone: the long old stone in the Keepers' bell that Armand had read aloud on the first day as the First City's record, and that Ressar had told him in front of everyone was a burial song.

Ressar said what it was for. Imani got it through the pilot, who had some Kaldo, and through Armand's face, which had more.

The land Keeper could have it. For the land's collection, the one Ressar had heard the land king meant to build at home, to keep what the voyage brought back. On these terms. It went into the collection as a song. Not a record. It was labelled in the words Ressar would give now, with his own name under them as the Keeper who'd allowed it. And it was never, by anyone, in any hall, to be read aloud as a record of anything.

Armand took out his notebook. He wrote the label as Ressar dictated it, word by word, in the Rim's script and then in Kaldo, and read it back to him in the Rim's tongue, slowly, getting it right, and Ressar listened to every word and corrected one and nodded.

Then the old Keeper rolled the rubbing up again in its oilcloth and gave it into Armand's hands, and went back down the stages without another word.

Ferra came out onto the step and Armand showed it to her. She opened the gift ledger to Aumere's page and entered it under the tools. A rubbing of the burial song, from the Keepers' bell; given by Ressar, Keeper of the Stones, on his terms (label as dictated, never to be read as a record). And in the value column, where she'd put a figure for everything else in the book, she wrote nothing at all, because Ressar wouldn't have one put on it.

The watch's last permits came up the same morning, on a slate, by a runner. The surf, on the ring's outer face, inside the markers, by permit, with a watch skiff and a pilot standing by, tomorrow. The feast on the black-sand cove by the reef station, tomorrow night. And the departure, the day after, out through the fetch line by the pilots' lane, at dawn.



Bram went to the glassmakers' yard on the last morning too, before the fleet's work began, because the young glassmaker had sent for him.

Imani went with him as far as the arch. She hadn't been asked; she'd been passing. She stopped under the arch where the young man had stood a month ago and turned his back on her and Ferra in the street, and she stayed there, outside, in the shade, where he'd see her if he looked up and wouldn't have to if he didn't.

He'd said no to the berth. Bram had offered it on the Monday, through Dagny, properly, the same terms as Neris's; a place on the K Endeavor, his craft his own, leave at any port. The young man had listened to all of it at the door of his yard, and said no, and shut the door.

Now he'd sent for Bram, at dawn, and here Bram was.

The young man said nothing at all. He took Bram by the sleeve and put him at the end of his bench, where he could see, and went to the furnace. He took a gather of the green glass on his iron. He rolled it, and blew it, and swung it, and laid it out flat on the iron table in a long pane, as thick as a thumb, and while it was still glowing he did something to it with a stone in his bare hand, a held stone, laid flat on the hot glass and drawn along it, slowly, end to end and back; and the pane changed. Imani saw it from the arch. A grain came into it, fine, like sand caught in ice, and the green went deeper. And then he took a second stone and did something else, at the edges, that she couldn't follow at all.

It took most of an hour. He didn't explain any of it. He didn't say a word to Bram the whole time, or look at him, or look at the arch. He worked the pane from the furnace to the table to the annealing shelf, start to finish, the whole of it, with Bram at the end of his bench watching every movement of his hands, and when it was on the shelf to cool he turned round and wiped his hands on his apron and looked at Bram.

Bram looked back at him.

Then the young man went into the back of his yard and didn't come out again.

Bram stood at the end of the bench until the pane on the shelf had gone from glowing to green, looking at it. When he came out through the arch, past Imani, his face was doing something she'd only ever seen it do over a machine that had run first time, and he walked straight past her down the street toward the quay, and she let him.

Halfway down the street he stopped, and took his notebook out, and stood in the middle of the Rim's morning traffic with porters going round him on both sides, and started to draw.
