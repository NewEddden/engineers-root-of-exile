Chapter 74 - Lime and the Tide Line

The iron went into the ground and the ground would not hold it still, which was the problem he had not thought hard enough about until the first laid run of waste line shifted a finger's width in the wet clay of the island and threw its own pitch off, and a pipe that will not keep its grade is a pipe that pools, and a pipe that pools is the plague again wearing patience for a coat.

He needed to bed the iron in something that did not move, and he needed to pave the streets he was tearing up anyway, and he needed footings under the housing he had not yet built that would not settle and crack the walls he set on them, and all three of those were the same want, which was a made stone, a stone he could pour into a trench or a form and have it wake up hard in the shape he gave it. He had known the answer since before he owned the barony. He had simply been too poor, and then too busy, and then too consumed by iron to reach for it. Now the iron had created the need for it, which was the way of the whole enterprise, each thing he built calling the next thing into being by the hole it left.

He built it out of what the barony already made. The lime was there. They had been burning quicklime for mortar since the wall went up, driving off the breath of the stone in the kilns until what was left could be slaked with water into the paste that held the wall's facing to its rubble core, and the same quicklime, slaked and then mixed with sand and with the broken aggregate the quarries threw off as waste, was concrete. That was the whole of it. There was no new material and no rare one and no mana in any step. A man who could burn lime for mortar could make concrete, and every man in Kaldo who had built the wall already knew how to burn lime for mortar, which meant he was not teaching a new craft, he was teaching an old craft a new shape, and the difference in effort between those two things was the difference between a season and a decade.

He taught it the way he taught everything, by making the first batch himself in front of the men who would make all the rest, so that it lived in their hands and not only in his instruction. Slake the lime. Cut it with sand, three of sand or thereabouts to one of the paste, more or less as the work wanted, and he made them feel the more or less rather than memorize a number, because a crew that understood why the ratio moved could fix a bad batch and a crew that had only the number could only fail by it. Fold in the aggregate. Wet it to a stiff mud, not a soup, because water was the thing that made it workable and also the thing that made it weak, and the whole skill of it lived in giving it exactly enough water to place and not a drop past. Then form it, and wait, and here was the part that separated the men who would make good concrete from the men who would make rubble, because concrete did not dry, it cured, and the difference was the difference between a thing losing water and a thing changing its nature, and a batch rushed or let go thirsty or caught by a frost before it had set its character would spall and sweat and crumble in a year no matter how right the mix had been.

That was its failure surface, and he made a point of naming it aloud to Bram and to the crew leads and to Ferra for the tallies, because a barony that now ran on iron and glass and lime and magic needed to know that each of those failed in its own separate way, so that a man diagnosing a broken thing knew which broken thing he was looking at. Iron failed by rust and by casting flaw. Glass failed by shock and by the master's absence in the copy. Magic failed by the dead zone and the drained crystal and the shape a pantograph could copy but not comprehend. And concrete failed by curing wrong, by being hurried, by freezing green, by drowning in its own mixing water, and none of those looked like any of the others, and a crew that knew the shape of its own failure was a crew that could catch it before it went into the ground. He would rather they feared the frost than trusted the paste.

The present use he locked hard and told them not to reach past. Roads, the main arteries he was about to cut through a re-platted town. Culverts and drains, the small works that carried surface water off the streets so the streets did not become the very standing pools he had just gone to war against. The bedding under the new iron, poured into the trench beneath the pipe to give the pitch a stone spine that the wet island clay could not shrug out of true. Foundations and footings, the flat hard pads he would set the new housing on so a boom town's weight did not settle its own walls into cracks. All of it dry. All of it above the water, or draining water off, or holding water in a pipe, but none of it living in the water, and that distinction was the whole discipline of the material and the exact place a clever man would get people killed by forgetting it.

Because it would not set underwater. He stood at the harbor end of the island on a grey afternoon and felt the full weight of that limit land on the one part of the barony that most wanted the material and could least have it. He was a coastal lord. He had ocean on one hand and lake on the other and a river splitting to hold his town in its arms, and he had a shipyard that had just floated a metal hull, and every one of those was a reason to want to pour a stone quay, a proper harbor wall, piers that did not rot, foundations that reached below the tide line into the mud and set there hard as bedrock the way he had read that the old empire of his lost world had set its harbors, moles a mile long standing two thousand years in the salt. And he could not do it. Lime concrete was non-hydraulic. It needed air to cure and it would not cure in water and it was weak in the constant wet, and a quay poured of it would slump and soften and give back to the sea inside a few winters, and so the one application a coastal engineer dreamed of first was the one the material forbade, and there was no cleverness that got him around it, because the limit was not a gap in his knowledge, it was a fact of the chemistry, and chemistry did not care how badly he wanted the harbor.

He knew the answer. That was the particular frustration of it, the answer sitting fully formed in his memory and no way to reach it. Hydraulic concrete, the kind that set underwater and only grew harder for it, needed a pozzolan, a volcanic ash cooked by the earth into exactly the reactive stuff that let lime cure without air, and the old world had found it under a mountain and named a whole art after the town they dug it from. He knew what he needed. He knew what it looked like and what it did and why it worked. He simply did not have any, because his three territories were a river-island and a mine and a trade-junction, and not one of them had ever sat under a burning mountain, and a pozzolan was not a thing a man made in a kiln, it was a thing the earth made in a catastrophe and left lying for whoever was lucky enough to be born on top of it.

Rowena was born on top of it. The thought arrived the way the good ones always arrived, not as invention but as connection, two things he had known separately for a long time snapping together into a thing neither had been alone. Emberfell was volcanic. Rowena ruled a country of black rock and heat and old fire, the most respected knight alive sitting on a land nobody had ever valued for anything but its danger, and under that danger, in the ash of the mountain that made the place a byword for hardship, was the exact material that would let him pour a harbor that outlived his grandchildren. No one in this world had ever connected volcanic ash to a stone that set in the sea, because no one in this world had a reason to, because no one in this world had stood in the ruin of an empire whose harbors were still standing after two thousand years and asked how. He had. He was the connection. And he could do nothing with it, not yet, because Emberfell was not on his board, Rowena was an alliance he had not yet made and a thread he had not yet pulled, and a barony did not get volcanic ash by wanting it any more than it got a harbor by wanting one.

So he banked it. He wrote it down for Ferra in the ledger of things the barony would need before it could have them, filed under the same discipline that had kept him from putting a crystal under the town's water, the discipline of not solving a problem before he had honestly earned the means to, and he made himself leave the harbor unpoured. The town would get its streets and its footings and its bedded pipe, all of it dry, all of it inside the limit, and the harbor would get timber and stone stacked the old way for now, patched and maintained and honestly provisional, a placeholder that announced its own impermanence, until the day Emberfell's ash came up a road he had not yet built to a port he could not yet properly make, and he could pour the thing he already knew how to pour the moment he had the one ingredient his own land would never give him.

He turned back from the water toward the town, where the first arteries were about to be cut, and let the harbor go. It was the hardest kind of restraint, the restraint of a man who could see the finished thing entire and had to walk away from it because wanting was not having and knowing was not making, and it was exactly the restraint that kept the whole enterprise honest. A lord who poured a harbor of the wrong stone because he could not bear to wait would have a harbor for three winters and a ruin after, and a town would learn to trust a thing that failed, which was worse than never having built it. He would wait for Emberfell. He would pour dry until then. And the streets, at least, the streets he could have now, clean and hard and straight as the island would let them run, which was the next thing, and the thing the town would actually see.
