Book 2, Chapter 36: The Salt Line

Ferra's shoulders were pink from the reef station, where there'd been no wing to sit under, and at seven on the Thursday morning Imani sat her down on the end of the bunk in the K Discovery's cabin and painted them white.

"It's zinc," said Imani. "Zinc oxide, in grease. It sits on top. The sun hits it and not you."

"I know what it is."

"Then hold still."

It was cold out of the pot and thick as butter. Imani laid it on with two fingers across the tops of both shoulders, where the pink was worst, and down the backs of the arms, and Ferra sat there and let her, because Imani was right and the sun on the ring's outer face would be worse than anything on the reef station's walk. Then Imani dipped her fingers again and reached for Ferra's face.

"No."

"Your nose is already going."

"My nose is fine."

"Your nose," said Imani, "is the colour of a radish," and painted it, a white stripe straight down it from between the eyebrows to the tip, and then one across each cheekbone, before Ferra could get her hands up.

Ferra looked at herself in the shaving glass on the bulkhead. A woman with red hair and freckles and three white stripes on her face like a clown at a fair, glaring.

"I look ridiculous."

"You look like you won't burn," said Imani, and put the lid on the pot.



The Rim thought it was the funniest thing it had ever seen.

Ferra had known it would. She'd watched the merfolk on the quay all week in the hard sun at noon, going a shade darker at will, the whole of them at once, and coming back pale again in the shade of the kilns; and she'd watched the children of the reef station on the Wednesday watching Emeric go red on the back of his neck while he planed, and saw them work out, slowly, with growing delight, that the land people couldn't. That the dry ones had no colour to change. That they just burned.

And now here was the land king's wife on the ring's outer reef at nine in the morning with stripes painted on her face out of a pot.

The reef apprentices came round her in a ring. The six from the station, and their friends, and their friends' friends; and they looked at her nose, and at each other, and the scarred girl with the good rail said something, and they all went off into the bright clicking hiss that the Rim's children made when they laughed, and couldn't stop.

Then one of them asked her something. A boy, solemn, pointing at her face. Dagny was standing by with her own nose painted and put it over.

"He wants to know if you can do another colour."

"Tell him no."

"He says not from the pot. He says on you. By yourself."

Ferra looked at the boy.

"I can do one," she said.

And she did it, because they were all looking at her and she could feel it start before she'd decided to, from the collar of her shirt up: hot, all at once, right up to the roots of her hair, so that the white stripes stood out on her face. The children shrieked. The boy who'd asked clapped both hands over his mouth and fell over backwards onto the reef rock, and lay there with his feet in the air and his colour going through every shade of green he had.

"Red," said Dagny, unnecessarily, and put it on her slate.

Arthur's lot had stripes too, by the time the watch's skiff came round the point. Arthur had come down the beach path with a stripe on each cheek and one on his chin, very neat, the way he did everything, and Bren had done his own and got it in his eye and was swearing about it, and Mira had a stripe across her nose and a stripe down each side of her jaw like a war mask; and Maeve, beside her, had the same, exactly the same, stripe for stripe, and the two of them stood side by side on the reef in their swimming things with their arms folded and their faces painted alike and dared anybody to say anything.

Nobody said anything. Ferra looked at the two of them, and then at Imani, who'd seen it too, and neither of them said anything either.

Imani found Emeric last. He'd been down at the water's edge with the boards, waxing the rails with a cake of something Bram had made, and he came up the reef with a board under each arm and a long welt of pink across the bridge of his nose from Tuesday, and Imani stood in front of him with the pot.

"You too."

"I'm fine."

"For fairness," said Imani, and painted his nose.



The break was on the outer face of the ring, where the open sea came in from the west over a long flat reef and stood up in the shallows and fell. Inside the markers. By the watch's permit, with the watch's skiff lying off the end of the reef with two officers in it and a pilot standing in the stern, all three of them watching the land people closely.

They didn't have to pull Emeric out.

Ferra watched him go out through the white water on his belly, paddling, with the board under him and his locs tied up on top of his head in a knot the size of a loaf. He went through the first wave by pushing the board's nose down under it, and came up the far side, and through the second the same way, and out past the break to where the swells came in smooth and dark and humped; and sat up on the board, astride it, with his back to the reef and his face to the sea, waiting, as if he'd done it all his life.

He had. She knew he had. Not this life.

A swell came. He turned the board round, and lay down, and paddled, two strokes, three; and the swell picked him up, and he came up off his belly onto his feet in one movement and stood, and the wave stood up under him and he came down the face of it, slanting, fast, with his arms out a little and his knees bent, and the white breaking behind his shoulder; all the way along the reef, until it gave out in the shallows and he stepped off it into the water up to his knees.

The reef went silent.

Then the Rim's children started screaming.

Arthur tried it next. He'd been watching with his arms folded and his jaw set, and he went out through the break the way Emeric had, more or less, and sat on his board past the white water, and waited; and when he came back in, on his belly, having fallen off his first two waves standing up, he lay in the shallows with the board on top of him and said to Emeric, who was standing over him, "You're using the stones."

"I'm not."

"You're using something."

"I'm using my feet," said Emeric. "Go again."

Arthur went again, and stood up on the next one, and stayed up for the length of a breath, and fell off it in a way that Ferra would remember for the rest of the voyage, sideways, with his arms out, into the face of the wave; and came up spluttering without his board and with his stripes washed half off, and didn't say anything else about the stones.

Ferra stood up for about two seconds.

She counted them. She'd let him take her out on the second board, lying on it with her chin on the wax and him swimming beside her holding its tail, out past the break, which was the most frightening thing she'd done since the hatch; and then he'd turned her round and held the board and said now, and pushed, and she'd felt the swell take it, and got her knees under her and then her feet, and stood. On the sea. With the reef going by under her and the wave's face shining.

One. Two.

Then she was in it. Under, with the board somewhere and the sea in her nose, rolled over twice on the sand floor in the white water; and then his hand on her arm, pulling her up, and she came up with her hair over her face and the zinc on her nose and salt water running out of her ears, and found she was laughing.

"Two seconds," she said.

"About that."

"Two," said Ferra. "I counted."



Zale stood up on his third wave.

Imani had cleared him for the water that morning, on the K Discovery's deck, with her hand flat on the strapping and her face set: lying on a board, on his belly, in the shallows, and not standing. Not standing. She'd said it twice. He'd said yes both times, and looked at her with great sincerity, and Ferra had known from the look exactly what was going to happen.

He lay on his first wave. He lay on his second. On the third he came up onto his knees, and then onto his feet, and stood, a big man on a small board with his side strapped under his shirt and his gills open under his ears, in the sun, coming in along the reef on a wave that wasn't very big, grinning like a boy; and he rode it all the way in, and stepped off in the shallows, and turned round and looked up the beach at Imani with an expression of total and shameless triumph.

It lasted one ride.

Ilenne had come out on the second set. She'd come out with no board at all, on her tail, the way the Rim's young people rode the reef for a dare, and she'd gone out past where any of them would go, past where the swells came in humped and smooth, to where the ring's outer face dropped away and the big ones came in from the deep water steep and dark and twice the height of the ones the land people were on. Ferra saw her go. Everyone on the reef saw her go, and the watch's skiff came round a little, and the pilot stood up in the stern.

A wave came that was too steep for anything with a board on it. Ferra could see that from the beach. It stood up out of the sea at the reef's edge, green, with the light through it, and its top came over.

Ilenne went down the face of it.

Not on anything. On herself, her arms at her sides and her tail in, straight down the steep green face of it with the sea standing over her, and along it, inside it, under the curl; and the whole of it came down behind her in a long white roar, and she came out of the end of it, out of the white, into the flat water at the reef's edge, about ten yards from where Zale was sitting on his board.

He fell off it. He didn't do anything; he was sitting on it, watching her, with his mouth open, and the board went one way and he went the other.

Imani, on the beach, stood up.

"In," she said, quite loudly, across the water.

Zale came in. He came in slowly, on his belly, holding the board, with the face of a man who had nothing whatever to say for himself and was going to say it anyway; and Ilenne swam in beside him, not touching him, a hand's width off, with her colour doing nothing at all in front of the whole reef, which was watching her, and her eyes on the sky.



They were covered in salt by four.

Not the sea; the sea washed off. The salt it left. It dried on them all afternoon in the sun on the reef rock and the black sand, white and fine, crusted in Ferra's hair and the creases of her elbows and the backs of her knees, in Imani's eyebrows, in a white tidemark round all three of them where their swimming things had been, so that by the time the boat brought them back out to the K Discovery to dress for the feast they looked like three hams that had been cured.

Emeric stood in the middle of the cabin with salt in his locs and looked at the two of them, and Ferra saw the look, and put her hand up flat.

"No," she said.

"I didn't..."

"You were going to." She pointed at him. "We're covered. We're both covered. We've salt where you don't want to know. You're not touching anybody until we've had the water."

"I don't mind salt."

"You don't mind salt in your food," said Imani, from the bunk, where she'd sat down to unlace her shoes. "You've never minded salt in your food. You put it on things that have already got salt on them."

"That's different."

"How," said Imani, "is it different."

He opened his mouth. He took a step toward Ferra, and Ferra put both hands flat on his chest and held him off, at arm's length, and the salt on his chest came off on her palms; and he looked down at her painted nose, two inches from his own painted nose, and Imani started to laugh.

She laughed first. She always did. She laughed sitting on the bunk with one shoe off, helplessly, the laugh she'd laughed at the breakfast table at his dragon's hand, until she had to put her forehead down on her knees; and Ferra felt it start in herself, felt her mouth go, and hit him.

Not hard. On the arm, with the flat of her hand, as she hit him at the long table at home when he was right about something.

And then didn't let go of his arm.

She stood there with her hand round his forearm and the salt between her fingers and his skin, and looked up at him, and stopped laughing.

"Water first," she said.



The K Discovery's washroom was the size of a cupboard. There was a tank on the deckhead, fresh water from the ring's springs that the watch's boats had brought out that morning, with a chain to pull and a rose on the end of a pipe; and a slatted floor of teak over a tray that drained over the side; and that was all. One person fitted. Two if they were friends.

Three got in.

It was a mistake. Ferra knew it was a mistake about the time Imani shut the door behind them, because there was nowhere to stand that wasn't somebody, and the slats were wet and her elbow was in Emeric's ribs and Imani's knee was against the back of hers, and somebody pulled the chain before anybody was ready and the water came down out of the rose cold, cold as the ring's springs, straight onto the top of her head; and she shrieked, and Imani swore, and Emeric made a noise like a man who's sat down on a cat.

And then it was warm. He'd put his hand flat on the tank's side over his head, and she felt the warmth come out of him into the iron, the way the stones did on a winter morning, and the water coming down through the rose went from springs to rain to bath in the space of a breath.

"Cheat," said Imani.

"Do you want it cold?"

"No," said Imani, and put her face up into it.

The salt came off. It came off them in the warm water in long white runs, out of hair and off shoulders and down legs into the slatted floor, and Ferra stood with her eyes shut and the water on her face and felt it go off her; the reef, the sun, the zinc, the sea. She felt somebody's hands in her hair. His. Working the salt out of it slowly, with his fingers, from the roots down, and then the water running through it clean. She leaned back against him without opening her eyes.

Then the hands came down. To the back of her neck, and the tops of her shoulders, where the zinc had been and Imani's water had run it off, and stayed there. Warm. Warmer than the water. Then down.

She opened her eyes.

Imani was in front of her, with her back to the bulkhead and the water running down her, and her hair down, all of it, out of its work tie for the first time since the ring; long and dark and curling all over her shoulders and her breasts and wet through, and she was looking past Ferra at him, over Ferra's shoulder, with the look Ferra had seen on her at the hatch and on the hospital deck and not anywhere else, the look of a woman who has decided something.

"Bunk," said Imani.



They didn't get as far as the bunk. Not at first.

They got as far as the cabin, wet, with the washroom door banging behind them on the roll and the water running off them onto the boards, and he caught Ferra round the waist from behind as she went past him and turned her round and kissed her; and he was warm all over, hot, the way he ran when he pushed the mana in, his whole body like a hearth stone on a cold night, and she put her hands flat on his chest and this time didn't hold him off. She pulled him in. She felt him laugh against her mouth and kissed him harder so that he'd stop, and he didn't stop, and she didn't mind.

Imani came up behind him. Ferra felt her come; felt along the bond the warmth of her go up his back, and saw his eyes close. Imani's arms went round him from behind, round his chest, under Ferra's, and her mouth went to the side of his neck, under his ear, where the locs were heavy and wet, and she bit him. Gently. He made the noise again.

"Bunk," said Imani, against his neck.

"Floor," said Ferra.

"Floor's wet."

"Floor's nearer."

It was the floor. The cabin's boards, with the two dry towels Imani pulled off the hook and threw down and missed with one of; and he went down onto them with the two of them, carefully, the way he did everything with his body now that he knew what it could do, so that Ferra's back came down on the towel and not the boards and Imani came down beside her with her hair everywhere. On something solid. He'd said so once, at home, quite seriously, laying out the reasons with his hands: that you couldn't float for this, that every push sent you the other way. Imani had laughed at him for a week. He'd been right.

His locs fell round Ferra's face when he leaned over her. Long, heavy, down past the middle of his back now, wet, smelling of the fresh water and under it still a little of the sea; they fell down round her on both sides like a curtain, so that there was nothing in the world but his face over hers and the lamp coming through his hair in threads, and she put her hands up into them and held on.

He kissed her throat. Her collarbone, where the zinc had been. The pink line across the tops of her shoulders, slowly, all the way along one and then the other, following a line; and then down. Beside her Imani had his hand. Ferra didn't see what Imani did with it; she felt it, along the bond, Imani's breath catch and go ragged, and his answer to it go through all three of them at once like a struck bell.

It had always been like that, since the wedding. Since before. You felt it twice: once in your own body, and once along the bond, in theirs. Every touch on Imani came to Ferra a breath late, softened, like a sound through a wall. Every touch on her went to both of them. She'd thought, at first, that it would be too much. It was too much. She'd stopped minding.

He was slow. He was always slow, at the start, the way he was slow over a drawing, with all night and meaning to use it; and she lay on the wet towel on the cabin floor with the boards hard under her shoulder blades and his mouth moving down her, and his hands, and Imani's hand in hers, gripping, and let him be slow until she couldn't bear it, and then pulled at his hair until he wasn't.

He came up over her. She took him in with her knees drawn up and her hands in his locs and her breath going out of her all at once, and the cabin went away.

It didn't go far. It went as far as the lamp swinging on its hook over them, and the creak of the K Discovery's frames, and the hiss of the sea along her side, and Imani's voice saying something low against his shoulder that made him laugh and lose the rhythm and find it again, harder; and Ferra's own voice, which she heard from a little way off and didn't recognise and didn't try to stop. She held on. She felt it build in her and in him and along the bond in Imani at the same time, the three of it twisted together like a rope, and she didn't know any more which of the strands was hers.

When it broke over her she had her face against his neck and her teeth in his shoulder, and she felt it go out along the bond into both of them, and felt theirs come back.

She lay on the floor afterwards with her heart going hard and his weight on her, half on her, half on the towel, and his face in her hair, breathing. She could feel his heart through her own ribs. It was going as fast as hers.

"Bunk," said Imani, above them.

Ferra opened her eyes. Imani was kneeling up beside them with her hair down her back and her face flushed and her mouth swollen, looking down at the two of them on the floor with her doctor's face, and then not her doctor's face.

"Now," said Imani. "The bunk. Me. He's got an hour."

"Have I," said Emeric, into Ferra's hair.

"You've the hour the feast starts late," said Imani, "because nothing in this fleet ever starts on time."

He got up. He picked Imani up off the floor, all of her, in one movement, the way he'd lifted the second board off its trestles, and she gave a short undignified shriek that Ferra had never in her life heard her give, and he put her down on the bunk on her back and went down onto it with her, and the bunk took both of them with a crack from somewhere underneath that none of them paid the least attention to.

Ferra lay on the floor on the wet towel and watched.

She hadn't meant to. She'd meant to get up, and find her other towel, and dry her hair, and lie down beside them. She lay on her side on the boards instead with her head on her arm and watched him with Imani, because she'd never been able to stop herself and had stopped trying: the long brown length of Imani under him on the bunk with her hair spread all over the pillow and her hands flat on his back, on the muscle there, pulling him down; his locs falling round Imani's face now as they'd fallen round hers; the lamp on both of them. Imani didn't close her eyes. She never did. She looked at him the whole time, steadily, with her mouth open and her breath coming short, as if she were reading something written in his face and meant to have every word.

And along the bond it came to Ferra, softened, a breath late, like music through a wall. She lay on the floor and felt it. She felt Imani's hands tighten on his back, and Imani's heels go into the backs of his thighs, and the thing build in Imani that had built in her; and she felt his answer to it, and her own body answer that, lying on the floor with nobody touching her at all.

"Ferra," said Imani, from the bunk, not looking away from him. "Come here."

She went.



They were late to the feast.

The sun was nearly down when the boat put them in at the black-sand cove by the reef station, and the fire was lit and the smoke going up straight in the still air, and the whole of Kaldo's people on the beach and half the Rim, and every face on the sand turned to watch the three of them come up out of the boat; and Arthur, at the edge of the firelight with a cup in his hand and his stripes washed off, looked at the three of them, and at the sky, and said, "You're late."

"We were rinsing," said Ferra.

"For two hours."

"There was a great deal of salt," said Imani, and walked past him to the fire.



Emeric had dug the pit himself, that morning, before the surf.

A long trench in the black sand above the tideline, knee-deep, the way he remembered it being done on a beach on the other side of everything, a long time ago, on a holiday he'd been too young to understand was expensive. The furnace yard had sent the hot stones over by sledge at noon: vent rock, black and porous and holding the seam's heat, from the slag heaps behind the big kilns, still too hot to touch with a bare hand, dragged along the beach path on an iron sledge by six of the yard's men and a great deal of shouting. Two of Armand's fire stones bedded in among them, set low, to keep the pit up to heat through the afternoon. Then the green leaves on top, the Rim's broad sea-kale, and the food on the leaves, and more leaves, and then sand over all of it, and the whole thing left to steam.

There was no pig.

He'd said so, at length, to anyone who'd listen, all week. There was no pig on the Rim. There'd never been a pig on the Rim. The merfolk had never heard of a pig, and when Dagny had tried to describe one to the cook at the furnace yard the cook had gone a colour Dagny said meant she thought she was being made fun of. You couldn't have a feast like this without a pig. It was the whole point of the pit.

"Then why are we doing it?" Bram had said.

"Because it's the closest I can get," Emeric had said, and gone on mourning the pig.

So there were Ilenne's fish instead. Wrapped in kelp, whole, a dozen of them, big flat silver ones she'd had brought up from the queen's own fishing grounds below the ring, that the cook at the furnace yard had looked at and nodded over. And there was the salt pork out of the K Discovery's stores, two casks of it, soaked in fresh water since Tuesday to get the brine out and wrapped in the sea-kale leaves, so that there'd be some pig in it, even if it was a sailor's pig out of a barrel; and yams from the terraces, and the Rim's breadfruit, and a great deal else.

He opened the pit at dusk with Bram and two of the yard's men, with shovels, and the steam came up out of the sand in a cloud that smelled of smoke and sea and pork and something green, and the Rim's people on the beach came closer.



He'd caught a fish too. He'd wanted to. It had seemed, at noon, between sets, a thing a man ought to do at his own feast.

He'd done it in the shallows off the cove's end, with Imani on the rock above him with her board. He'd asked her for it; she'd nodded, and he'd waded out up to his waist and called the forearm, the right one, and the scale had come up it to the elbow and the claws had come down, and he'd stood very still in the clear water with the claws open, waiting; and a fish had come by, a good big one, mottled brown and gold, and he'd had it. One movement. Clean.

He'd carried it up the beach to Ilenne, in the claw, quite pleased with himself.

Ilenne had looked at it.

Then she'd said something to the reef apprentice beside her, a boy of the station, and the boy had looked at the fish and burst out laughing and run off down the beach into the shallows; and Ilenne had turned back to Emeric with her colour very courteous.

"Heavy one," she said, in Kaldo. "Nobody eats."

"Heavy?"

"Here." She touched her own belly. "All night. Nobody eats it. Not even the old ones." She looked at the fish. "Pretty, though."

The boy came back up the beach. He'd waded in off the cove's end in the place where Emeric had stood, with nothing but his own two hands, and come out with two fish, silver, smaller than Emeric's and slim and quick, one in each hand, still flapping; and he held them up to the queen, and the queen nodded, and he took them off to the pit.

Ferra, on the rock beside Imani, looked at the heavy one hanging in Emeric's claw.

"Again?" she said.

Imani was writing on her board.

"Session two," said Imani, without looking up.

It had cost him. He found out how much at dusk, by the pit, when the fire was lit and the sun was gone and he couldn't get warm. One forearm, for about the time it took to stand still in a stream and catch a fish; and an hour after, by the pit, with the hot stones a yard off and the fire going up beside him, he was cold to the bone. Cold like a man who'd been in the sea too long. His teeth wanted to chatter. He held them shut and sat on a log with his arms round himself and watched the pit steam.

Imani came and looked at him. Then she went to the furnace yard's cook, who was standing by the pit with a long fork, and came back with the cook's own blanket, a thick grey wool one off the cook's shoulders, and wrapped him in it from the neck down without a word, and tucked it in.

Then she wrote on her board, and showed him.

One forearm, a minute or two. The hour after: cold to the bone. Blanket.



They ate it all.

The fish came out of the kelp white and falling apart and smelling of smoke. The salt pork came out of its leaves soft as butter and nearly fresh, and the merfolk who tried it made faces at first and then took more. The yams were black outside and gold in. And Emeric, at the end, when he'd stopped being cold, made poke: raw fish, the silver ones the boy had brought, cut in cubes on a board with his own knife, with the Rim's sea salt and a seaweed Ilenne gave him and oil and a little of the sharp green fruit from the terraces; and the merfolk who tried it looked at each other, and then at him, and then said a great many things all at once that Dagny didn't put over.

"They want to know," said Dagny eventually, "whose table you've been eating at. They say that's theirs. That's how the old families do it in the shallows. They want to know who taught you."

He couldn't tell them. He said a man he'd met once, a long way off. Dagny put it over. The merfolk looked at him with deep suspicion and ate the poke.

Imani kept her register all night.

She'd put it up on a plank on two crates at the edge of the firelight, where everyone could see it: her card, the dose card from the K Discovery's galley, with what was in every dish on the beach written on it in her hand and the dose for the unchanged beside each. The knights grumbled. Every knight on the beach had been eating merfolk food for two months and felt entirely well, and every one of them was made to come to the plank and read the card before he took a plate, and Bren read it aloud each time, at the top of his voice, until Mira hit him.



The burned ones came.

Not all of them. Some of the furnace hands off the stair came down the beach path after dark in a little group, slowly, the ones whose dressings were off, and stood at the edge of the firelight as if they weren't sure they'd be let in; and Ferra got up from the sand and went to them and brought them in herself, and sat them down, and the cook brought them food first.

Dauvre was among them. He sat at the edge of the group with the soot still in his hairline, and took the plate the cook gave him, and looked at it, and at the land people round the fire, and at Ward, who was sitting across the fire from him; and Emeric watched him decide nothing at all, again, and eat anyway, every scrap, and hold his plate out for more.

Esseth didn't come. Emeric looked for her. He'd hoped she would, and known she wouldn't; and she didn't. Her son came without her, and ate, and when one of the watch asked him where his mother was he said she'd stayed home, and nobody asked him anything else.

Ward sat with Taurre. He'd come in from beyond the fetch line on the watch's boat at four, by permit, with two of the K Amara's crew, and he sat on a log by the fire beside the watch's captain with the furnace hands on his other side, and said almost nothing all night. He ate. He listened to Taurre talk to his officer in the Rim's tongue, and nodded at the right places, though he understood none of it. When the furnace hands spoke to him he answered in two or three words through whoever was nearest, and when they didn't, he sat there with his big hands on his knees and his face to the fire; and Emeric saw the Rim's people on the beach look at the land knight sitting among the burned ones without talking, and look at each other, and let their colours come up warm.

"They think he's being respectful," said Dagny, at Emeric's elbow.

"He is."

"They think it's on purpose."

"It's Ward," said Emeric. "Everything he does is on purpose."

Neris was there, three days signed, sitting with Dagny and Bram and arguing with Bram about something to do with glaze through Dagny, with her hands. Armand kept well back from the pit; he'd stood too near it at noon when they opened the sand for a look and singed his eyebrows, and he sat on a rock at the edge of the light with the boy who'd brought the fish and explained to him, at length, what a museum was. The boy listened with the face of a child being told about a country that might not exist.

And Ilenne came as queen for the first hour, with the band at her throat and two of her guards, and sat on the cushion the furnace yard's people had brought for her, and was given the first of everything and thanked for the fish in front of everyone; and at the end of the first hour she took the band off her throat and gave it to one of the guards, and the guards went up the beach path into the dark, and she took her shoes off and sat down on the sand by Zale, and stayed as herself.



She came and found him before the end. At the edge of the firelight, where he'd gone to get the set.

"You go south," she said. "From here. Ferra says. You go to the orcs, or the dwarves, or the winged ones. She says you don't know yet."

"We don't."

"Go to the orcs' door before anyone else's." She said it in Kaldo, plainly, as a queen says a thing to another who's asked for nothing. "They count you coldly. Everyone listens to who the orcs count."

He looked at her.

"Ferra will want reasons," he said.

"Ferra will find them," said Ilenne. "I give you the advice. She gives you the reasons. It is how you work."



He gave her the set in front of her people, by the fire, with the whole beach watching.

He'd brought it from the K Discovery's locker that afternoon, after the washroom: the first of the twenty that Tess and the line room's people had built at home in the spring, one for every stop, one for somebody at each that Kaldo liked. This one was hers. It had been hers since the Works, since Tess had stood at the bench with it and said that if it was going to a queen who lived under the sea it had better not leak.

It didn't. It was a brass case the size of a writing box, gasketed all round its lid with rubber that Tess had cured herself, so that the handset sat sealed inside it on its cord and the sea couldn't get at anything; and in the lid a water stone, set to push, so that if the gasket ever started to weep, when it was old, the stone would push the water out of the seam instead of in. The gasket did the work. The stone was for the day it got tired. A warm stone on the valves, under a plate, so they'd keep in the cold of the floor. A call lamp on the front, that lit when the fleet rang. A crank on the side, to charge it.

And on top, in a cradle, a glass float the size of a child's head, on a line.

"Radio doesn't go through seawater," he told her. "Not at all. The water eats it. So the mast goes up." He lifted the float out of its cradle and showed her the line, and the fine wire in the line, and where it came up into the float, which was hollow and held the mast. "You let it up from the water. The float comes to the top, the mast stands up out of it, and you talk from under it. Below, at home. When you're done you wind it in." He turned the crank's handle once. "And when the Rim's ground relay's built, beside the runway, you can plug it into the relay's cable down your stages and not bother with the float at all."

Ilenne took it. She held it on her knees on the sand and looked at it, at the brass, at the float, at the crank.

"Ferra," said Emeric.

Ferra had already opened the lid. There was a card in it, in the lid's pocket, in Ferra's own hand, and she read it aloud, by the firelight, in Kaldo, and Dagny put it into the Rim's tongue after her, line by line, so that nobody on that beach could say later they hadn't heard what it said.

That it rang the fleet. The fleet's ships, wherever they were. Not Kaldo's own board at home, not unless Ferra said so herself, later, in writing. That the fleet's radio room heard whatever it took, every word, because that was what a radio room was for. That anyone else in the world who had a set of their own and patience enough could hear the rest, if they were listening, because radio went where it liked. And that it was the queen's, and she could throw it in the sea any day she chose.

Ilenne listened to all of it. Then she looked up.

"Who else has one?"

"Nobody yet," said Emeric. "You're the first of twenty."

"Twenty." She turned the brass case a little on her knees. "Who are the other nineteen for?"

"I haven't met them."

Ferra wrote it in the gift ledger, on her knee, by the firelight, under Ressar's rubbing. The first gift set: a sealed radiotelephone, waterproofed (float mast; ground cable when the Rim's relay is built), to Queen Ilenne of Aumere, Thu 2 Jul. Its value. And under it a line he read over her shoulder: Sealed. No drawings. A sealed set teaches nothing a stranger can build without the valves; not a strategic transfer. And Ferra's own card, the one in the lid, copied underneath, word for word.

Zale was sitting beside his wife on the sand, watching her turn the case over.

"You'll use that," he said, "to tell me off."

"Yes," said Ilenne.



They walked along the black sand after, the three of them, barefoot, when the fire had burned down to red and the Rim's people had gone up the beach path in twos and threes with their lamps and the knights had gone back to the boats.

The sand was still warm where the pit had been. Ferra stopped on it and stood with her feet in it, and wouldn't come off it for a while. Further down, by the water, it was cool and black and fine as soot and squeaked under their feet.

Imani told him on the way that Abloh had come out to the K Discovery in the boat while they were at the pit, with two parcels done up in brown paper, for the cabin. For Ferra and for her. From the sea silk. The first two pieces off his table.

"What are they?"

"We don't know," said Imani. "We haven't opened them."

"Can I see?"

"Abloh said they're for us," said Ferra. "He said particularly. Not for you." She looked at him sideways in the dark. "He said you'd find out."

"When?"

"He didn't say," said Imani, and he could hear that she was enjoying it.

They walked. The surf came in along the cove's mouth, low, white in the dark, with a sound like breathing. Out past it the reef's edge, and the dark water, and the stars.

He told them about it then. Quietly, with nobody else on the sand. The other place. Two places, really, on the other side of everything: a long cold beach with the sea coming in off the bottom of the world, where he'd learned, badly, as a boy, falling off every wave for a whole summer; and the islands. A chain of black volcanic islands in the middle of the biggest sea there was, warm, with black sand and green mountains straight up out of it and waves that came across the whole ocean before they broke. He'd been there once. He'd only been once.

"It looked like this," he said. "Exactly like this. The sand. The mountain. The way the reef sits." He looked back up the cove at the black cliff of the caldera wall going up into the dark, and the lights of the Rim along the top of it. "I stood on the beach there and thought I'd never see anything like it again."

Ferra was walking beside him with her hand in his.

"Did your other world have waves like these?" she said.

He thought about it.

"Smaller," he said. "Same water, though."

At the water's edge he stopped, and turned round, and took Ferra's hand and Imani's, one in each, and walked backwards toward the surf, pulling, gently, with a look on his face she knew.

"No," said Ferra.

"Just to the knees."

"I've been thrown by the sea," said Ferra, planting her feet in the soot-black sand, "enough for one lifetime. Today. Twice."

Imani laughed, and pulled her hand out of his, and didn't come either.

So he stood at the edge of the water with the surf round his ankles, alone, and the two of them stood on the sand a yard off and looked at him; and then Imani lifted her chin, past him, out at the sea.

He turned round.

Off the reef, out past the break, in the flat dark water where the swells came in smooth, there were two heads. Close together. Very close. One of them had its hair loose on the water, pale in the starlight, and the other had a man's short dark crop and a strapped shoulder showing white when the swell lifted him. They were too far off to see faces. Much too far to hear.

The three of them stood on the black sand and watched the two heads in the water, close together, rising and falling on the swell. Whatever they were saying to each other out there, in the water, they could have been saying it for ever.
