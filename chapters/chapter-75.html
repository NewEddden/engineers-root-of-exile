Chapter 75 - Straight as the Island Allows

He wanted a grid, and the island would not give him one, and the argument he had with the island lasted the better part of a week and taught him something about himself he had suspected and disliked.

He had drawn it clean the first night, alone, the way it lived in his head. A perfect lattice of streets crossing at right angles, blocks of even size, every plot square to its neighbors, a town a man could describe to a stranger in one sentence and the stranger could walk without ever being told a second thing, because a grid is a town that explains itself, and he had spent five years in a place that explained itself to no one, whose lanes wandered where the cattle had once wandered and whose houses sat where the ground had been dry the year a family fled a plague, and he wanted, with a want that surprised him by its heat, to lay a clean order over all that accident and be done with the accident forever.

The island refused. Kaldo sat in the arms of the split river like an almond in a shell, wide in the belly and pinched to points at both ends, and a grid was a thing that fit a plain, not a thing that fit an almond. Where he laid his lattice across the wide middle it sat beautifully, block on even block, and where it ran out toward the pinched ends the river arms sheared it into wedges and slivers and leftover triangles that no square would ever fill, and he found himself at the drawing board doing the thing he most despised in other men, bending the true shape of the ground to protect the beauty of his plan, and he caught himself at it, and he stopped.

Ferra was the one who said it plainly, because she had watched him fall in love with the lattice and had let him fall out of love with it on his own, which was how she managed him when the thing he was wrong about was a thing he would only believe once he had proven it to himself. "You keep drawing the town you'd build on a field," she said, "and then getting angry at the river for not being a field. The island isn't going to move. You can have a grid that fights the island and looks like a mistake at both ends, or a grid that admits the island and looks like it meant to. It's the same town. One of them is honest."

So he built the honest one, and admitting the island cost him the clean sentence he had wanted and gave him a better town. A single spine, the one long straight artery, ran the length of the island from the landward gate above the ford down through the whole belly to the harbor at the seaward point, because that was the axis the town actually used, the road every cart and every message and every man arriving already traveled, and he paved it first and paved it widest, cement over a graded bed, crowned to shed its water into the culverts that ran beneath the walks on either side, so that the first thing a traveler saw coming through the one gate was a straight hard clean road running unbroken to the water, and the last thing a sailor saw leaving the quay was that same road running unbroken up to the seat of the man who ran the place. A cross street met it square in the wide middle, and where the two crossed he set the market square, the civic heart, the flat open paved ground where the town did its public business, and around that crossing he zoned the things a town was proud of, the hall and the ground he had set aside for the Academy and the good shops, all on the high graded rise near the gate where the fall of the land drained cleanest and a fire had the least to catch.

From that cross he let the grid run into the belly where the island was wide enough to take it, regular blocks of even plots, party walls sharing their weight, each block served by the iron he had just laid, a clean-water standpipe set where four blocks met so no household in the belly carried water more than a short walk, and the streets between them drained and paved or hard-packed to be paved as the cement came. And at the pinched ends, where the lattice failed, he let it fail on purpose and made the failure into something. The wedges took the uses that wanted odd ground anyway, the boatwrights and the net-lofts and the warehouses down at the harbor point where the streets bent to follow the water's edge, the drainage works and the last culverts at the upstream point where the ford's overflow needed room to spread. The grid held the middle, where regularity was worth having, and gave way gracefully at the edges, where the ground had its own strong opinion, and the town read, end to end, as a thing that had been thought about, which was the whole of what he had wanted underneath the wanting of the lattice.

The industry he put outside the walls, upstream, and that was not compromise but design. The rapids district, the foundry and the glasshouse and the mill, all the fire and the smoke and the industrial noise and the industrial danger, sat above the town on the mainland by the water power that drove it, off the island entirely, so that the one place a spark could start a catastrophe was the one place there were no packed streets of homes for it to run through, and the smoke blew off the works and not across the market, and the river carried the works' filth away downstream of the town instead of through it. It was the oldest civic wisdom he knew, keep the fire and the stink downwind and downstream of the people, and it was also the newest, because the rapids district was the only part of the whole town he got to lay from nothing, clean from the first stake, a planned company town he built to the grid he could not fully impose on the old island, straight streets and standard plots and honest housing, the thing the island would not let the old town be. He got his perfect lattice after all. He just got it upstream, where no one had lived before, at the price of admitting he could not have it where people already did.

The housing was the part that would either make the town or expose him, and he built it to the rule he had already staked at the foundry row in the plague years and would not walk back now that he had the means to build at scale. Not company housing in the sense that mattered, not homes a man held only as long as he pleased the works, because a town whose people could be turned out of their beds by their employer was a town that obeyed out of fear, and fear was a foundation that cracked the first hard season. The new blocks were built well and built to last and built to be held, plots a family could own or hold on terms that survived a bad year, cement footings that would not settle and split the walls, iron water to the door of the block, drained streets that did not become the standing pools that bred the flea and the fever. He built the houses he would have wanted to be poor in, because a town where the poor lived decently was a town that did not burn its lord's hall in a bad winter, and because, more simply than that, he had buried too many people in this place to build them anything less than a home once he could afford to build them a home at all.

It cost, and he made himself carry the cost with his eyes open, because the census had cured him of the habit of loving the finished thing so much he forgot the wound of making it. Straightening a lane moves the people who live crooked on it. Every block he re-platted into the grid was a block where families had to come out of the houses accident had put them in, and be housed while their new house rose, and trust that the mud and the open trench and the year of disruption would end in something better, and some of them did not trust it, and they were not wrong to withhold the trust, because they were the descendants of people who had been moved before by lords who moved them for the lord's benefit and called it theirs. He gave the displaced first pick of the new blocks, the best of the plots to the people the plan had cost the most, and he still had families who would not go, old people mostly, who had lived their whole lives in a crooked house on a crooked lane and would rather die in the accident they knew than move into the order he offered, and he let them. He left the stubborn blocks crooked. He drew the grid around them and let a few knots of the old wandering town stay knotted inside it, because a man who evicted the old to perfect a map was a man who had forgotten what the map was for, and Ferra had said it best and cruelest when he had grumbled about the blocks that ruined his lines, that the town was not a drawing and the people were not a legend on it, and that the day he started to prefer the drawing was the day he became the kind of lord the grid was supposed to protect them from.

He thought about the capital while he watched the first paved block cure, because the capital was the measure he could not stop himself from measuring against, and because he knew, with the part of him that never turned off, that others would measure it too. Randillia City was grander than Kaldo would be for a generation. It had wealth Kaldo did not have and would not have soon, and it had the thing Kaldo could never buy, the crystal-carved stone of its great roads and its high walls, the magework of centuries laid into the very streets, opulence a coastal exile could not match with all the iron and lime in his three territories. He had seen it. He had walked those carved roads in the life the prince had lived before Marcus arrived to inhabit it, and he remembered them as beautiful and as old, and he remembered, now that he had built a town of his own, the other thing about them that a boy raised in that grandeur had never noticed and an engineer could not stop noticing. The capital was magnificent and the capital was a snarl. It had grown for centuries the way the old part of Kaldo had grown, one accident on the last, palaces backed onto slums, carved marble roads that ran into lanes where the waste still went into the street, grandeur laid over a body that had never been planned, because no one had ever had the chance to plan it, because it had always already been there.

Kaldo would never out-shine the capital. But Kaldo, he understood, standing on a hard clean street that drained its own water past iron pipe into a river downstream of every home it served, could out-think it. He could not carve his roads from crystal. He could grade them and crown them and drain them so a man crossed the town in the rain and arrived dry-shod, which no carved crystal road in the capital could claim. He could not raise the walls the old kings had raised. He could put every family in the town within a short walk of clean water and carry every family's waste out below the tide of everyone's life, which the capital, for all its magework, had never managed for its poor and never would, because you could not retrofit a plan into a city that had already decided its own shape. A man from the capital would come to Kaldo expecting to be unimpressed by a provincial exile's mud town, and he would find, if Emeric built it right, that the mud town worked in ways the capital did not, that it was smaller and plainer and newer and also cleaner and healthier and more legible, a town that read like a thing that had been designed by someone who knew what a town was for, and that the difference between them was not that Kaldo was catching up to the capital but that Kaldo was ahead of it in the one race the capital could not enter, the race a place could only run before it was already built. Kaldo was not the past made grander. It was the future, small and unfinished, showing what it meant to.

And that, he knew, was the danger dressed as the triumph, the way it always was in this arc he had not yet named. A town that made a capital man feel his own capital was behind the times was a town that told that man, and the crown behind him, and the peers behind the crown, exactly how much the exile on the plague coast had learned to do. Every clean street was a fact about his competence. Every drained block, every walk that let a man cross in the rain dry-shod, every family within reach of iron water, was evidence, entered into no ledger and needing none, of a subject who had taken a ruin sized for eighteen hundred and was building it, straight as the island allowed, into a seat men would travel to see. He was building a town worth being proud of, and the pride was real and earned and his, and the very thing that made it worth being proud of was the thing that would, before this was over, make it worth taking from him.

The island was finite. He knew that too, standing at its pinched harbor end where the grid gave out into the wedges of the boatyards, the walls he had built to enclose eighteen hundred now enclosing four thousand and more, the town already pressing outward against the stone skin that had once had room to spare. There was only so much island. A town that kept growing would have to grow up, or grow out past the walls, or grow somewhere else entirely, somewhere with room, somewhere on the coast where a proper harbor could be poured the day the ash came down from Emberfell. He did not follow the thought all the way that afternoon. He only noted it, the way he noted every fact the systems handed him whether he had asked or not, and filed it under the growing pile of things the town's own success was about to make necessary, and turned back up the long straight paved spine toward the gate and the hall, past the crews laying the blocks and the families waiting to move into them and the old crooked houses he had left standing on purpose inside the clean new lines, up the town he was making worth counting, into the last of a light that fell, for once, on streets that meant to catch it.
