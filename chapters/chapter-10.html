Book 2, Batch 10: The Last Dawn

Chapters 75–83 | Monday 21 September – Thursday 15 October, Year 17

On the Sun Shore's dry edge, Emeric has spent two weeks among the Children of the Banked Sun, a charity that gives water without a share, healing without a fee and a home to orphans, built over an ancient machine the Children call the Sun: the Deep Lift. Its High Mother, Hadra, has fed him every night, shown him a gifted memory he found to be built, seated him on her council, and agreed with every word of his confession about the tyrant's list he will not make. She does not know he is a king, though the water court does. Ferra has found the temple's water carts flowing by night to court estates. Imani has noticed that an orphan named Uri has unusually high mana density. Ferra's ledger system now tidies the orphanage's register of each child's strength. The whole crew has bet that the charity hides something bad, and only Emeric bet that it was real.

This batch is where he loses that bet. For forty years the woman who mothered him has been putting willing children into a machine left by the old makers, because the desert dies without it, and she is right about the machine. Emeric finds out the way Kaldo finds out everything out here: a family gone on pilgrimage, a court envoy who cannot resist a cruelty, a dwarf who comes at night with silver in his hand, a doctor reading a register. At the Festival of the Last Dawn he could end her in seconds, and the draw would keep running. He walks into her water instead.

Others carry the rest. Bram and Ulf hold the draw down by hand, the orc wardens open the doors both ways, a Sabir shaft-keeper sits in the makers' seat so the desert does not die, and the water court judges the High Mother. When the relief pair from home comes up the river into a sandstorm, a girl who cannot yet speak a sentence of Kaldo's tongue finds the stranded crew by smell. And a share of water owed to humans for two thousand years is finally asked for.

Chapter 75: The Human King

On Monday 21 September, the family the court evicted on a technicality, whom the Children took in, is gone. Their places at the kitchen bench are empty. The priests say they asked to go on pilgrimage to the Sun. Nobody on the dry edge has ever met a pilgrim coming back. Emeric adds a line to the list in his head and does not say it aloud.

Across these days he lives through ordinary moments, each seen from where he stands: a dry-edge mother in the healing house thanking Hadra for her son, a Sabir orphan girl telling Imani she wants to be the High Mother when she grows up, a family emptying a court-share purse into the Children's box. None of them knows what the charity is tied to.

On Wednesday the Children announce the Festival of the Last Dawn at the temple court to every people's envoys, promising an end to drought, sinking ground and failing fields at the turn of the water year. Hila is thrilled. The winged couriers carry the invitations out, and one of them looks at the K Discovery on the caravan ground for a long time before she flies. She is Sefa, the couriers' flight-leader, grey-winged with one white primary feather she was born with, and she comes back. The festival wants its news carried home the same morning, so the couriers who carried the invitations stay to carry the results, and from Thursday Sefa waits on the dome's rim, watching the human king's ship more than the temple.

The court's envoy arrives to accept the invitation and greets Emeric across the temple court as the human king, meaning to embarrass Hadra in front of her own people. It works. Maeve reads the envoy for Kaldo, the way Saban would: he enjoyed it, and somebody at the court told him to do it.

That night is the only evening with no food and no cup. Hadra asks why he hid it. "Would you have fed me?" She would not have. He says, "I took the crown because someone was going to, and I'd rather it was someone who wants it gone." She thinks it is the cleverest lie she has ever heard.

Chapter 76: Show Me a King Who's Starving

From Thursday 24 September, for a week, Hadra watches Mochi follow Emeric around the temple and looks for the trick. Every child on the dry edge knows a king slime will not stay with the greedy. She has an answer: "Slimes read hunger. He isn't hungry because he already has everything. Show me a king who's starving." Emeric cannot answer it. She wins the argument, and is a little less sure. At the temple door an old orc warden tells Ward he is guarding his own grandchildren. Ward notices Sefa on the dome's rim watching the K Discovery more than the temple.

On Friday Hadra takes Ferra and Imani aside on the healing house porch and quietly offers them shelter with the Children, safe from him. Ferra laughs first. Imani explains it the way a doctor explains something to a patient: he was so ashamed of wanting both of them that he would have chosen neither, so they decided between them how it would work and then told him. Ferra adds that she holds a written veto over him in the crown's own charter and has never had to use it. "We chose him. He was the one who needed convincing." Over Friday and Saturday they overrule him in public. In the middle of a council sitting Imani orders him, as his doctor, to sit down and eat. Ferra cuts a number he is proud of in front of the Children's clerks. He does what they say. Hadra's answer reveals something of her own past, an old marriage to a court family's son, without stating it: "Women in a king's house say what keeps them safe. I said much the same at their age."

At home in Cinderhale, Corbin presses Martha Ollin too hard about what she saw in the watch yard. She stops trusting anyone from Kaldo, packs her bag that night and leaves Cinderhale. The League loses its witness. On Saturday's call Emeric hears that Keller's bailiffs, Pim's word, the weapon register and the matched ball have put one shooter under charge, while the officer who gave the order has not been charged. Corbin says that Martha Ollin is gone, and says why, which costs him. Emeric listens and says nothing useful, and knows it.

That night Ulf, the dwarf on the temple's machinery contract, comes to Emeric on the guest house step with three things. The draw runs on nights the temple's records say the seat rests. His own gauges, which he has kept privately for a year, show it. And he holds out silver water-court seals, which he found in the silt of the filter galleries, where the salt slimes could not eat them. The Sabir wear their court seals as jewellery. Ulf does not know what it means and will not guess. He asks Emeric to.

Chapter 77: Raised to the Given

On Sunday 27 September, two dry-edge families come to the Children's council asking where their kin, raised to the Given, have gone, because the seals Ulf found have come back to them by name through Ferra's ledger. Imani lays the orphanage's register, the healing house's notes on each child's strength, beside the list of the Given. The strongest children are the ones raised. They are chosen by density. Uri's name is on the festival's list, in the very register Ferra's system tidied. Imani brings it to the guest house table as a column of names and numbers. Her objection is not a speech. It is the column.

On Monday the disclosure lands on Emeric, and it is not an explosion. He goes quiet, finishes the allocation sheet in front of him, and leaves the council room. That night, with Ferra and Imani, he says she was doing so well, that he had let himself hope there was nothing under it, that he had been glad there were people like him out here. He is angrier at the hope than at her. The pool pays out, and Emeric is the only loser. He pays everyone in full that night by field set to Linnet. The next morning Ferra takes the whole pot back off the winners and puts it in the families' fund. Nobody argues. It is not funny any more.

On Tuesday, in the healing house, Imani tells Uri what the Given are, because his name is on the list and he asked. Emeric stands a little behind her. Uri asks whether the High Mother loved them, or whether they were always going to be offerings, not as an accusation but with the honest confusion of a child who thought she cared.

Armand goes down the shaft's maintenance stair with Hila and Ulf to read the core's makers' script. Gisela's word fits: the one who sits. The core is a seat, a control-stone heart built for a mind to regulate the draw the way a rider sits a golem. Its operator left with the makers, and it has run on its last standing order ever since, drifting. Mochi, riding Armand's shoulder, pulses at the core and refuses to go near the seat, so Armand has to put it down on the stair. From a line he copies twice, Armand also works out that the makers built the Lift to run under a threshold, and that running it at full would be loud in a way the script cares about. He writes that down and does not decide what it means. At the guest house table Emeric's plan to feed the Lift power instead of people, liquid mana from the relief pair's stores and stones if he must, fails on paper in front of Armand. Emeric read it as a pump short of fuel. It is short of a mind.

On Wednesday Kaldo takes the seals, Ulf's gauges, the families' testimony and Imani's column to Ghassan. The court will not move against the temple, since its own families drink the temple's water, but people on the court's roll are below the temple floor, and the seals prove some were. Ghassan's office issues the court's warrant for persons of the roll, to be served on a court officer's call, which also lifts the firearms condition at that call. Siv carries the truth to the orc warden captain in the wardens' own speech, with Ward beside her, because an orc should hear it from an orc. The captain, the same old warden guarding his grandchildren, says the doors will open both ways if the Children try to bar them.

At the guest house table Emeric lays out his plan: stop Hadra at the shaft's lip first, then go down for the Given himself. Ward says no. The Given cannot wait for a fight to end, and one man cannot be at the lip and in the chamber at once. Imani, Maeve and Zale should go down the maintenance stair at the first minute, Ward and the Crestless should hold the doors, and Emeric should stay on the floor. Emeric argues once, hears the arithmetic, and gives way. It is the first time Ward's objection has changed what Emeric does. That afternoon Zale comes up from the river mouth on the K Amara, his ship left in company under his mate, because the Lift is water everywhere and Imani wants him below.

That night Hadra, knowing now that he is a king, offers him the place beside her, because she needs him more than she hates kings. He tells her she has been a mother to him these weeks, and means it. He tells her about his mother: she died when he was seven in the attack on her carriage, he had forgotten her face, he found out how she died, he got her back, and he left her within the year because the world past the wall needed him. "I keep losing her. This time I did it to myself." In his head, briefly, he thinks of Marcus on Earth, adopted, who never met the woman who had him, and he notices the pattern runs through both lives. Hadra does not soften. That is why she wanted him beside her, she says: a mother does the hard thing for the children who cannot. The tenderness and the horror are the same instinct. He says no. She says she had hoped he would not.

Chapter 78: The Last Dawn

On Thursday 1 October, in the hour before sunrise, the temple court is full: envoys of every people, and the dry edge come in its thousands. The Given are already underground in the chamber beside the seat. The draw begins, and the glow far down changes from a pulse to a steady burning. On the dome's rim, where she has sat for a week, Sefa calls down that the sky over the dry edge is wrong. She does not know that what she sees is the Lift running hot.

Ward holds the doors with Arthur's group. The orc wardens, told the truth, open them both ways, and the evacuation goes through. Ferra and the court's clerks move the crowd out to the caravan ground by districts, the way the court moves people at a hearing. Bram and Ulf throttle the draw by hand at the shaft head's brake gear, buying minutes rather than saving anyone. The priests' guards wear shard charms, and two ball rounds from the doors are caught on them. The knights close hand to hand in the heat, and a K Amara knight's arm is broken. Nobody of Kaldo's is killed.

Below, Imani goes down the maintenance stair with Maeve and Zale. Zale holds the Lift's water off the chamber's floor, Maeve shapes it out of the stair, and Imani chooses who is carried up first. That choice is hers.

On the temple floor, Emeric tells Hadra, "I meant it. Last night. I still mean it." Then he steps into her water. This is her ground, the floor around the shaft with the Lift's water everywhere. In a desert she would be short of water; here she has never been stronger, she is better at her two magics than he is at any of his, and the air is thick. Her mirage puts her where she is not, but it does not work on him, because he fights by mana sense rather than by eye; she has never fought someone her illusions cannot touch. Only the first signs of his change show, gold eyes flaring and forearm plating under the fire, no wings and no full change.

He has the easy way and she knows it: boil her where she stands, or bury her under the pull, seconds at little cost. But the draw would keep running on the shape she left in it, because only the caster knows a spell's exact shape and can reverse it. So he takes the hard way: get to her alive at the shaft's lip and take her hand off the command stone, so she has to talk to him. He walks into her water instead of boiling it off her. He holds fire skin on his arms and chest but not his face, because full cover takes his breath, so his face and throat take the scalding. He keeps the work small and controlled at the edge of his reserve on purpose. There are two lines he will not cross: he will not pull the floor out, because the Given and the priests are below it, and he will not touch the drain, because it would take from the crowd. Then she throws Uri's mirage between them. He senses it is empty and still flinches, because he cannot be sure the real boy is not behind it. She sees the flinch.

She is a delver and reads mana. She sees that he has left his strength unspent all fight and paid for it in skin. Kings do not do that. Nobody does that for an enemy. At the shaft's lip he has her down with the pull, finds the finger's width that would end it, holds it, and does not. He stops because she is the person he hoped existed, doing good in the worst way, and he has the means to change her circumstances. That he needs her alive for the reverse is true, and is not why. A clean kill would have cost a tenth of what this cost. The High Mother lies on her back at the lip of the shaft with Emeric's pull holding her at a finger's width, his face and throat scalded raw, and the draw still running on her shape.

Chapter 79: Eleven Rooms

While Bram and Ulf hold the draw down, Emeric makes his offer. "You make Seeings. You know what a built room looks like. Come in, look at mine, and find me one lie. If you find it, I'll leave and you can finish." She says yes, because she is certain, because she can check, and because underneath it she wants another way more than she has let herself want anything. Without consent it could not happen at all.

Every room is memory, not built, and she checks each one the way she checks her own priests' Seeings. She walks to the back of a kitchen and finds a pot boiling over that he never looked at, and that is how she knows. He has walled off his other world; she finds the wall and says he keeps a door shut too. He says yes, and she lets it go. Nothing in the rooms is built, and nothing shows what has not happened. The rooms go in order. First, what Kaldo was and is, and who built each piece. Second, his count: Tam; Tess on the dynamo-house floor and then in her chair; the forcing deaths; Sela not blaming him; and that he never picked a name. Third, the people who can stop him: the Standing Order, Ferra's unused veto, Commencement, Tess guessing ahead of him. Fourth, the Wayford cellar with its eight chairs and Tansy, and then the Lift's chamber as he saw it from the shaft's lip that morning, the same chair made holier; he says nothing. Fifth, where her bargain leads: the Church's rite, a continent-sized version of her trade. Sixth, Uri, from Tuesday afternoon. Seventh, the ones who trusted her: the mother, the orphan girl, the warden and the purse, checked and real. Eighth, because she asks, who chose whom: Ferra and Imani deciding and telling him, from his side. Ninth, what someone does afterward: Corbin holding Kaldo, Armand teaching without a tower. Tenth, the council table, where weeks before anything hung on it Ferra half-joked about the High Mother being a monster and he said he would still get her out alive, with Ferra's pen, Imani's cold tea and the smell of the ink Hadra hates, all real. So the fight was not about the stone, the wives were not giving safe answers, and the slime may simply be right. Eleventh is her own room, the well, the only room she controls: her mother's voice, bread on a stone, the spring the desert bloomed and she ran in it, "So our children can live here and be happy," and then the dry year and the clerk turning them from the well. The back of her room is full. She lays it beside the sixth and seventh rooms herself.

Then the confrontation. "You showed me what I wanted to see." "You checked. You walked to the back of the kitchen and found the pot." "You think I didn't know what those children meant to their families?" "I think you knew. That's why you stopped asking them." "I carried the whole desert." "And decided that meant nobody else was allowed to carry it with you." What breaks, it breaks in order: her belief that every royal is a leech, her belief that he was using her, and then, in her own room, the realisation that she made herself the only one allowed to decide. She asks, "How many are you willing to let die for your morals?" "I don't know. I've got a number already. You saw it." "Then what's the difference?" "I never picked one of them." His answer ends with everybody being stupid in the same direction until something works.

She cancels the draw with the exact reverse through the command stone. It takes her a long time, because her hands shake. Afterward she cries, not because she is good now, but because she understands she did not have to do it alone. It is the first time in forty years she lets herself consider she was wrong. At the shaft's lip, scalded and nearly empty, Emeric puts his arms round her and lets her cry into a coat that is still steaming. When she can look at him she sees what she did to his face and throat, and that breaks her the rest of the way: she was going to put him in a chair, and she burned the one man who walked into her water to keep her alive. He tells her it will be healed by the end of the day. It will not, not on the ground. It is the kind of thing you say to someone crying on you, and she half believes it because she wants to. Imani, below the lip with the first of the Given, hears it and says nothing.

At midday on the caravan ground, where the carried Given lie in the shade of the K Discovery's legs and the court's clerks are still counting the crowd, Ward faces a zealot: one of the priests' guards, charmed and carrying a hooked blade, who will not accept that the High Mother cancelled the draw and believes the Given have refused the Sun. He is offered a clear way out through the orc wardens' line, refuses it, and goes for the Given on the ground. Bren kills him with his blade, because the charm catches anything fired. Then a K Amara knight, wild with heat and the morning, goes at three faithful who have already knelt and dropped their staves. Ward stops him bodily and holds him until he stops shaking. Imani tends the dead man, and Ward gives his account in writing to Ghassan's officer before dark. Hadra will have to answer for what her movement inspired without being reduced to its worst adherent.

That night Imani's count comes up from below. Of about two hundred carried up, fourteen are dead. The rest are drawn thin, and most will wake within days in Bettany's ward. Among the living is a girl from the orphanage list. Lying under a hull with his face in wet linen, Emeric knows the number. Later he finds Ward at the K Discovery's legs and tells him the two hundred were saved by Ward's plan, not the king's. Ward says he knows. Emeric says he wanted to say it anyway.

Chapter 80: The Seat

On Thursday night, in the K Discovery's cabin with his face still scalded, Emeric tells Ferra and Imani the whole of it, Earth and all: the adopted kid on his other world and the woman who had him, his mother here, both losses, and that he had never thought of himself as a man with mother wounds. Ferra writes none of it down. Amara is not on the dial for it; she will get her own call when he is on his feet.

On Friday, with the draw cancelled, the Lift begins to drop. Armand's reading is plain: it needs a mind in the seat, or the desert dies over the next generation. Hadra was right about that. Hila volunteers, permanently, believing it is what her faith was for. Emeric refuses the permanent part. A golem ride is a borrowed body and the rider comes back, and he has already brought a mind back from a stone into a body, his mother's. A seat built for a maker who lived thousands of years does not have to be one mortal mind forever. It can be held in shifts. Trained delvers will sit it in turns, their bodies kept warm and fed in a ward beside the shaft that Bettany sets up and the Children's healers keep, and nobody is drained, because the rider regulates the draw rather than feeding it. The delvers will be the Sabir's own, the Children's delving priests and Hadra. Kaldo gives no stones.

At the shaft head Ghassan asks why the Lift cannot simply be replaced, and Emeric answers from a stool because he cannot stand for long. The Lift is a makers' machine that lifts brackish water from a deep salt aquifer, sweetens it through the filter galleries and pushes it up into the fresh aquifer every well and channel drinks from: pumps, filters and a regulator, and the regulator is the seat. The rota is not a sacrifice. The Given died because the draw had no limit and a body was left in the chamber to be emptied; a rider's mind goes into the stones while the body sleeps warm and fed, nothing is drawn, and the mind comes back. A shift is a night watch, not a death. Why not a script, like Kaldo's golems? A standing order is exactly what the Lift has run on for two thousand years, and standing orders drift. Writing a better one means writing into a makers' stone in a script nobody alive reads in full, and one wrong line could stop the only thing keeping the desert alive. Why not tear it out? Kaldo has the pumps but not the scale, a dead Lift during a ten-year build would mean a dead desert, and it is not his to tear out; it belongs to the Sabir and the dry edge. But he doesn't stop at no. A desert shouldn't hang forever on a machine nobody alive can read, he tells Ghassan, and it shouldn't swap that for one only Kaldo can mend. More wells alone aren't new water, since the Lift is what fills the aquifer they draw on. So he offers a road the Sabir can build themselves: now, a salt bore into the deep salt water the Lift draws on, drilled on a rope-and-treadle rig, and glass stills of their own desert glass with the sun doing the boiling; later, when Kaldo has planes past the wall, a plant at the river mouth drawing sea water and a pipe up the river's gap, pushing new water into the ground under the dry edge so every well and channel keeps working. Privately he thinks the Lift kept them alive and made them hostage, and the road mustn't do either. He leaves the road open: when Armand can read the slab and the core through and Tess can write the makers' script, a scripted regulator might take some of the shifts, with the Compact's leave. Ghassan writes it down with the date.

Imani prescribes the Band. On the ground, nearly empty and under strain, Emeric's self-healing barely moves, and the densest air within reach is five miles up. At dusk on Friday the K Discovery makes a routine climb to charge and check the river relay, whose heartbeats have been ragged since the Lift ran hot. Emeric stays out there. Clipped in his Band suit to the river relay's harness rings, with Sorrel and a flight knight on the next drum as his dragon watch, he hangs in the densest air on that side of the world. His scalds close through Saturday, leaving only a few days of pink, and his reserve is full by Sunday morning. Ferra notes that the most powerful man in the fleet spent the weekend hanging off a radio like washing.

That same Friday night, Hila goes into the seat first, by choice, since nobody else is trained. Hadra guides her in under the court officer's guard, because she knows the seat and Emeric is already on his way to the Band. Letting it happen is the first time he trusts Hadra with anything, and her first act of cooperation. Hila, briefly in her own view, goes in.

On Saturday, before the dry edge, the court's clerks and the envoys still in the city, Hadra confesses what the Children did: the Given, what she and the priests knew about the Lift, and who was involved, the priests who led the Giving, the court families who bought Lift water by night, and the dwarf contractors who kept the machinery. Ulf testifies with her and is cleared, since his crew did not know what the draw fed. She also reveals the oldest secret. The temple's founding book, kept in the High Mother's locked rooms, says the Children's first High Mother was human, an engineer from one of the households that went with the old peoples, in Sabir service, who kept the Lift running by her own wits and some terrible guesses after the makers faded. The religion grew around her procedures after she died, and the Children hid her for two thousand years, because on the dry edge humans are the war's villains. Gisela was right that the founding was younger than they said. Hadra answers for the zealot as the head of what he believed. The families of the Given ask their questions aloud to her face: why did you not tell us, why did you not trust us to help. She argues for nothing, neither her charity nor her remorse. She dismantles the Giving, its rite, its registers and the chute to the galleries, keeps the Lift running, and gives the families names and dates. The silver seals go back to the families who know them. She gives up the High Mother's seat and every power that let her decide for others.

On Sunday the court has sealed the temple wells pending a compact, and the dry edge's stations are a day apart. At one settlement a grandmother and two children are dying of heat and thirst, a day's walk from their nearest share, and Bettany's ward cannot move them. Dagny finds shallow water under the settlement within reach of Kaldo's hand drill in an afternoon. Emeric, by field set from the river relay five miles up, reads the Charter's emergency clause one way: temporary lifesaving help is allowed, so bore it, set the held well stone on a pump for as long as the danger lasts, then take it out. Ferra reads it another way: a bore stays a bore, whoever holds it next week decides who drinks, which is taking a side in the court's dispute with the dry edge, and permanent political intervention never counts as an emergency. Both are right. He orders it, and the household lives. Ferra enters her dissent in the ledger, where he cannot delete it, for the Assembly to read on their return. The stone comes out on Thursday when Bettany says the danger is over, Kaldo caps the bore, and Ghassan is told where it is.

Chapter 81: The Red Signal

On Monday 5 October, the relief pair from home, hulls five and six, come up the river's gap on the day in Ferra's book. Aboard hull six is Cade, the yard man who laid both hulls, sailing to nurse them the whole way. In the afternoon a haboob rolls off the basin's western sand, a wall of dust a mile high, and the pair is separated. Hull five gets down on the river below the city. Hull six is blown off the river over the salt flats downstream and forced down at dusk on the crust, holed.

That night a red hand flare shows where no light should be. Dry-edge raiders have lured caravans onto those flats with lights before; Mukhtar's men did it. The task is to verify the signal and get the people out without losing a second hull. The K Endeavor goes, at night, her planted deck and trim harness straining, the night-landing drill Ward ran before the fleet ever sailed now paying off. Sorrel, grounded by the dust, stands on the K Endeavor's bridge, takes an old struck jet drawing from her case, looks at it and puts it back. From the deck, the flare is lost in the dust. Runa, harnessed at the rail beside Grau, points: people, sweat and burned powder, that way. She is right, and she never leaves the rail.

The signal is real, but the crust cannot bear the K Endeavor. A team goes down on lines, Arthur, Mira, Dagny and four K Endeavor hands, into a world where sight and flight give no advantage. The night glass shows a wall of grey and the heat layer shows nothing past fifty yards, so Runa's nose at the rail is the only guide anyone has. Straps alone cannot lift hull six: nobody can pass a sling under a ship lying on the ground, at night, in a haboob, without digging, and the crust caves under anything that digs. Her own lift stones are flat from fighting the storm. But she is carrying the answer: twelve gravity anchors in her hold, built by Tess at home since the first call day for the night Zale's ship could not lift, and sent out on the relief pair for Zale. Cade knows the crates and says so on the set. Each anchor is a steel pad the size of a cartwheel hub, with a magnet that grips steel plate, a grip stone that holds it to anything solid, a lift stone that pushes in any direction the pad's dial sets, a winch eye for the ordinary carry, and a needle showing its charge. The line team slaps them along hull six's ribs, and the K Endeavor holds over her with cables from her winches to the eyes.

The anchors were rated for Zale's ship, not a loaded relief hull, and the K Endeavor's own lift is drawn down by the storm flight, so it comes to people or equipment. To lift hull six off the crust with her crew aboard, her master orders the crates of a new house printer, sent from home, over the side. People first. Dagny thinks to put two anchors on the printer's gantry before it goes over, which is why the gantry is the part later recovered. One of hull six's riggers has a broken leg, and the line team carries him. Hull six comes up off the crust hanging under the K Endeavor.

At dawn on Wednesday, hull six comes up the river under the K Endeavor on Tess's anchors and is set down holed beside hull five at the anchorage. The rendezvous has slipped two days and stayed inside its window, so the fallback at the river mouth is not needed. The printer's gantry is found, its head and sand-bed smashed. Emeric took no part. Hila was in the seat, he was the only one who could bring her back if it went wrong, and Ward would not send a flier into a haboob over a salt flat. He spent both nights at the shaft head with the set, hearing every minute of it, which is worse than being told.

Chapter 82: The Sentence

On Thursday 8 October, Emeric is whole again from the Band. Imani has not let go of his "end of the day," which was two days out. "End of the day." "I didn't say which day." The relief cargo comes ashore: about two months of food, spares, liquid mana, medical stores, what is left of the printer, two new relay drums from Tess's floor, and the mail, including Nan Coombe's letter from Wayford. He reads it, keeps it, and does not answer. On the K Endeavor's deck the relief pair's Kaldo bread is opened, and Runa says her first sentence in Kaldo's tongue: the food smells too clean. Grau laughs out loud.

On Friday, under Hila's watch from the seat, one charge of liquid mana is run into the core and measured by Armand and Ulf against Ulf's gauges, written up for the court as a technical study. It steadies the draw for a day and shows what power can and cannot do. After that it is the rota's. On the edge of Nadi's district Bram sets up a trial of the printed house. With the print head gone, the walls are laid in kiln block and dustcrete with crushed wadi gravel, and Bram copies the chimney termites' mounds for cooling, a tall vent pulling hot air out through the roof. By Wednesday one house's walls stand, and Nadi's neighbours roof it with palm beams their own way. Emeric writes the sand's purity in his notebook and says nothing about why. Beside the printer rig Bram draws a still from the K Endeavor's distiller: the court's potters throw the basins and Sabir glassworkers cast the lids, and by Saturday the first bank stands on the pilot's brackish drain water. Imani's cards pass Monday's water, and Nadi's daughter keeps the tally.

On Saturday, at the Lift Compact's first sitting, the water court sits with dry-edge elders beside it and sentences Hadra. She will serve on the rota for up to ten years, every third night, reviewed each year at the water year's turn and able to be shortened for good service. She will give public testimony until every family has its answers, and hold no title, seat, vote or office in the Children or the Compact while the sentence runs. After it she may serve again from the bottom, as a rota rider first, then whatever the Compact's own rules let any rider rise to, by vote and never by appointment, and never again as High Mother. It is not execution, because her forty years are what make the rota work, and water law says you repay what you took. It is not exile, because the rota needs her at the shaft. The families who wanted her dead say so aloud and say ten years is nothing. The court records it, and nobody tells them they are wrong. The elder reading the sentence answers them once: the share is repaid in water, and a dead woman pours none. Kaldo testifies and holds no seat.

Now that the bench knows why the cursed share was written, owed for the founder's work and withheld because of the war, Emeric asks for it, the first human to do so in two thousand years, and directs every drop to the families of the Given. The court pays it in shares written to the families by name and closes the article. Ferra enters it as received and passed on, in a single line. On that day's call from home, one shooter has been convicted and the officer who gave the order has walked, his log rewritten and witnesses scared. Maudie has joined the League, refused the crown's compensation, and told Corrick off for his banner. Harrold reports the Isle's oil flowing under his exception. Afterward Amara comes on the dial. He tells her about the delve, and she says he walked into scalding water for a woman who was going to put him in a chair, and asks whether it was worth it. Yes.

Chapter 83: The Compact

On Monday, with the court families' night water purchases now public, the two canal districts rejoin the joint sitting. The salt pilot passes under the court with independent monitors, Qamar's agricultural house and a clerk from the orc treaty-house, each able to stop it: soil maps by depth, measured irrigation, test drains, the saltbrush belts first, and the lined brine channel across the herders' basin to the salt flats, with the herders paid a court-guaranteed well share for the crossing. The cost moves onto the herders, whom the court also protects. Their elder says so to the bench, and it is written in. Nadi's district goes first.

On Tuesday Emeric brings Hila back from the seat himself, as he once brought his mother back, with Hadra beside the seat holding the handover. Hila comes back thinner, her faith changed but not gone: the Sun was never a god, she says, but sitting in it is the nearest she has been to one. Nobody laughs at her. The first rota, four of the Children's priests and Hadra, takes the seat that night.

On Wednesday the Lift Compact is signed at the court's orc treaty-house. The water court, the dry edge and the rota will govern the Lift together. Ferra drafts it, and Kaldo holds no seat. Its first rulings reopen the temple wells under the Compact, take the capped bore onto the dry edge's register with a hand pump and a measured draw held by the settlement, leave the well stone with Kaldo, and let the Children's priests keep their stones to sit the rota, which loosens the court's grip on who may delve. Iddo is the first to try selling a rota seat, and the Compact strikes his name off its list before the ink is dry. The court charters a dry-edge mutual society the same day to carry on what the Children fed, its kitchens and orphanages continuing with no High Mother; Wafa keeps its water tally. Some of the Children never knew, some knew and believed there was no other way, and nobody is written as a villain for having been kind. Ghassan's survey is answered: the falling wells and sinking ground were the Lift weakening, and he writes it in the court's book with Emeric's line about pumping beside it. Bettany leaves the vaccine method and the first Sabir blood samples with the court's healers, results to come by relay, nothing promised. The Compact gives the new water an article of its own, which Ferra calls the Water Road: the stills and the salt bore are the Compact's, and the sea road is written in as intent, with nothing promised as cargo and Kaldo to return by invitation. Court, dry edge, dwarves and orcs will each hold a piece of it and nobody can turn the tap on anybody, which is the point. The bore was spudded at the dry edge on Monday, with Ghassan's diggers on the treadle, Dagny showing them the drop and the catch, and a bit forged by Ulf's dwarves; the Sabir will finish it. Ferra's letter to the Sea Hold, asking what pipe the dwarves could make, goes down the river with the pilot.

Gisela asks to come, the first passenger who asks. Her terms are a berth, her own key to whatever Kaldo keeps when they get home, and the slab coming with her as her fare so Armand can finish reading it. Ferra fights her for a day over who holds the slab's notes and loses half the argument. Gisela takes the spare berth, and the interpreter's contract ends and she stays in her city. The relief pair turns for home carrying the two badly burned from the dragon fight, letters (Emeric's to Harrold, none to Nan), the memory-metal drawings and the turbine page, the lodestone sand, a first cargo of desert glass chosen by Neris, and Imani's quarantine rules for every seed and sample, including date-palm offshoots, sorghum, chickpeas, long-staple cotton, and saltbrush seed for Milford's salted ground. The burned go home; their diaries stay in the K Endeavor's strong box under Ferra's wax until the fleet itself returns, which the burned knight minds more than the burns. Cade goes home with the hull he laid. Emeric gives Hila the sixth gift set at the shaft head with Ferra's card read aloud. She asks if she may open it. "Not this one." That night she calls the K Discovery to ask what the third valve is for. Fourteen sets remain. Ferra asks the water court for flat ground on the caravan ground for winged machines, and it agrees on its own terms, the court's ground under water law with a paid share for the crew's water, kept by the court's officer and closable by the court, Kaldo's third field. Emeric again names the catch: no regular flights until the old promise is answered.

Hadra does not come to see him go. She still feels guilty for everything she did and for hurting him. He finds her anyway, before dawn on Thursday, at the shaft head with its one lamp, at the ward's door before her first night's shift. He is whole, and she still looks at where the scalds were. She cries again, and this time he does not hold her; he sits beside her on the step until she stops. He asks whether, when her time is served, she will come and visit him. She says it is a long way, but yes. "By the time you come, it won't be a long trip at all." He means the field on the caravan ground and the planes he has not yet built. She thinks he is being kind. "And I'm coming back anyway. I owe your court a pipe." He tells her Hila has a telephone and that she should use it. Then the hulls lift down the Sun River toward the sea, and the shaft head's one lamp stays lit with nobody at it to watch, which is how she wanted it.

Where the Story Stands After Chapter 83

The Giving is over. Hadra cancelled the draw after Emeric walked into her water and let her check his memories for a lie, but fourteen of about two hundred Given died before she did, and he knows the number. She confessed, revealed that the Children's founder was a human engineer, gave up her title, and is now sentenced to ride the rota every third night for up to ten years, from the bottom. The Deep Lift is governed by a new Lift Compact, held by a rota of the Sabir's own delvers in shifts, and Hila came back from the first eleven days changed but not broken. The cursed share owed to humans for two thousand years was asked for and paid to the families of the Given. The salt pilot passed with independent monitors, though its cost now falls on the herders, and Ferra's dissent over the emergency bore waits in her ledger for the Assembly. The Water Road gives the desert its first way off the Lift that the Sabir can build and mend themselves: glass stills and a salt bore now, and a sea road when Kaldo can come back.

Emeric fought without his best protection and healed in the Band, his violence held at the very edge, and the full dragon change still waits for its trigger. For the first time, Ward's objection changed his plan, and Ward's plan saved the Given. Armand has found that the makers built the Lift to run below a threshold, and that running it hot would be loud in a way their script cares about, and has decided nothing about what that means. Every people past the wall now knows that Kaldo's humans can work on the makers' machines.

The relief pair arrived through a haboob, holed but with everyone alive, thanks to Tess's gravity anchors and Runa's nose, and it has turned for home with the burned, the drawings, the lodestone sand and the desert glass. The anchors are recovered and stowed for Zale's ship. Gisela has joined the fleet with her slab, Runa has spoken her first sentence in Kaldo's tongue, and the interpreter has gone home, leaving no one aboard to speak for Kaldo at the next stop. At home, one Cinderhale shooter was convicted while the officer walked, Martha Ollin is gone, Maudie has joined the League, Corbin works with it rather than commanding it, and Nan Coombe's letter remains unanswered. The fleet sails down the river with seven relays, fourteen gift sets and about 104 tonnes of food toward its next stop.

This batch is the heart of the desert stop: a good woman doing a terrible thing for a true reason, and a king who could have ended it in seconds choosing the slower, more costly way that let her end it herself and left the desert in the hands of the people who live there.
