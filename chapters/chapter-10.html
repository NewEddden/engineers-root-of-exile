Chapter 10 - The Crown's Judgment

The judiciary chamber wasn't large. Emeric had expected spectacle, the kind of room a kingdom builds to remind people what power looks like from below. What he got was close and plain. A long table, hard chairs, windows set too high to see out of. Built for work. That told him something about Keller before the man said a word. Keller was older than he'd expected, grey at the temples, a stack of papers already squared in front of him like he'd read every page twice before anyone arrived. He didn't stand when they came in. He didn't need to.

"Prince Emeric." A nod, nothing else. "Baron Wexler."

Wexler sat across the table, composed in a way that was visibly costing him. Cold and calculated, same as he'd been in Kaldo, but the wariness had a new edge on it, a man running his own odds and liking the answer less every time he ran it.

"I petitioned this hearing," Wexler said, "because rumor has a way of becoming record if it's left unanswered. I'd rather answer it directly."

"Then answer it directly," Keller said. "What rumor?"

"That I've misused the barony's debt records for personal gain." His voice stayed even. "I haven't. I'd like that stated plainly, for the record, before anything else gets attached to my name that doesn't belong there."

Keller didn't respond to that at all. He turned to Emeric instead. "You have evidence, or you don't. I'm not interested in which of you says it louder."

Ferra stepped forward before Emeric could answer for her, which he preferred. She'd ridden in the night before, ahead of nothing but her own courier, and slept about as much as the horse had. This was hers to carry.

"Three ledger pages out of the barony's own accounts. Scraped and rewritten." She laid them flat and turned them so Keller didn't have to. "The original entries recorded a debt discharge routed through a waypoint marker, not a merchant house. That marker belongs to a dispatch route, and the route belongs to a man named Cobb."

She set a second paper beside the first, one that hadn't existed two days ago, still creased from hard travel.

"This confirms the rest. Cobb's payment records, cross-referenced against the coin marks in the barony's own remittance log. The coin that discharged those debts came out of Baron Wexler's treasury under his own seal. Not laundered through a house. Direct."

The room went quiet in the specific way rooms go quiet when something just became harder to argue with.

"Timed," Ferra added, "to within a week of the ambush on the road into Kaldo."

Wexler's composure didn't break. It stopped moving, which was worse.

Keller studied the pages longer than felt necessary, reading them twice the way a man reads something not because he doubts it but because he wants to be certain how bad it is.

"Baron. Explain the seal."

"I don't control every payment that leaves my treasury down to the coin."

"You control the seal." Keller didn't raise his voice. "That's what a seal is for. Try again."

"Then the pages are forged. His Highness has had my ledgers in his possession for weeks with no oversight of any kind, and he arrived in my barony carrying a grievance."

"The pages are yours," Ferra said. "Both hands are yours. The one underneath and the one that came back over it."

"And the grievance." Keller turned. "Your Highness."

"I have one." Emeric didn't dress it up. "I'd have brought this to you whether or not I did. You don't need my grievance, and you don't need my word. You've got the barony's own accounting disagreeing with itself in the baron's own hand, and a coin trail that ends in his treasury. Rule on that. Not on me."

Keller looked at him a moment longer than the answer required, then went back to the pages. Wexler tried the forgery line twice more from different angles. It didn't hold any better the second time or the third, and Emeric watched something happen he hadn't expected to enjoy and briefly did anyway: a man's story failing to survive contact with a process that didn't care whose title was in the room. Wexler had run to the crown believing procedure would shelter him. Procedure was the thing taking him apart.

Keller set the pages down.

"I have what I need." He looked at Wexler without anything that could be called sympathy or its opposite. "The judiciary finds sufficient evidence of treasonous conspiracy, specifically the financing of an attempt on the life of a prince of the blood. Sentence is death, to be carried out by crown order. Not baronial discretion. Not personal grievance."

A glance at Emeric, brief, close to a warning.

"This is the crown's judgment. Not yours. Remember the difference."

"I will," Emeric said, and meant it more than Keller probably expected.

There was no scene after that. No dramatic removal, no last words worth writing down. Wexler was escorted out by men who did this sort of thing without needing to feel anything about it, and the chamber emptied the way rooms empty once the actual work is finished. Quietly. Everyone already thinking about the next thing.

Ward found him before he left the capital. He didn't ask how it felt.

"Cobb's still out there."

"He is." Emeric put the hearing away, filed next to the other things he'd stopped expecting to resolve cleanly. "That's a thread for later. Not this one."

Ward didn't press. But he didn't leave either, and after a moment he said the part that had actually brought him. "Wexler kept knights. A garrison. Whatever he was, he paid armed men to answer to him, and now there's no him for them to answer to."

Emeric had known that in the abstract, the way he'd known the fields and the mine were his now. Hearing it said out loud gave it a weight the abstract hadn't. A body of trained men, loyalty unknown, some of them almost certainly bought, all of them suddenly looking up the chain and finding him at the top of it.

"How many?"

"Enough to matter. Not enough to feel safe about." Ward's jaw did the thing it did when he was choosing not to say more than he could back up. "I'd want to know which of them were soldiers and which of them were Wexler's friends before I put a blade in any of their hands and told them to stand between us and anything."

"After the farm," Emeric said, and heard how easily he'd said it, the way he set down every problem that couldn't feed the town this season. "The farm eats first. The garrison keeps."

Ward accepted that the way he accepted most of Emeric's ordering of the world, which was to say he filed his disagreement somewhere Emeric would find it later if he was wrong.

The mage tower hadn't changed. Emeric stood at the base of it longer than he meant to, looking up at the same climb he half remembered, before he made himself go up. Armand looked older than the memory. The memory was fifteen years out of date, so that was hardly fair to either of them. He came down when word reached him that a prince was asking at the gate, and whatever he'd braced for, it took him a moment to square it with the man standing in the courtyard.

"Your Highness." Then, quieter, dropping the formality like it had never quite fit him. "Emeric."

"I need you to see something."

No greeting, no easing into it. Emeric heard himself do it and didn't correct course.

"Not hear about it. See it. Better, put your hands on it."

Armand's eyebrows went up a fraction, the specific look of a man who'd spent decades being difficult to surprise. "That's not how you used to talk to me."

"I've made a discovery." He left it there on purpose. "Crystal work. The kind that changes what you think a crystal is, not just what you think it can do. I'm not going to explain it standing in a courtyard. You'd have to be there for it to mean anything."

Something moved behind Armand's expression. Old instinct recognizing itself. A teacher hearing a student describe something worth walking toward instead of something he'd been assigned.

"I have obligations here," Armand said. "The tower doesn't run itself, and I've spent longer building what runs it than I've got left to spend rebuilding it if I walk away carelessly."

"That's not a no."

"It isn't." His mouth moved, not quite a smile. Closer to a man recalculating a decision he'd already half made. "Settle what needs settling there first. Then, best believe I'll be there."

Emeric nodded once and didn't push. He'd learned that much, at least. Some things go worse the harder you press them. He looked up one more time before he left. The same floating gallery he'd watched them pass hand to hand as a boy, initiates still holding it aloft on nothing but discipline and cost. He knew now exactly what that discipline was worth, and exactly how little of it the job actually required. He didn't say so. Some things you let a man arrive at on his own.

They rode back to Kaldo without much said. When they reached it, it was a barony without a baron for the first time in longer than anyone there could remember. The fields. The unassigned labor. The administrative knots that had existed for no reason except that Wexler had wanted them to. The garrison, too, quartered in the castle's lower ward, drilling out of habit because no one had told them to stop and no one had told them why they should keep going. All of it sitting open now, waiting on someone to decide what came next.

Imani had asked him, the night before he rode out, whether the Circle ever actually sent anyone. He'd told her he'd find out. He had an answer now, of a sort, and it was coming on a schedule an old man hadn't committed to yet. Emeric found out three days later, secondhand, the way most executions probably reach most people who aren't standing close enough to make it personal. A line in a crown dispatch. Sentence carried out. Nothing more. He read it twice anyway. Habit now.

It should have felt like something closing. It didn't. Wexler was dead, and the coin trail and the route and the timing had all held up exactly the way Ferra built them to hold up. Clean. Provable. Undeniable even to a man as procedurally cold as Keller. Emeric had done everything right, and the system had worked exactly as designed. Tam's name was still sitting where he'd left it, and no ruling anywhere had touched it. Nobody had asked about the guard on the duty roster. Nobody could prove Wexler had ordered that specifically, only that he'd paid for the attempt in general, and general was enough for a treason charge and nowhere near enough for the thing Emeric had actually wanted answered.

He'd gotten a win. He turned the word over the way he turned the crystal over, without fully trusting either of them anymore. It didn't feel like one. He wasn't sure it was supposed to. He already knew what came next. He just hadn't let himself think about the farm until now, because thinking about the farm meant admitting the other thing was as finished as it was ever going to get. And under the farm, quieter, a second thing he'd promised Ward he'd get to. A garrison of men he did not yet know how to trust, sitting in his lower ward with nothing to do but wait to find out what kind of lord had inherited them.
