Book 2, Chapter 32: The Seat

The watch brought Ushar up out of the dark at the first bell of the Friday sitting, and Emeric, standing where guests stood with the sea pressing on his glass, watched him come in and thought that thirteen days in a closed bell hadn't bent him.

He came in his own shape. Not on legs; the watch hadn't given him legs to walk on. He came between two officers in the tail he'd been born with, long and dark and heavy through the body, and on the tail, below the hips, a band of water held hard as glass that ran from it to the hand of a third officer behind, a lean young water mage with his eyes half shut and his whole attention on the band. A held-water cuff. Emeric had never seen one. It was the watch's bond for a prisoner of the sea: you couldn't chain a thing that could boil, so you held the water round his tail with your own and let him feel it.

They brought him to the floor below the seat and held him there, a little above the polished stone, so that he had to look up.

The seat was empty. Ilenne hadn't come in.

Ushar looked up at it. Emeric watched his face. It was a big face, strong-boned, handsome in the heavy way of the deep houses, and it had the bell's dark on it still, a grey sheen like a fish kept too long out of the light; but his eyes went round the hall like a man's in a room he means to own. The benches. The Keepers' wall. Sevrin, on the third tier, in his place beside the empty one. The land king in his coat on the left of the floor, with his wives on either side and the old Keeper behind. Ushar's eyes rested on Emeric a moment, without interest, and moved on.

Nobody had told him anything. Ferra had had that from Taurre's officer that morning and put it in Emeric's glass on the stair: the watch had given him food and water and the court's summons and nothing else, by the queen's daughter's asking, on the watch's own authority to tell a held man only what the court allowed. He didn't know who'd lived. He didn't know what had been said in this hall yesterday. He knew the bell had broken, and that his men had come up and said a beast; and he'd had thirteen days alone in the dark to decide what that meant.

The door behind the seat opened, and Ilenne came in alone.

Emeric saw Ushar see her. Alone, in the long plain grey, walking slowly as everyone walked, with nobody behind her. No husband. And his colour came up before he could stop it, across his whole broad chest and up his throat, a warm bright gold, the colour Emeric had seen on lords when the court voted for them; and every person on every bench saw it, and saw him try to pull it down, and saw that he couldn't, not all of it, not fast enough.

He was glad. In front of the whole court, he was glad.

And then she went up the step and sat on the seat.

The gold on Ushar's throat stopped where it was. It didn't go. It stopped, as a man stops on a stair when the step under him isn't where he thought it would be.

The watch officer at Emeric's shoulder said, very low, a hand's width from his glass, "He didn't know."

The far doors opened.



Zale came in the length of the hall.

He came slowly, because in the queen's hall everyone walked, and because he was two weeks into a change that had come on him drowning and not yet healed, with Imani's strapping under his shirt; and he walked the whole way, from the far doors past every bench, every tier of the great houses and the old families, past the Keepers' wall, down the long polished floor between them, on his own two legs.

No helm. No suit. He'd come down the stages in a shirt and his sea trousers and nothing else, and he breathed the sea. Emeric could see it from where he stood: the four lines open under each ear and working, slowly, as he walked; the webbing dark between his fingers; the fine grey plates come up along his forearms and the back of his neck above the collar. His hair lifted round his head in the water like every other head in the hall. He looked, in that light, like one of them, and like nobody in the room.

The hall watched him come. Emeric felt it watch, as he'd felt it hear the times on the Saturday after the fire: a pressure on his skin through the suit, a hundred people seeing a dead man walk past them and holding their colour so hard it hurt.

And on the seat, Ilenne's colour went.

It came up her throat from the collar of the grey coat, blue, deep, the deep-water blue he'd seen come up on her at his brother a handful of times since the spring and be put away again every time, and it climbed into her face, slowly, and stayed. In front of every house in Aumere. She didn't try to hold it. She sat on the seat with her hands on its arms and her colour gone blue to the eyes and watched her husband walk up the hall toward her, and let them all see.

Toward Ushar she showed nothing at all.

Zale reached the step. He didn't look at Ushar, held on the floor beside him. He went up the step of black rock, slowly, with his hand pressed to his side, and sat down on the step at the foot of the seat, at her right hand, where a consort sat; and settled himself; and looked out at the hall.

Emeric couldn't see his face from the floor. He could see Ushar's.



She questioned him without colour.

Emeric had been told what that meant, a long time ago, in Kaldo, from Ilenne herself across a table with Ferra's forty courtesies cut down to seven: never speak without colour. A colourless sentence was what a thing in the deep said before it ate. And she spoke to Ushar for a quarter of an hour, from the seat, in a voice with nothing in it and a throat as blank as a slate, as a thing in the deep is spoken to; and the watch officer put it into Emeric's glass a sentence behind, flatly, and once stopped and had to start again.

She told him what had been said in that hall the day before. All of it. The custom, named. The finding. That widow-making was a crime from that sitting on, and the attempts on the husband were his house's, under the watch's seal. That the regency was laid down. That she was queen.

She asked him whether he'd confess.

He wouldn't. He said so, in his own colour, which had gone from the stopped gold to a hard dark bronze while she spoke, and he said it well; he'd been a great house's heir all his life and he knew how to stand in that hall. He said a steward of his house had done murder in its name, and the court had found it, and his house would answer. He said he'd been in a closed bell for thirteen days on the word of that steward. He said he'd done nothing.

And all the time he said it, Emeric watched his eyes go to the step. To the land man sitting on it at the queen's right hand, breathing the sea through his neck, with his elbows on his knees, looking at him.

Zale didn't say anything. He didn't do anything. He sat there.

It was the sitting there that did it. Emeric could see it working in Ushar like heat in a bar. Thirteen days in the dark told nothing, and he'd come up out of it into one minute: the heir was alone and he was glad, and the heir was queen, and the husband walked in. And then she'd spoken to him as you speak to a thing in the deep, and the custom he'd lived by had a name, a crime's, and the husband sat on the queen's step a few yards above him with his hands hanging, looking at him, the way a man looks at a fish he's decided not to eat yet.

Patience was for a game you could still win. Emeric understood that as he watched it go out of Ushar. Ushar had been patient for years. He'd been patient at the door when the binding was made, and patient through every attempt, and patient in the dark. There was no game now. There was nothing left to be patient for.

His tail came in.

That was all. A half-second. The long heavy tail, held above the floor in the band of hard water, drew up under him and in, as a merman's does before he goes, and the young mage behind him opened his eyes very wide.

Ushar broke the bond.

The band of held water went. It didn't snap. It went soft, all at once, like ice going to slush in a hand, because the man inside it was stronger than the man holding it and had stopped pretending not to be; and Ushar boiled.

Up. From the floor, straight up at the seat, in the queen's hall where nobody swam, tail in, the water tearing white round him in a sheath, with the kettle-scream of it filling the hall, at the speed Imani had told him about, Ilenne's, in the market, after a thing two hundred yards off. There wasn't a hundred yards. There were five. Ilenne was on the seat with her hands on its arms.

Zale was already up.

Emeric never saw him stand. He saw him on the step, and then not on the step, between the seat and the white tear coming at it, his right arm out; and the arm had changed. From the shoulder down. The webbing and the plates were gone into something harder, dark and close-scaled to the fingertips, and the fingers ended in claws, and Zale drew them through the water in front of him, in one line, low to high, the way a man draws a line in sand with a stick.

The water along the line went hard.

He saw that. Through his glass, with his sense deaf and the hall a blur of colour, he saw it as you see a pane of glass when the light catches its edge: a thin plane in the water where the claws had gone, harder than the water round it, thinner than a knife, and moving with the hand. It went through the boil. It went through Ushar at the neck.

The boil stopped.

It stopped all at once, a kettle taken off the fire, the scream going out of the hall and the white going out of the water; and what had been in it went on up, slowly, the last of its speed, and turned over, and the head went one way and the body another.

Zale moved his hand once more. Not the claws; the flat of it, a slow wave, out to the side, as a man waves away smoke. And the dark cloud that had come out of Ushar's neck in the water went with it, away from the seat, away from Ilenne, out over the floor toward the far doors, thinning, as if the water itself had taken it and carried it off.

The queen's guards had their bidents up. Four of them, from the door behind the seat, too late, and they knew it. Two of them took the body. Two the head. They held them, in the water, and turned their faces up to the seat, and waited.

The Rim's house, on the third tier, didn't move. Not one of them. Sevrin sat with his hands on his knees and his colour gone to nothing at all.

Zale stood on the step a moment longer with his arm out, between the seat and the floor, as if there might be more.

Ilenne said one word to him. Without colour, as she'd spoken to Ushar; the watch officer put it over flatly.

"Sit."

The arm went back, from the claws up, scale to plate to skin. He looked at it, and let it fall, and sat down again on the step at the foot of the seat, at her right hand, with his elbows on his knees, as he'd sat before; and waited for her next word, like the guards.



Emeric hadn't moved.

He'd felt himself want to. He'd felt the whole of himself go, in the half-second when the tail came in, as it went at home when a cart went over in the street; and he'd held it, because it wasn't his court and wasn't his water and wasn't his to stop, and if he'd moved one foot off the guests' stone in that hall he'd have been a land king boiling at a sea queen's seat. He hadn't moved. He stood where guests stood with his hands at his sides, and the watch officer beside him had his hand on Emeric's arm, and Emeric didn't know when he'd put it there.

He was looking at the line.

Not the head. Everybody else in the hall was looking at the head, or the body, or the queen. He was looking at the place in the water in front of the step where the line had been, and wasn't now, and couldn't be seen at all. Water. Made hard, and thin, so thin that it had parted a man the way a wire parts cheese, with nothing in it doing the parting but how thin it was. Zale hadn't thrown anything. He'd drawn it, and it had been there, and then it hadn't.

He'd think about that. He knew it standing there. He'd think about it again, a long way from here.

And under that, the other thing. His brother, on the step. His brother, who'd crawled into the sea at Emberfell before he could walk, who'd been a sailor and a joke and a man who'd turned down Emberfell for a boat, and who had just stood between a queen and a boiling lord and taken his head off in her hall in front of every house in the sea, with one hand, without a sound, and sat back down. Emeric was afraid of him. For the first time in his life he was afraid of Zale, and in the same breath he was so proud of him that it hurt in his throat, and he kept both of them off his face, behind his glass, because nobody in that hall could be allowed to see the land king feel anything at all about what had just happened in it.

Beside him Ferra had her slate out. She was writing. He saw what she wrote, over her shoulder, in Kaldo capitals: the bell of the sitting, and the time by her watch, to the minute.



Ilenne gave her word without colour.

The body home, in its bond. That was all. The watch to take it to its house, as the law gave a prisoner's body to his house, with the watch's band on it still, so that it went home a held man. The guards bowed and went with it.

And then her colour came back.

Not blue. The ordinary pale grey-white of her in good light, with the faint sheen on it, coming up at the throat and through her face, slowly. She sat on the seat with her colour back and looked out at the hall, and asked, in a voice with colour in it again, whether anyone of the house of the Rim would speak to the charges.

Taurre had stood up from his stool. He said something first, short, and the officer put it over: that the bond had been the watch's, and the watch's water mage had held it, and the prisoner had been stronger than the man who held it, and that failure was the watch's and his to answer for, and he'd answer for it in the record that afternoon, in full. He sat down. Nobody spoke against it.

Then Sevrin stood up.

He'd run the house's defence the day before and lost it in front of everyone. Emeric had watched him lose it. He'd watched him stand there with his grief colour on and offer the shallows money and an enemy in one breath, and nearly get them; and then watched Ilenne's one line and the taster's word take it away from him piece by piece. He was young. He'd sat in his cousin's empty place for thirteen days speaking for a man who'd just boiled at the queen in her hall, and the man was in two pieces going out of the far doors in the watch's hands, and Sevrin was the house now.

He stood below the seat, on the floor, where the asked stood, and looked up.

He confessed for it. He did it well, which Emeric hadn't expected; he did it the way the old families' head had stood up the day before, plainly, without a speech, and named who'd known. His cousin. Maudrec, who was already held. Two of the house's own men of business, by name. A cousin's wife who'd kept the house's table and sent the taster. And a woman of the queen's own house: a servant, who'd kept the door of the bell in the queen's house since before the binding, and who'd been bought through Maudrec in the spring, and who'd let the house's man in through the bell's water door on the night the husband's suit hung there, and set the sliver herself.

The hall took that one hard. Emeric felt it go through the water. A servant of the queen's house. In the queen's own bell.

Ferra, beside him, had stopped writing. She was looking at the slate in her hand, and he knew what she was thinking about, because he'd seen the sheet: a line, near the top, the sliver under the strap, and in the last column, all week, one word. Blank.

She wrote something on the slate, small, and he read it.

Hand: named. Sevrin, Fri 19 Jun.



And then Zale got up again.

Emeric saw the blood first. Zale had his hand to his side as he stood, and when he took it away the hand was dark, and the shirt under it was dark, a slow cloud going off it into the water; the boil, or the reach, or the line, had torn Imani's stitches open, and he was bleeding into the queen's hall. He didn't look at it. He stood on the step at the foot of the seat and turned round and looked at his wife on it.

Then he bent down, and put his arms under her, and lifted her out of the seat.

The hall made the sound a hall of merfolk makes, which is no sound, and every colour in it at once.

They went, every bench, every tier, the whole long hall from the seat to the far doors, going through colours like a field of lamps somebody had kicked: gold and slate and dark and a hard bright green he'd never seen and the deference colour going pale up the throats of the queen's guards at the door. And in the middle of it his brother sat down on the queen's seat of Aumere with the queen in his arms, and settled her across his lap, sideways, the way a man settles a woman on a bench in a tavern when there aren't enough chairs; and leaned back.

Ilenne didn't move. She sat across his lap with her hands in her own lap and her colour doing absolutely nothing.

An old lord stood up on the second tier. Not the heavy one. The lean old one, the one who always asked, who'd asked about the land king's brother and hadn't asked anything yesterday; and he spoke, carefully, courteously. The watch officer put every word of what followed into Emeric's glass a sentence behind, flatly, and afterwards Emeric remembered only the words and whose they'd been.

"Your Majesty... that is the queen's throne."

Zale looked at him.

"Yes," said Zale.

A pause. Emeric watched it in his brother's shoulders.

"She's sitting on it."

The old lord stood there.

Ilenne's hand had come up out of her lap and found Zale's side, the bad side, the dark wet place on his shirt, and lay flat over it, and pressed. Emeric saw Zale's face go grey and stay pleasant. Ilenne didn't look at him. She looked at the old lord, from her husband's lap, from the seat, with her colour perfectly courteous.

"Was there something you wished to bring before the crown?" said Ilenne.

The old lord's colour went, slowly, the pale of deference, from the throat up.

"No, Your Majesty."

"Then sit."

He sat.

The hall settled, slowly, the colours going down bench by bench. Ilenne sat across her husband's lap on the seat with her hand pressed flat over his ribs, and looked out at her court; and then, not turning her head, not looking at him at all, she said something very short to her husband, and the watch officer at Emeric's glass hesitated before he put it over.

"You are bleeding on my throne."

"Am I?"

"Yes."

"Should I move?"

"Not until I've finished."

Beside him, behind her glass, Imani had shut her eyes.



The court did the rest of it in the afternoon, without hurrying, and Emeric stood where guests stood and watched it done.

Taurre answered for the bond first, in the record, as he'd said he would: the watch's water mage named, the watch's rule for a prisoner of the sea read aloud, the plain fact that it hadn't been enough for that prisoner, and Taurre's own name under all of it. He asked the court to enter it against the watch. The court entered it. Then he read the finding on the death, from his slate, flatly. A prisoner had broken the watch's bond in the queen's hall and boiled at the queen on her seat, which was an attack, and the king had stopped it there, in the hall, as the old killing rules gave him to. The court entered that too.

The king. Emeric heard the officer say it and didn't let himself look at the step.

Then the house. Struck from the court's count, its seats gone, its voice gone. The family to keep its lives, and nothing else. Its holdings to the crown, under the crown's seal: the furnaces and the glassworks, to be run by their own stewards under the certifying bench; the certifying bench itself, which the house had run since before anyone could remember; the pilots' concession, which the court gave to the crown under Taurre's published rate book, with second pilots licensed beside every first, and loss of bond and office for any pilot who wrote a false route; the dwarven iron trade, to be brokered by the crown. Those Sevrin had named as knowing lost their places. The workers who hadn't known kept theirs. The furnace hands kept their work. The pilots kept piloting, for the crown's fee now.

Maudrec and Keddrin, under Aumere's own law. Emeric didn't follow what the sentences were; the officer gave him the words and they were the court's, not Kaldo's, and he didn't ask, and nobody asked Kaldo. Lusse, who'd carried for the house and been drained by it and tied under a quay, was released to the watch's bond for his testimony. Oddrin lost his licence for a season, for selling a posted hazard without reporting the raising; his tally stood as evidence.

And the house's papers, which the watch had taken out of its records that morning while the court sat, were read into the record one bundle at a time.

The officer stopped, partway through the third bundle, and looked at Emeric through the glass.

"Drawings," he said. "Of your gun."

They were a smith's. A Rim smith of the house's, the officer said when Taurre read the name, who'd had the rifle on his bench for a week in May before it went to the roof; and he'd taken it apart and measured it, every pin and spring of the action, and drawn it, and weighed its rounds, and set the weights down beside the drawings in a neat column. Taurre read the list. Then he read the smith's own tally of his sheets, which was in the bundle with them, and stopped, and read it again.

"One tracing," said the officer, "is missing. His tally says nine sheets. There are eight."

Emeric stood very still.

The fresh marks on the pins. Bram had seen them on the morning of the kelp racks and said somebody had had it apart to see how it was made. Somebody had. And a sheet with part of that seeing on it wasn't in the bundle, and wasn't anywhere the watch had found.

The court gave Kaldo the eight. Emeric took them from Taurre's officer at the end of the sitting in a sealed oilcloth, and held them, and thought about what was in them. A smith of the Rim couldn't make the rifle's steel. He couldn't make its carbide core or its rounds. There'd be no working copy of that gun on the Rim this year or the next. But somebody now knew how its action went together, and the idea of it was out past the wall in a drawing, and one drawing of it was gone.

He gave the oilcloth to Ferra. She looked at it, and at him, and put it in her bag without a word.



Ward came down on the Saturday, for the first time.

Ferra watched him come into the hall from the left side of the floor, where she stood with Imani and Emeric and the watch officer, and she'd never seen him look as uncomfortable in her life. He'd come in from the Grey Mark on the watch's boat at first light and gone down the stages behind a pilot in a Mark II that hull four had let out across the shoulders for him the night before, with the king's coat's plainer cousin over it, braced; and he walked into the queen's hall with the sea pressing on him like a man walking into a room where everybody knows something about him, which was exactly what it was.

He went to the floor below the seat and stood there, as the asked stood, and looked up.

The queen was on the seat. Zale wasn't on the step. Imani had seen to that the night before, on his ship, with a needle and a face Ferra had last seen on her at the hatch, and he was on his own ship at the gate today under orders, as far as Ferra knew, not to move his arms.

The court judged the deaths first.

Ferra had known it would. The killing rules, which she'd had read to her in the first week and written down, said the host's law judged every death in the host's water after the fact, and every death in Aumere since the fleet had come was on Taurre's slate. The market: a knife for hire, Orsaule, killed with the queen's daughter's bident, which the court found was a man killed in the act of trying to kill, and entered, and moved on. The hatch: two men with fishing spears at the top of the yard stair in the smoke, one dead and one wounded, by the land's two women. The bell: two of the house's men, dead in the queen's house, by the husband. The kelp racks: Pirro, a mage of the Rim, dead, by the land's knight, Mira, whose account the watch had taken on the Rim since she couldn't come down and who stood now on the record in her own words.

And the man at the hatch had a name.

Taurre read it from his slate. The watch had had it since the Wednesday, from Keddrin, who'd been hired with him and had given it up when he was asked. The officer put it into Ferra's glass.

"Davrel," he said.

She hadn't known it. She'd shot him three times at the top of a stair in a dead zone with Imani below her and fourteen people behind the hatch, and he'd died at her feet with his spear still in his hand and his face surprised, and for two weeks he'd been a line in her book that said the man at the hatch. She wrote it on her slate. Davrel. And beside it, after a moment: family? Tonight it would go into the book, on the page under Nimo's.

Imani, beside her, saw her write it. She didn't say anything.

Then the court asked them.

The asked stood. They walked out onto the floor below the seat and stood beside Ward, the two of them, and looked up at the queen, and the queen looked down at them with her colour level and courteous and nothing else, as a queen looks at people her court has asked a question.

The question was the same for both, in the old form: what had they done, and would they say it wet.

Ferra took her helm off.

She'd thought about it all night. Emeric had done it, at the floor, after the fire, and she'd heard from him exactly what it was like, the cold, the salt, the sea in your ears and on your eyes and in your mouth, the breath held; and she'd thought, on the stair coming down, that if she was going to say a thing for ever she'd say it in the water and not through a slate. So she undid the clips and took the helm off and held it under her arm and the sea came onto her face. Cold. Salt. She braced, and held her breath, and opened her eyes in it, and the hall was a blur of lamps and colours.

She said it in Kaldo, slowly, through a mouthful of the sea, and felt every word go up past her face.

"I fired three rounds. At a man with a spear who was coming at me through a hatch with people behind it." A breath, let out a little at a time, to carry the words. "I'd fire them again."

She put the helm back on. Her hands were steady on the clips. The officer did the second one, as he'd done Emeric's. She purged, and breathed, and the water ran out of her ears, and through the glass she heard him putting it into their tongue for her, word for word.

Three rounds. That she'd fire them again. True for ever.

The court entered it and moved on.

Imani took her helm off too. Ferra hadn't known she would. She did it faster than Ferra had, and said it shorter: that she'd shot a man in the hip at the hatch, twice, who was coming up the stair with a spear; and then knelt by him in the zone with her healing gone and kept him alive with her hands until he could be carried out. She put the helm back on.

A deep lord on the right side stood up. Not the heavy one; a younger one, thin, with a sharp curious colour, and he asked her why. Why shoot a man and then keep him alive. The officer put it into Imani's glass.

Imani didn't take the helm off for that. She answered it on the slate, in capitals, and turned it to the officer, and he read it into the water for her.

"She says," said the officer, "that is the other half of what she's for."

The thin lord stood a moment, and then let his colour go, very slightly, warm, and sat.



Then Kaldo's terms. And the queen ruled every one of them herself.

Ferra stood on the floor with her slate and wrote them as they came. She'd known for a week what they'd be, more or less; she'd sat in the hard room with Emeric and drawn up the list of what a court like this would have to do to a guest whose ship had broken its markers and whose stone had come in, and most of it was on her list, and some of it was worse.

Ward's crossing. Entered as what it was: a rescue, and a breach. The queen named Esseth, and the skiff, and the woman in the water under the land's ship's wind, and said it was harm done in her water by the land and the court would hold it so. And she said, in the same breath, that there was no charge against the captain of the ship. He had asked the watch twice and not waited, and he had come for people on a stair in the smoke.

Ward stood on the floor below her and heard it.

And then the breach. The queen didn't put it on Ward. She put it where it had been put already, in that hall, wet, on the Saturday after the fire: on the land king, by his own word. If my ship came past your markers last night, it was my word she broke. Entered under his name. The officer put it over flatly, and Ferra put it on her slate, and looked up, and saw Ward's jaw.

It set. She watched it set, behind his glass, and stay set. He'd told Emeric on the skiff hands' row that he'd do it again and that the word had been a mistake, and he'd meant both; and now he stood on a queen's floor and heard the court give the whole of his crossing to the man who'd given the word, and keep none of it for the man who'd done the crossing. He didn't move. He didn't look round at Emeric. He stood there with his jaw set and let it be entered.

The K Amara out of Aumere's water. At once. Beyond the fetch line, and escort three with her. The stone in her iron room barred from Aumere's water, on any hull, without the certifying bench's inspection; and the bench was the crown's now. Any land hull coming back into Aumere's water, by the crown's permit and with the watch's escort, and not otherwise.

Restitution. To Nimo's family for the boy's ribs, and to Esseth's for the skiff and the days and the water; the two the land had harmed. The court entered what had already been paid from the expedition's chest, and Ferra heard Taurre read her own figures out of her own book back to her from his slate, which she'd sent him on the Monday; and then the court set what remained. She did the sum against it on her slate while the officer was still putting it over, and it was short. A little. The court's figure for the skiff's gear was a little higher than hers had been on the doorstep, which was fair, because she'd paid what Esseth would take on a doorstep and the court had priced what the gear was worth. She wrote the difference and the reason under it, and at the next pause she asked the officer to tell the watch that Kaldo would pay it now, before the court rose, and the watch's clerk came across the floor with his slate and she counted it out of the chest's iron in the water, bar by bar, with the officer watching, and the clerk entered it.

And the Challenger.

The queen gave her reasons for that one, because the court would want them. The house had argued, in the dry hall, that the land's diving ship had hastened the seal's failure. The house was struck; there was nobody to make the argument now. Taurre's finding stood as he'd written it: the Challenger hadn't started the failure, and the stones couldn't say whether she'd hastened it. That was a question the court couldn't answer. And a hull the court couldn't clear of a dome's failure didn't berth beside the domes. The Challenger's berth at the Rim was refused.

She'd guessed that one. She'd hoped not to be right.

And then Ressar came off the Keepers' wall, and said the last thing, because nobody else in the hall could. The house of the Rim had made a charge against the land in the dry hall at the start of all this, under the dragons' Leaving Terms. The house was struck, so the charge lapsed with it; it had no mover. But it hadn't been answered. Nobody in this hall could answer it. The text of the Terms wasn't in Aumere; it was at the elves' ledger-house, and one of the winged ones had carried word of the charge south in May.

"The charge is not proven," said the officer, for Ressar. "It is not answered. It has gone ahead of you."

The house's other business went the same way. Ushar's motion to set the binding aside, deferred to the season in the dry hall: no mover, and the binding stood as made. Ferra wrote that line with a small hard stroke under it.

The last thing before the court rose was the rifle. Taurre's officer brought it across the floor in its case, the eight rounds in their box beside it, and the queen gave it back to the land as the land's declared property, and the watch kept the two casings on its record. Ferra signed for it on the clerk's slate, in the water, and wrote under her name, from memory, the line in her own book from the first week: declared to the watch's hazard log, Wed 6 May, with position and mark.



She asked after Davrel's people before they went up. She asked the watch officer at the foot of the stages, who looked at his slate and said the watch had a name and no house. She asked again at the pilots' hall on the Rim, through Linnet, of three pilots and a porter who knew everybody. Nobody would say. One of the pilots looked at her for a while and then looked away, which she wrote down too.



They walked back along the Rim to the hard rooms at dusk, the two of them, with Imani and Ward a little ahead.

Ferra had her book under her arm. She'd read the terms over on the stages coming up, all of them, and she'd read the list she'd made in the hard room a week before beside them, and there wasn't a line on either that she'd want to change.

"The house's version's still standing," she said. "In every hall Taurre's seal doesn't reach. A land gun and a land stone and a land king who came to the Rim and a great house fell. That's what they'll be saying at the other seas by the autumn." She shifted the book. "It cost us the berth. It'll cost us more than that before we're home."

"I know."

"If we'd brought it ourselves. The ledger. The rifle. Laid it in front of the court with your name on it." She didn't look at him. "It would've been faster."

"The old families could only leave the house," said Emeric, "because it was Aumere's case. Not mine. If it'd had my name on it, Sevrin's offer would've held. A land king's evidence against a great house of the Rim, and the land's stone in the water. They'd have gone with him."

"I know," said Ferra.

They walked. The Rim's lamps were coming up along the breakwater, one by one.

Neither of them said the other had been wrong.



Mira was sitting on the hard rooms' step in the last of the light with her rifle across her knees, and Ferra told her the whole of it, both days, from Ushar coming up out of the dark to the rifle across the floor, because Mira had asked to be told everything and Ferra had promised she would be.

Mira listened without interrupting. When Ferra got to the line in the water, she leaned forward a little, and stayed leaned forward until Ferra had finished.

"What did you think of it?" said Ferra. "The blade."

Mira thought about it. She looked out at the gate, where Zale's ship lay against the steps with a lamp lit in her after cabin.

"I'd have needed three rounds," she said.



Imani had been furious on the Friday night. Ferra had heard her through the after cabin's door from the top of the ladder: not loud, Imani was never loud, but going on for a long time in the voice she used on the hospital deck when somebody had taken a bandage off to look under it. The stitches. The water. The boil. The reach, with that arm, with those ribs. The lifting. The lifting. A queen, out of a seat, with a hole in his side, in front of the whole court, so that he could sit on a chair.

Zale had submitted to all of it. He'd lain on his bunk and been sewn up again and told, and said yes, and said he knew, and said he was very sorry, and had looked, Imani said afterwards in the hard room, more pleased with himself than any man she'd ever stitched.

"It doesn't help," she said. "It doesn't help at all."

And Ilenne, she said, had come up the stages at midnight and gone down into the cabin and shut the door, and been in there an hour, and when she came out her colour had been perfectly level and Zale's face had been the colour of a dish of porridge. Imani didn't know what had been said. She thought it had been worse than hers.

"Good," she said.



Ferra wrote the line that night at the outer room's table, under Saturday's date, as she'd written one at the end of every hard week since April, so that anyone who opened the book after her would know where everything stood without reading the rest.

Off the board: Zale. Wounds: Zale's side, reopened Fri, closed again Fri night. Rounds: none fired. Stones: the stone barred from Aumere's water without the bench. Fuel: the K Amara's burn, logged. Hulls: the K Amara and escort three out of Aumere's water, beyond the fetch line, from today. Promises: the breach, entered under E.'s name. Restitution: paid, in full, before the court rose.

She looked at it. Then, under it, in her smallest hand, because it belonged in the book and nowhere else:

Davrel. Family not found.
