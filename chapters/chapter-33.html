Book 3, Batch 14: The Shortest Line

Chapters 137–159 | Monday 1 December, Year 22 - Sunday 14 November, Year 23

When the Gathering ended, Kaldo had shown eight peoples and every crown on the continent what it had built, and its institutions had held in front of guests who owed it nothing. An inquiry with a dwarven engineer on it was looking into why her people's stones went dark. The pope had called the visit a breach, and the emperor had answered with his own law. The papers for the Year 23 budget sat on Ferra's desk, and the sheet on slowing the groves was on top.

This batch covers most of a year again. It is the year when nothing forces anyone's hand. The bodies Emeric built or joined just do their work, and much of that work goes against him. The Assembly slows his groves. An inquiry won't name a cause he can guess. A table of foreign crowns throws out his draft and rewrites it without him. An inspector holds his railway for a week, an ally won't sell him a core, and a dragon won't carry his seeds. He keeps every rule he signed. The one time he wants to fly to someone else's fight, nobody has asked him, and he stays home. Two weeks later he goes past the wall to the edge of the elves' forest, where Kaldo stands under arrows and fires nothing, and where he is afraid for his wives before anyone has raised a bow.

Chapter 137: A Step Down

On Friday 12 December, through Ivo Thorne's eyes, the December freight hull lands at the Sky Yard with the Long Watch's mail. In a padded case is the First Glass's first plate, with the keeper Junia Bask's one line on its sleeve: the field, the date, the hours of exposure. Ivo prints it in the Polytechnic's optics hall and looks at it longer than he means to. It shows a faint spiral of light with two arms, off-centre, with a scatter of stars in front of it. Emeric comes up when Ivo sends for him and looks for a long time. He knows privately what it is: another island of stars, too far off to count, for which this world has no word yet. Aloud he says only that it is very far away and very big, and that the next plates of the same field will tell them more. Ivo writes "spiral, Northern Field 1, object 1" in his book, and that becomes its name. Kaldo News prints it on the front page the next morning, with Junia's line as the caption. At the dock school a boy asks his teacher what it is. The teacher says nobody knows yet, and that's what the glass is for.

In the Sky Yard's sheds the K Sorrel and her sister are in for their winter overhaul, being re-skinned in the merfolk cloth under Saelle's eye. A liner needs no stealth, so she'll wear her livery by day and go dark only if something comes at her. Ivo photographs a panel changing colour for Kaldo News, and the paper prints it in black and white, which misses the point.

At the Sky Yard the freight hull's 40 mm guns now sit on fast-swinging mounts firing tungsten shot, refitted because April's Razorwing showed how fast the fliers turn. On the test stand Sorrel fires her first sounding rocket, burning made kerosene and liquid oxygen with no stone aboard. It climbs about thirty kilometres and comes down in the bay on a parachute. Junia wants its photographs. The sky watch's radar on the Field's tower follows it up and loses it near the top; the bay drum's mirror finds the chute coming down. Emeric watches from the stand and says nothing about going higher.

Through Ferra's eyes, December has already been busy. On the first of the month the state inspectorate's new furnace branch took over the eastern hills' ironworks, and Wystan's foremen gave its first inspector a desk. Commissioners from Randillia and Duvrenne initialled heads of terms for a cross-border rail line at Kesrath. On the March's salt marsh Merryn Pellow's crew killed the nineteenth of the Isle's young beasts in daylight, while the dog carried the case. That leaves two in the wild.

Before the budget the year's count is read: ninety-one adults in a hundred in Kaldo's states can now read, and about four in ten across the kingdom.

On Thursday 18 December the Year 23 budget goes before the Assembly. Ferra lays her sheet before the grove line: eight attacks abroad plotted beside her column for the air, the gaps between them shrinking as the air thickens. Above it she has written one line: this shows the beasts come faster as the air thickens; it doesn't show why. She lays it down and sits. She doesn't argue it. Morwen Thorvey, the member for the Narrow March, whose coast faces the Isle the young came across from, moves to cut the next spring's planting from about four thousand seeds to about three thousand. "I'm not asking for a cause. I'm asking for a year." Ward's evidence carries the motion. He doesn't talk about beasts. He talks about men: the Watch can guard about fifteen hundred new groves next year and no more, and he won't put a pair of seeds in the ground without its guard. Emeric argues the other way. The only people who ever wanted this land thin burned groves to keep it so. The old peoples' groves abroad fruit this year whatever Kaldo does. The beasts woke before Kaldo's first tree bore fruit. And if the Assembly waits for a cause it may wait for years, while the land stays as sick as the Church left it. The cut passes by eleven votes. He asks for one amendment, that the cap be weighed against Ferra's two lines at every budget from now on, up or down. Thorvey seconds it herself, and it passes. He signs the grove line as voted. In the corridor he says to nobody that they've just slowed the cure because the disease got faster. Ward, who heard, says that might be right and the vote still is. Emeric doesn't say he was wrong. Ferra's written veto stays in its drawer. It exists for an oversight like a dead man's hand on the law, and this was a vote.

The budget's last line is a dam. The Headwater dam will stand at the outlet of the mountain lake where the river rises above Wayford, and it goes through with no debate worth recording, because Ferra's quarterly power figure has been negative since the autumn and every member has read it this time. Its lines will run down the valley beside the Capital Line. It won't light before Year 26, and Ferra writes the date down where Emeric will see it.

Chapter 138: The Second Sitting

Through Hulda's eyes, the winter has been an experiment. In November the Sea Hold's dark stones, the ones that weren't sealed as evidence, went home with her on a Kaldo hull. Every dawn a warden lit one on deck. It stayed dark until the morning after the hull crossed the wall's line past the last islands, and then it lit. Coming back to Kaldo in February she did the same thing the other way. It lit every morning until the hull came inside the line, and then it didn't. She logged both against the hull master's positions and had him sign them. On the way in, the hull's set brought news. On Wednesday 28 January, at the Sun River ford, orc wardens and Sabir boatmen killed a young beast in the shallows. As the orcs' roll voted two years ago, they sent its head north in a shut case. On Monday 9 February Tess read the mark grown into its eyes. It pointed at a cradle under the islands off the Sun River's mouth, on the peoples' own side of the sea again.

On Thursday 12 February the inquiry sits for the second time. The sealed copy is opened and resealed in front of her, both seals checked first. She puts her log in. Armand, sitting for the stones, reads the three sealed ones by touch and says what Emeric said in October: full, shut, refusing. The inquiry's finding is read aloud. The refusal is bound to the Sea Hold's people's stones and to Ostrellin's ground. It isn't Kaldo's hall, Kaldo's air or anything Kaldo did. It is consistent with a standing order written into stone, as the king guessed. Who wrote it, when, and where it is kept, the inquiry cannot say without the order itself. It adjourns until the order is found, and Hulda keeps her seat. Afterwards she asks Armand whether anyone in Ostrellin could write an order into a stone that holds for two thousand years. Armand says he knows one man who can do it now, and that man is the one who told her it was a guess. She writes that down too. Neither of them treats it as an accusation.

On Monday 16 February, through Albin Keswick's eyes at the Core's legation, the parish clerks' season ends after thirteen weeks. They didn't relent. From the New Year the Core's high court ordered every birth, sale and will filed twice, once with the parish and once with an imperial register clerk in each district town. By February the parish copy was no longer the only one, so slowing it down stopped hurting anyone, and the clerks went back to their old pace. Keswick enters it in his report as "fixed", with no adjective. For the first time in its history the Core has registers the Church doesn't keep.

The same morning he carries a sealed rescript to Ferra's office himself. The emperor has voided the December rail heads of terms. Two subject crowns may not make agreements on cross-border works without the Core, and the heads never mention the Core. He isn't bluffing, and he's right. The commissioners took the Gathering's promise, that the Kesrath compact would go through imperial ratification, to cover only Kesrath, and nobody in Ferra's office caught it. Ferra reads it twice and agrees before she sends it upstairs. Emeric is annoyed for an hour, mostly with himself, and says so. The Core gets a seat at the rail table, and the rule that every cross-border works agreement goes through an imperial ratification procedure. Under the receipt Keswick adds a sentence of his own: his Majesty reads everything.

Chapter 139: Equal Rights of Entry

Through Alina's eyes, spring opens on Lantern Night in Kesrath's harbour with Waylon. In January she wrote to every crown and every lawful port authority asking them to a Conference of Shared Works. The old peoples were told and not invited, since they have never asked to sit at such a table. The delegations arrive by sea and by Kaldo hull. In the harbour a Saltreach fishing crew is talking about living three days out past the old line from shore, which no ship has done in two thousand years.

The Conference opens on Monday 8 March. Duvrenne hosts, and Alina convenes but doesn't speak for her own country. Randillia sends Emeric, Ferra and Bram. Velmoor sends Countess Henrike Marrant and its treaty lawyer, Lothar Breck. Solreth sends its minister, Teodora Vasse, with Benno Sarrat from the Gathering's pump room. The Core sends its legation's lawyer, Doctor Ansgar Lell, with Princess Celestine beside him as her father's observer for the archive. Lell's first act is to read the rescript's rule into the record: every agreement out of this hall goes through imperial ratification, and he will write the category of routine works himself. Nobody objects.

On Tuesday the table takes up Ferra and Emeric's standards draft. It gives Kaldo's accredited inspectors the right to enter any joint-works site, and any yard that supplies it, in any member's lands. Breck reads the clause aloud twice and asks one question: which yards may a Velmoor inspector enter? The draft lets Velmoor's inspectors check sites on Velmoor's own soil. It says nothing about Kaldo's yards, which make most of the parts. On Wednesday Velmoor moves that the draft be refused, and Duvrenne votes with Velmoor, Alina's hand up first. Solreth joins them. The Core abstains until ratification. The draft is refused.

Through Emeric's eyes, he is furious for about a minute. He wrote the clause so the parts would be safe, and it reads like a master's rule anyway. Waylon catches him in the corridor. "Loyalty isn't obedience. She'd have voted against me too." He's right, and Emeric says so before the corridor ends. At the table Ferra reads her own clause again and sees it before anyone has to show her: they wrote it from the side of the people who built everything. She tells the hall the draft was wrong and why. It was a genuine mistake, and it was both of theirs. The table gives the pen to Breck and Duvrenne's treaty clerks. Kaldo sends its comments in writing like everyone else, and Emeric doesn't ask to be on the drafting panel. The price is real. Any member's inspector may now enter any yard in any member's lands that makes a part for a joint work, and that includes Bram's shop and the Works Town's parts yards. The secret Forge stays out under the published restricted list. That means every joint-works part has to be made in an open yard the ordinary way, never printed, which is slower and about a fifth dearer. The first joint works slip from spring to autumn. Duvrenne pays too: it gives up the favoured access it was promised in October, so that Velmoor and Solreth aren't second-class.

Chapter 140: Turnkey

On Thursday 11 March, through Teodora Vasse's eyes, Emeric offers Solreth's capital a hospital that Kaldo would build, equip, staff and run at cost for ten years. She refuses it. When the ten years end, Solreth would own a building it can't repair and a staff it didn't train. She asks instead for the drawings, Kaldo's instructors for five years, the right to make every part that isn't on the restricted list, and a repair school in Solreth with Kaldo's teachers. Sarrat mentions the zinc collar again, and everyone who was in the pump room knows what he means. Ferra says out loud that it's the better deal before Emeric does. He agrees a sentence later.

On Friday morning Countess Marrant, who heard the breach read at Saint Edwy's, asks Emeric plainly whether a king who can find a man by his wristband from across a kingdom, or that king's successors, could find and watch a political opponent inside Velmoor. He starts to say Kaldo wouldn't, and stops himself. "Trust me isn't an answer." He accepts the limits she lists: complaints, inspection, remedies. Breck turns them into a protocol. Watching is allowed only on targeted grounds, with prior authorization where it can be had and audited exceptions in an emergency. As little is kept for as short a time as possible. Any member can bring a challenge without being shown Kaldo's equipment. That night Ferra writes one line in her book under the heading she keeps for times when it's the king: the relay drums' other eye, the secret camera, is to be kept to the protocol's terms as if Velmoor knew it was there. The eye stays secret, but Kaldo binds it by its own rule anyway. It isn't the whole truth, and Ferra knows it.

On Friday afternoon, through Celestine's eyes, she hands Odile Ashdown the first eleven volumes of the imperial archive's catalogue, her father's seal on each. With them comes the rights line in his hand. The catalogue may be read in Kaldo's Stacks only and copied nowhere else, and a sealed backup is kept at Kesrath. Film of any work it lists will come only in later deposits, batch by batch. Odile signs the receipt in front of her and the clerks. Then Celestine asks whether a foreign reader can train as a librarian in the Stacks. By the same examination as anyone, Odile says. Celestine says she'll ask her father, and doesn't mention that she has already decided.

Chapter 141: Side by Side

Through Ward's eyes, spring has its own business. Since January a second machine has hummed in the Works Town's machine hall, and Merrin has stopped going home at night. On Tuesday 30 March the werewolf packs drove off a beast at the High Shore's margin, the tenth pin on the board. On Friday 2 April Runa turns sixteen. A letter comes from Princess Liane with eleven questions about her tail. Runa asks Ward for the knights' first-year course, which the school has given any boy or girl who asked since the day it opened. He says yes, the way he says it to everyone, and tells her the guard will still walk four paces behind her on the course.

On Friday 23 April the groves fruit for the third time, about eleven thousand pods. Three thousand go back into the ground because the Assembly said so, which is also the number Ward said he could guard. The rest go to the Hearing, the works and the reserve, and once more a share goes by set to the old peoples' groves. Emeric signs the planting order at three thousand without a word. Ward notices that he doesn't say a word, which on Emeric is a speech. On Saturday 15 May Bren's Axion walks, the second of the soldiers' mechs, so Kaldo now has two. Mira stays in her Outrider by choice.

On Thursday 8 April, through Merrin Sallis's eyes, the two machines first disagree. Both are running Ferra's grove ledger off the same cards: the new keeping engine, built on chips, and Tess's old counting engine, built on relays. The figures differ in the seventh column. Merrin assumes the old one is wrong. It isn't. The keeping engine has a hairline crack in one chip of its adder that only shows when it's warm, and Isa Brann finds it by tapping boards with a pencil while the totals run. On Monday 10 May they disagree again on the same ledger, now with the third fruit's counts in it. This time the counting engine is wrong: a relay contact worn hollow after nine years. Dov signs off the new contact. Merrin writes on the hall's board in chalk why you run two machines for a year, and nobody rubs it off. The keeping engine's first job that matters is Ferra's chart of attacks and air, and it shows the two lines moving together faster than her pencil did. Ferra tells Merrin it proves a link, not a cause, before Merrin can tell her. On Saturday 22 May the hall's radio reports Milford's dome lit over the farms and the seed stores. Two days later the planting begins: groves first, guards before seeds.

Through Merrin's eyes, the first held message between offices travels through the keeping engine's store, from Ferra's office to the hospital's. Emeric calls it electronic mail; Merrin calls it the post. Within the month the line room takes down its check-in board.

The keeping engine now runs Kaldo's first higher language, so a program written at Cinderhale's office runs in the Works Town's hall without its writer in the room. Merrin's people stop writing programs only Merrin can read, and they complain about it.

Before Runa asks Ward for the course, she runs his fourth birthday problem and finishes first, and is cross that nobody chased her.

Chapter 142: Feeling

Through Imani's eyes, the start of June brings news from far off and a new device at home. On Wednesday 26 May a swarm of small fliers came over the Guano Isles' outer stacks at dusk. The winged ones went indoors and stayed there two nights, and asked for nothing. On Monday 7 June the first fourth-generation wristbands go out, with a small black-and-white picture screen, little maps, a fixed menu and pocket phones. They go first to the menders, the clinics' night staff, the line room's runners and the crews who will run the new railway, and to everyone else next year. The heart tracing ships. The alert for an irregular heartbeat doesn't, because Imani has seen what it does to a healthy clerk at three in the morning and won't sign it yet. Wren's team argued with her for a month and lost.

A sorter from the Stannary sheds comes in with a red, peeling patch on his hand the shape of the ore he carried in his glove. Imani names it a radiation burn, Emeric's word, and writes the first handling rules: tongs, not hands; lead-lined boxes; little time near the ore and plenty of distance; an ore counter in every sorting shed. She sends them to Ferra to be made law.

On Thursday 10 June, through Oswy Tamsk's eyes, there's a new piece on his hand. Oswy is the docker who lost his forearm at the Stannary fall. Two years ago he closed his first electric hand on a cup. Last year Imani rerouted his nerves so the hand could read them. Now there are pressure pads in its fingertips, wired back through those nerves, so the hand tells him how hard he's holding. Imani gives him an egg. He breaks the first, because the pads are talking and he hasn't learned what they're saying. He holds the second. He doesn't say anything clever about it. He asks whether he can take it home.

Not every step goes cleanly. On Monday 14 June a reader at Wayford's clinic gives a carter twice the Kindling on his card. The carter is sick in bed for a week and fine after. Mabyn Hoskin writes the reader rule into the healers' book: every dose is read off the patient's card by a second reader before it is given. The reader keeps his place and reads second for a year. On Friday 18 June Edda Rowe qualifies as a mender. There are now about forty menders, one at every Kaldo clinic, and more than five hundred readers. A bleed forty miles from Imani now has a mender within reach.

The clinic labs are open: samples go by the mail train to the hospital's lab and the counts come back by teletype the same day, so a pilot-town clinic reads its own blood counts without Imani in the room.

Chapter 143: The Soak Ledger

On Saturday 26 June, through Armand's eyes, the liner leaves for the dragons' country. The trip was asked for by Sohra's set in May, and her grandmother said yes. The K Sorrel flies on the wing on kerosene, her lifting stones at idle and shut, so they take nothing from the Band's air, and she refuels at Aumere's field each way. The two Outriders stay at Aumere, because the dragons' sky doesn't take armed fliers and the liner isn't armed. Ward objects once and loses. Armand, nearly a hundred and looking thirty-two, watches the sea thicken under the window. Ferra and Imani stay home. Ward won't take them where Corvan lives, and Emeric agrees for Ward's reason. He doesn't know Saldis has a reason of her own. Zale meets them at Aumere's field and says his brother has never once flown anywhere without escorts. "He has now," says Ward.

On Sunday 27 June, through Saldis's eyes, the liner sets down on the sea below her cone on her own sealed stores. She draws nothing from the air, and every gram she uses is logged. Corvan is up on the rim and doesn't come down. Emeric shows Saldis Ferra's soak ledger: every seed Kaldo has soaked on its racks since Year 18, every run, the racks' draw entered as soaking and not as drawing. He shows her the count of trees standing and the seeds sent south to the old peoples' groves. He leaves a bound copy for her own observers. Then he asks two things: how the old cycle worked, and whether the dragons would carry seeds again. She tells him in a few plain sentences. The house ate the pods in autumn, carried them up through the Band's cold in the long dark, and let them go in the spring storms over the ground that needed them. Armand hears an old Tower saying in it, "the dragons sow the winter", which he had always taken for a figure of speech. Armand asks the one practical thing, how a house breathes up there in the long dark. Most of it is the body, she says, and the rest is the long breath: air packed into the air sacs before a climb and let out slowly. The house's eldest teaches it at the Fall in the season before a hatchling's first climb, and nobody climbs who hasn't held it a night on the crater floor first. She taught Sohra herself. Emeric says nothing and keeps it, because the royal line has no eldest to teach it. Her answer to the second question is "Not while my granddaughter's horn is a stump." Not never, but not before the debt is paid. Then she gives him something he didn't ask for: where the old drop grounds lay over Ostrellin before the Thinning, named by landmarks two thousand years old. Armand writes them down. From the rim Corvan says, in both their hearing, "He wants us for seed-carts." Neither of them answers him.

The liner moors on the sea that night. The knights' night glass is useless here, because the warm rock of a live cone washes out its heat picture, so Ward keeps his mana sense open and two knights on deck. Saldis watches the liner's one lamp from the rim and thinks about the two women who aren't aboard. On Monday they fly home by Aumere. Nothing was promised.

Chapter 144: Omen

On the night of Saturday 17 July, through Sohra's eyes, two beasts come up the cone from the sea, hooking claws into the old lava, gold cracked into their grey eyes. The house kills them itself: her grandmother's weight, Corvan's fire, Sohra in the air. Corvan takes a hook through one wing, and it will mend. The bodies go into the vent's mouth. No head is sent and no core is taken. Afterwards Corvan says it to his mother in front of her: three weeks after the gold-eyed king stood on this stone, the gold-veined beasts climbed it. It is an omen, and the house should close its sky to Kaldo. Saldis answers, "It isn't him." Nothing in them smells of this world. Nothing in them remembers being anything. They are made of this world's stone and mana and are foreign all the way through, and she knows they are not natural. She refuses to close the sky, and Corvan leaves the rim. That same night Sohra rings Kaldo's line room on her own set, because her grandmother told her to. The dragons have never before told Kaldo anything they didn't have to.

On Monday 19 July, through Ferra's eyes, she reads Sohra's words back from the operator's log. She writes Saldis's verdict in her book as close to Sohra's words as the log has them. It is the twelfth pin on the board, fifty-two days after the eleventh, and the first at a dragon house. Emeric wants to fly to the cone. Nobody asked him to, and Saldis asked for nothing. Ferra doesn't stop him, and neither does Ward. He doesn't go. He rings Sohra back to say Kaldo heard, and nothing else, and she tells him that was the right answer. Within a fortnight the parishes of the Core and Velmoor are preaching about the beasts of the crown at a dragon's house. Not one of the preachers has ever seen a dragon.

Chapter 145: The Terms of the Treeline

In the first days of August, through Ferra's eyes, Kaldo's party comes to an orc treaty-house on the edge of the elves' forest: the king, Ferra, Imani, Ward and a small escort, with an apology and the thirty tree seeds Ferra has kept in her book for two years. The orcs keep the house and the ground around it, and three laws meet at its door: orc neutrality, the forest's own law, and the Leaving Terms. The elves' answer is no. Then a keeper named Arel writes a narrow permit anyway. A sick animal is already moving through one grove on the forest's margin, and the permit lets a named few in to help, Imani among them. A warden named Caedra argues against it and loses.

On the path to the treeline Emeric steps left a moment before the path bends at a split oak, and Ferra thinks nothing of it. She does notice what he does next. He moves so that she and Imani are on the side away from the trees, and when the party is rearranged he does it again. In a negotiation he has always let her choose her own ground. She asks him quietly whether he has picked something up. He hasn't. He goes over the escort's precautions, and leaves the permit and her seat at the table alone.

Chapter 146: The Man Who Looked Aside

On Thursday 5 August, through Ward's eyes, the king keeps both wives within reach when the guard plan doesn't call for it. He asks Ward about the withdrawal route, and Ward watches him remember that he has already been told. Veinleaf tea is steeping somewhere near the house, and Emeric says he is hungry for a meal he can't name.

The elves greet them under a mirage, a working that hides the wardens posted in the trees. The grove air floods Emeric's mana sense, so he looks with his dragon sight. His eyes flare gold, and he looks straight at a warden nobody was meant to see. To the wardens he is a human king with the eyes of their dead royal house, seeing through their own greeting. A young warden looses an arrow onto orc ground. Caedra orders the party contained. A Kaldo flight knight, seeing arrows, crosses the forest canopy, which only the winged ones may do, and the wardens bat him down into the branches with wind. He is bruised and nothing worse. Ward's order to his line is that nobody fires, stun rounds included.

Chapter 147: The Line That Did Not Fire

The same day, still through Ward's eyes, Kaldo's line stands with shields up and rifles slung while the arrows come in. Ferra counts the volleys and reads them slowing. Imani is on her knees at the edge of the shield line, treating one of the launch crew, when a wind-steered arrow curls round the shields toward her. Emeric takes it out of the air a hand's width from her shoulder, and it burns to nothing in his fist. Then his half-dragon form starts to rise without his calling it: scale along the forearm, heat coming off him, the grass browning at his feet. Ward walks in front of him with his back to the arrows and says, "Not here." Imani, not looking up from her patient, says, "I'm not hit. You're frightening him." Emeric brings it back down himself. It takes longer than it should. The arrow is ash, and his eyes are still going to Imani and then to Ferra.

In a short section through Caedra's eyes, she holds her wardens' volley. The humans have not fired, the orcs are objecting, and Arel is shouting at her. She walks out alone with her stave and strikes at the king to test him. He meets it with the counter her own family teaches, a form no human should know. The stave chars under his grip, and he lets go on purpose. She keeps her weapon, with the print of his hand burned into the wood and her own hand unhurt. She says a word in the old tongue, and he nearly answers it. Then the sick animal comes out of the trees, drawn by the wardens' own massed wind-working, and a Kaldo shield moves to where an elven keeper points. Kaldo's hurts for the day are two scrapes and one bruised knight. No elf is hurt.

Chapter 148: What the Forest Asked

From Thursday 5 to Friday 6 August, through Imani's eyes, the work turns to the animal. Emeric builds an enclosure to hold it apart, and the keepers show him why that is wrong. The rescue is done their way. The keepers need Imani farther off, out of his reach. He offers to come with her. She tells him what she actually needs, which is room, the keepers' guidance and clear ground, and he stays where the keepers put him. She can see what it costs him. The animal is steadied, not cured. Sirin, a junior keeper whose family lost its nursery right over the animal, notices something that may clear her.

Afterwards the downed flight knight is recovered with the forest's leave. Ward writes an account of the day for the orc house and puts his own knight's breach in it. The named few are invited into the grown hall. On the way in Imani passes old cradle fittings.

Chapter 149: A Song Through the Wall

On Saturday 7 August, through Emeric's eyes, the party sits at the table of Sennel, the Keeper of the Passing, in the grown hall, where people plainly live. Two cups of tea were poured at the places by the window before anyone told her who was coming. While she pours a third, Sennel lets her sense open on the three of them, as a keeper does with any guest, reaching for something she half recognises in the two women. Emeric feels it land. He wraps the three of them in his own bound mana, and for a breath Sennel can read nothing at her own table. Her cup goes over. The living wall behind them dims, and the escort's hands move. He says only, "You ask." She does not argue. She calls it a keeper's habit and a guest's insult, both, and says she won't again. He lets the working go, and the lights at their end of the hall burn brighter for an hour. Later a child is being sung to in the next room. He hums the ending of the song, and stops.

That night his wives raise it before he does. Ferra points out that he read every person in that hall when they walked in. He says he looked, and she knocked. Ferra says that from outside it is a thin line. Imani says she might have let a healer of that age look if anyone had asked her, and nobody did, the Keeper or her husband. That gets the rest out of him. He didn't like this place before the wardens gave him a reason. He keeps looking for the two of them. One of them says it was the gate again, the night Imani was taken. He says no: at the gate he knew why. Ferra asks whether he means to ask them to leave. He doesn't. They agree on ordinary check-ins and a plan for an emergency, and no escort.

In a short section through Sennel's eyes, she knows where her recognition comes from: the passing stone she carries, which holds what the keepers before her saw. She cannot say why it fits two human strangers. She takes the stone out and still doesn't read it.

Chapter 150: The Empty Cradles

From Monday 9 to Wednesday 11 August, through Imani's eyes, the grove-healer Ilwen shows her the cradle-house. It has sixty fittings, and four are in use. One empty cradle is still kept in repair. The healers already hold the records. Reading them with Ilwen, Imani finds a cause for the lost pregnancies that can be treated: the unborn child is dense with mana, and the groves no longer do half the feeding. Her own register of doses from Kaldo's births is the starting guess. She and Ilwen write a measured plan together, and Ilwen corrects one of her assumptions. A woman who has lost six pregnancies, and who has work and a life of her own, hears them out and asks for time. Emeric is not at the door once, which is what the three of them agreed.

Chapter 151: The Line on the Paper

Over the same days, through Ferra's eyes, she works in the ledger-house with Orvane, the Keeper of the Accounts, and Orvane's old teacher Maereth. They build a projection of the elves' numbers together, in pencil before ink, with every assumption shown. With the treatment, the line may hold. Maereth tells her how the old war began. Two women of the royal house, the human king's wives, were killed by their own kin as traitors, on a charge of hiding humans who stole from the groves, and the elves' council arrested the killers and offered the king justice. In the old Accords' books Ferra finds the text of the Leaving Terms and reads what the human side promised. Sennel agrees to open a question the elders sealed after the war.

That night Ferra tells Emeric all of it. He asks how open her work in those books is to challenge, and says he will back her. She decides for herself what needs answering and what she can prove.

Chapter 152: What a Life Costs

On Friday 13 August, through Emeric's eyes, the pope comes up in front of Sennel, and Emeric calls the pope "he." Sennel stops him. "There's no he. Maybe now there is. Back then it was a she." He says every pope has been a man. She shows them a memory from her stone: a council long ago, and a human woman at the table taking notes nobody asked her to take. All three of them react to the face, and none can say why. Emeric is shaken more by Ferra going white and Imani's hand finding his wrist than by the face itself.

Sennel then tells the room that she has an interest of her own in what she is about to open, and opens the sealed record. There is an old method, a rebuild of the body that cannot be undone, that turns the elves' long, nearly childless design into a quick one: a short life and many children, passed down to those children. An old couple asks what is being taken from them by refusing it. The answer is that it isn't only their own years they would be spending. An elf says aloud, looking at Emeric, that the children of elves and humans are long-lived and fertile both. A woman who wants the change is given a private consultation and time. In the record are the two women condemned together by their own house. It gets under Emeric's skin, and he looks at the two beside him and goes back to listening. One of the Hasty, the younger elves who want the change, asks the king what he would do. He won't say. He has just learned that the length of a life can be set, in a room where it isn't his to ask.

Chapter 153: The Leaving Terms

On the night of Saturday 14 August, through Ferra's eyes, the orc house holds a Guest Fire: each party cooks one pot from market ingredients, the pots stay open, and the winner's name is cut into the lintel. On Sunday the sitting hears three questions. Caedra argues the Leaving Terms against Kaldo. She also reports truthfully that Kaldo's line never fired, and that the king had the weapon of the old war the whole time and did not use it under arrows. She adds that he was looking for something before anyone gave him cause, and nobody from Kaldo denies it.

Then a descendant of the hardliners puts the old charge to Ferra: that she has been in the Accords' books taking what isn't hers for a human king. Emeric is on his feet before the sentence ends. Ferra tells him to sit down, and he does. She answers the charge herself, with the access record Orvane signed. Only then does he speak. The last time that charge was brought against two women here, he says, this forest's own council offered justice for what was done to them. No human king has said it in this forest before.

The sitting votes Kaldo a bounded permit to return, apart from anything to do with the sealed method. Sirin's family is to have its right back if the evidence holds. The exchange is made. Kaldo leaves the thirty seeds, Imani's dosing plan and register, and the projection. It takes home a deep numbing medicine called rootsleep, the brewers' moulds, clearwood, the text of the Leaving Terms by leave, and an offer: one use of the tree taught for every grove Kaldo plants and keeps. Nothing is traded for the method, which stays in the forest. As the three of them walk out of the grown hall, Ferra sees Emeric's shoulders come down. He doesn't seem to know they had been up. The elves' last word to them is, "When you find something worth asking, ask us first."

Chapter 154: The Founder's Stair

From Monday 16 August, through Ferra's eyes, the party does not go straight home. Duncan Hale, home since the spring on his first leave in five years, meets them on the Westgone's dense side with his march-council's leave: a page and a half that says what Kaldo may not do here. No trade, no talks, no stay past the week. Ferra reads it twice and likes it. This is not a visit of state. It is a few people from Kaldo crossing the march by the council's leave, and the Westgone keep everything else for another voyage.

On Wednesday they come to the council stair, and the founder at the top of it: an older man in stone, the prince of the old south-east who gave up his crown and crossed with the last ships. It is Emeric's face, as Duncan said in the steward's room. Duncan says nothing this time. He watches the three of them look at it. A Westgone child asks the king whether he is the founder's grandson. He says he doesn't know, and Duncan, behind him, says: kin, then.

Chapter 155: The Old Crossing

From Thursday 19 August, through Emeric's eyes, they cross into the thin march, the weak air he grew up in. The Westgone patrol riding with them goes groggy in it and works it in short shifts. Duncan does not, and gives the reason in one word: the Current. At the edge of the old towns, the ruins the Westgone's first settlers built in the war's last years, Leth and the residents' settlement council meet them. The people who live here now are displaced families of the old races, here by the Westgone's leave, and their leave is for two days and the old towns only.

A river crossing repaired in the founder's time still carries the carts. Emeric reads the load path and says what the builder was after. So the accounts say, Duncan answers. "I'd have liked to talk to him." He looks at the mill on its river wheel, the pumps and the repair shops that keep the place alive without mana, and says he respects them. In the old square the founder's likeness has its face broken off and the eyes gone. A resident tells him plainly why: gold eyes over the old forests. Nobody from Kaldo argues. Standing in front of it, he finds his hand flat on his chest. Ferra has a hand at her ribs, and Imani has gone quiet. Nobody says whose it was first.

Chapter 156: A Few Dozen Paces

On Sunday 22 August, through Imani's eyes, she asks Leth's council for something she has only ever named: a dead zone a few dozen paces across, outside the settlement, for a quarter of an hour. She tells them exactly what it is, and what a dead zone once did to the Murr. They say yes on terms: no closer to the houses than the old boundary stone, a resident to watch, and it goes in their records.

In a stubble field on Tuesday she runs the test with a forced stone, Emeric holding his core in so it doesn't bloom and fill the space. She asks each of them to describe what they feel alone, before anyone hears the others. Inside, the link between the three of them goes quiet. The ache stays. That rules out the live link and nothing else: it is hers, or the piece of him she has carried since Lantern Night. "That tells me something," she says. "It doesn't tell me what started it." Ferra writes it down. Leth asks whether the thin land is harming his people. Imani says she can't tell from a week, and what she would need to know. It is left there for another voyage. The patrol's log of the dead zone is handed to Ferra to read and given back, and Duncan stays on the dense side until spring.

Chapter 157: Kaldo to Wayford

Through Kezia's eyes, the summer has had its news. On Wednesday 1 September a Glassrunner came onto the caravan road at the dry edge. Orc wardens, lent across the border under the Shore Compact, rode with the Sabir and drove it off. It was the first time the Compact lent hunters. In July the safety inspectorate named three examiners for the new Capital Line, and Kezia was one of them. On Friday 3 September they walk its last section, and on the Lisle viaduct she finds one guideway joint a hair out of tolerance. The magnets would forgive it. The standard doesn't. She won't sign. Tess's crew reseats the joint over the weekend. The inspectorate sets its own sign-off date, Friday 10 September, and signs on that day. Only then does Kaldo announce an opening date.

On Monday 13 September, through Tess's eyes, the Capital Line opens on the inspectorate's date. Tess cuts the ribbon with Osric beside her. It was her drawing, and nobody else in Kaldo would have thought of cutting it. The seats in the first public car were drawn by lot from the crews who built the viaducts. Keller's and Odo's men run the train. Bram rides in the cab and touches nothing. The king rides in the third car with a ticket, and at his own wish he isn't in Kaldo News's picture. The train floats from Kaldo to Wayford in about an hour and a half. Wayford's platform is full of people who have never been anywhere in an hour and a half. The papers that week carry Ferra's figures: since the Blood Act every duchy and nearly every town in Randillia has taken the free wristband. Only the Church-held barony of Saint Ilda's and the moor parishes of Coldharbour have never asked.

On Monday 30 August a man in his early twenties comes to the castle gate and asks for the wolf-eared girl. He is the boy who ran from Coldspring, and he still smells of fear. He gives himself up to her. On Saturday 4 September, through Runa's eyes, she takes him to the werewolves' moot at the stone ring outside the orc coast's treaty-house door herself, with Grau and Ward. The packs' heads ask her what he did. She says she can prove he was there and can't prove what he did. She doesn't forgive him and doesn't touch him, and the moot keeps him for its own judgement. Later Emeric tells her it took more than any bout she fought that year.

Chapter 158: Ratified

On Thursday 16 September, through Alina's eyes, the Kesrath compact is signed at Kesrath. It includes Breck's inspection clause, Lell's ratification procedure, the repair-school annex and the limits on watching. Its first clerk is a Solreth jurist chosen by the members, not by Kaldo and not by Duvrenne. Velmoor signs the standards and the protocol on watching but declines the court that settles disputes for now. Its lords won't put their own courts under a foreign one while the Church watches them, and nobody punishes Velmoor for saying so. Solreth signs everything. Randillia and Duvrenne sign subject to the emperor's ratification, and Kaldo signs last, at Alina's asking. Countess Marrant chooses Velmoor's first joint project: a nursing school at Brannick's Ford, on Velmoor's side of the border, so that Velmoor's nurses can train without leaving home. It answers her own account of Kaldo hollowing her country out, on her country's terms. Then Duvrenne's council gives its answer about the tideshell beast's core, which Kaldo asked to buy in June for Zale's sea-going mech. The answer is no. The core stays Duvrenne's, in Kaldo's keeping under the written terms, because one day it will go into Duvrenne's own first machine. Alina tells Waylon and Zale it's the council's no and not hers, and that she would have said the same.

On Monday 4 October, through Severin's eyes at Ostrel, he ratifies the compact in his own council and in his own voice. The cardinal on his council protests for the record, and the record takes it. The lamps in the chamber are brighter than he has ever seen them on the same charge. His chamberlain blames the season. Severin doesn't. On the plain below the crater's mountain ring his surveyors have paced out three thousand metres for a runway and staked it, on the Core's own money and without a Kaldo engineer. The Core's foundry's first heat of heavy rail steel has cracked on the bend test. A young imperial engineer asked for the next heat anyway, and Severin gave it to him and told Kaldo nothing. On his desk is Keswick's report that a Velmoor inspector walked Bram's shop under the new clause and filed three findings, two of which Bram agreed with. Celestine asks to train in the Stacks under the exchange, and Marius asks for the Polytechnic again. "Next year. Both of you."

Chapter 159: The Shortest Line

Through Zale's eyes, autumn has been spent on a machine with nothing inside it. Since June the frame of his sea mech, the Undertow, has stood on the Cradle at South Sentinel. Wren's shop and Bram's yard sized it for a swimmer's core, because Kaldo meant to buy the tideshell's, and Duvrenne said no. On Monday 11 October the dwarves of the Sea Hold killed a beast at their sea gate with their own hand-piloted wardens. They called it in by set the same night, the first time they have told anyone anything they didn't have to. It was the fourteenth pin, forty days after the thirteenth. On Saturday 23 October Wayford's dome is lit, and all four of Kaldo's baronies are now under one.

Sunday 31 October is the second Hollow Night since the Gathering. The children wear felt masks, because last year's school rule stands and nobody had to be told again. The halls of the peoples open with the peoples' own words still on the walls. The orcs keep their vigil at home, and Kaldo leaves a lamp at the door of their empty tower floors anyway. Zale stands at the stone for the people with no graves, where Corvan stood last year, because the merfolk are among those people, and he rings Ilenne from the river wall. The stealth knights in the lit streets wear their night glass up, because the lamps and pumpkins wash it out, and the watch on the bay ring wears it down over the cold water. On Monday 8 November the Undertow's frame is finished but for its brain. There is no swimmer's core in anyone's hands. Zale stays the week to fit his harness and the seat in the sphere, because a frame with nothing in it is still his. Ilenne tells him over the set to come home when it's done or when something makes him. He is still in Kaldo on the eve.

Through Ferra's eyes, the last preparations have come in. On Friday 22 October Bram's shop delivered the second run of twelve gift sets into the line room's locker, scramblers fitted and no position sent in clear. Armand's survey of the old drop grounds Saldis named shows the dead ground from the Thinning greening at its edges. Saban's man north of the range reports that the Church's white hulls go up into the Band from Ostrel's towers on nights with no feast, twice as often as last year, and come down heavy. Ferra adds it to her list of what else they're hiding. Nothing is proved. On Sunday 14 November, the evening of the eve, there are fourteen pins on the board. The gaps between them have run 112 days, then 96, 91, 84, 79, 76, 72, 67, 62, 57, 52, 46 and 40. At that pace the next one is due about now. She does the sum, writes nothing that isn't a number, turns the lamp down and goes home. The sets are quiet.

On Hollow Night Zale watches his niece stop dead in the crowd, turn, walk forty yards through hundreds of people and kneel by a cart. Under it is a frightened child who lost her mother at the stone; Runa found her by the smell of fear. A week later Ferra finds the two of them on the castle steps, Runa teaching the king how she did it, and the king bad at it.

Where the Story Stands After Chapter 162

It has been a year of other people deciding. The Assembly slowed the groves, and Emeric lost the vote and won only the right to have the question asked again every year. An inquiry with a dwarf on it found what the dark stones are bound to and refused to guess who bound them. At Kesrath, Velmoor's lawyer rewrote Kaldo's own clause, Duvrenne voted against its closest friend, and Solreth turned down a gift for something it could keep. The emperor used his real powers twice, once against Kaldo, and both times he was right. An inspector held the king's railway for a week over one joint. Duvrenne's council kept its core. A dragon showed Emeric where her people used to sow the land and told him not yet.

In August Kaldo met the elves and came home with a permit to return. Its line stood under arrows and fired nothing. Imani found a cause for the empty cradles that can be treated, and Ferra read what the human side once promised. The Keeper of the Passing told the three of them that the pope was once a woman, and showed them a face none of them could explain their reaction to. Emeric was afraid for his wives before the first arrow, shut the Keeper's sense out of them without asking either one, and still can't say why. Nobody in the forest, and nobody from Kaldo, knows who the three of them were.

Abroad the attacks keep closing in. Fourteen are pinned on the board, and the gap between them has fallen from a hundred and twelve days to forty. The beasts have now climbed a dragon's mountain, and the oldest dragon alive says they are not of this world and not natural. Her son says they follow the gold-eyed king. North of the range the Church's ships fly at night more often than any feast needs.

At home everything Kaldo might need is in place: a railway that floats to Wayford in an hour and a half, a mender at every clinic, domes over every barony, two soldiers' mechs, a box of new sets in the locker, and Zale in Kaldo with a sea machine that has no brain. The towns that never asked for the wristband still don't have one. On Sunday 14 November Ferra turns the lamp down on the board. The next attack is due, at the pace of all the others.
