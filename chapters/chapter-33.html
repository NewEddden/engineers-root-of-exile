Chapter 33 - The Only Hand

He gave the network four nights to prove it before he tried to give it away.

Four nights of the board waking its clean small lamps at foxes and owls and, once, a poacher who wandered up the ford road and stood a long while at the treeline before deciding Kaldo was not worth the walk. Four nights of the wall doing a guard's work without a guard, seeing in the dark and saying what it saw and waking no one it did not need to wake. By the fourth morning Emeric was as sure of it as he was of the furnace, which was the bar he had set for himself, and he went looking for Ward to make good on the promise he had made himself on the parapet and again over the board, the promise that this thing would not live and die in one man's hands.

He had no doubt it would work. That was the part worth remembering afterward, when he was picking through where the certainty had come from. He had already done the impossible part twice. He had reached for Bram and pulled him into the delve and they had built a furnace together in a place that was not a place, and he had reached for Ferra and she had walked her census columns in the same false room, and neither time had it felt like a trick he might not manage again. Sharing the thing was not the question. He knew a man could stand in there beside him. The only question was the tidy administrative one of handing Ward a set of keys and going back to his other work, and that was the kind of question Emeric had never once found hard.

Ward listened to the plan the way he listened to everything, without moving his face much, and said only, "You want me able to reach it when you're not standing there."

"When I'm not standing there, when I'm asleep, when I'm three days' ride at the capital. It watches the whole approach and it answers to one hand, and one hand is exactly the thing I built it to stop being. So. Yours too."

Ward considered that, and did not point out the obvious thing, which was that he had never delved in his life and had no notion of how a man was supposed to begin. He only nodded, and set his feet the way he set them before he tried anything new with the bracer, braced for it to hurt or not, and waited.

Bringing him in was easy. That was the cruelty of the whole afternoon, that the easy part was the part that meant nothing.

Emeric reached for him the way he had reached for Bram, thinking of Ward the way he had thought of the furnace, present, invited, wanted here, and the office pulled back at its edges and was somewhere else, and Ward was in it. He stood in the false room turning his head slowly, taking in the soft-edged walls and the board floating where the real board sat down at the gate, every node a point of steady light, the whole wall laid out as a thing you could walk around and read at a glance. He put out a hand toward the nearest node the way a man reaches for a thing to be sure it is really there, and the light answered him, brightened under his attention, shifted when he willed it to shift.

"That's it," Emeric said. "You have it. Try the third node. Wake its lamp."

Ward tried, and the third lamp woke, and for a moment it was done, and Emeric felt the clean satisfaction of a problem closing.

Then he stepped out.

He did it without thinking much of it, the way you step out of a room you expect to walk back into, letting the delve go, coming up into the actual office with the actual light of late afternoon in it, meaning only to check the real board at the gate against what Ward was doing inside. And the delve went with him. All of it. The false room, the floating wall, Ward's hold on the third node, gone in the same breath he came up in, because it had never been Ward's room. It had been Emeric's room, and Ward had been a guest in it, and when the host walked out the house did not stay standing with the guest inside. It came down because there was nothing but Emeric holding it up.

Ward blinked, and looked at his own hands the way Bram had, and said, "It's gone."

"Wait." Emeric went back in, and brought him back, and this time did not step out. This time he stayed, and had Ward hold the third node, and while Ward held it Emeric reached down through the delve to the real network and tried to do the thing that would matter, tried to leave something behind, to set into the network some standing mark that said this hand also, so that when he let go the mark would remain and Ward would still have his grip. He knew the shape of what he wanted precisely. A second key cut to the same lock. A name added to a ledger of who may. A deputy sworn in, so that the office answered to the office and not to the man.

The network gave him none of it. There was no place to set the mark. He reached for the slot where a second key should go and there was no slot, not because it was hidden but because the lock was not that kind of lock. He was not turning a key at all, he understood, slowly, with the cold that came when a thing he had assumed turned out to be a thing he had invented. He was the key. The network did not know a hand from a permission the way a door knew a key from a thief. It knew him, the specific him, the signature of the specific mind that had bonded it, the way the rune under his mother's care had known her before it ever knew him. It answered that and nothing else. Ward in the room was Ward standing next to the man the network trusted, borrowing the trust by proximity, and the instant the man was gone the trust was gone with him because it had never been transferable in the first place. It had never been a thing you held. It was a thing you were.

He came up out of it and sat with that for a while, in the ordinary office, in the ordinary light, and Ward waited, having gathered from Emeric's face that the afternoon had stopped going the way it was meant to.

"I can bring you in beside me," Emeric said finally, mostly to himself, laying the true shape of it out where he could look at it. "I can't leave you the keys. There are no keys. There's only me, and whoever I'm holding the door open for, and the second I let go of the door there's only me again."

"Then teach me to hold my own door," Ward said, reasonably, which was the right instinct and the reason Emeric had wanted him for this.

"I don't know how a man holds his own door." And that was the floor of it, the thing under all the rest. He had come to this by accident, over years, sleeping near a stone his dead mother had left him until the hours of it crossed some line nobody had told him was there. He was, as far as he knew, as far as anyone knew, the only person alive who had ever done it. He did not know if Ward could learn it. He did not know if anyone could, or how long it took, or what it cost, or whether the thing that had happened to him could be made to happen to someone else on purpose instead of by grief and repetition. There might be a way. Somewhere down that road there might be a way to make a second delver, a real one, with his own bond that stood whether Emeric was in the room or not. But that was not a key he could cut this afternoon. That was a road he could not yet see the start of, and he was standing at the near end of it holding a wall that answered to no hand but his.

He should have stopped there and been afraid of it properly. He could see, later, that this was the place where the fear belonged and did not come.

Instead he did the thing he was good at, which was to weigh it, and he weighed it wrong. The network was new. He was here. He was almost always here, and on the rare turns he was not, the wall had stood two years without any of this and would stand a few nights more on ordinary men and ordinary eyes. The exposure that the single hand actually opened, the one where an enemy did not go at the wall at all but went at the hand, at him, personally, to make the one key walk itself to a lock, that exposure sat at the far end of a chain of ifs so long he could not make it feel real. He had just broken a coalition that came at him with armies and lawyers and a bought census. The idea that the next blow would be a quiet one aimed at his own person, that someone would understand his wall well enough to know its only weakness was the man who built it and patient enough to make him carry himself into the trap, was a thing he knew in the abstract and could not make his gut hold. He had told himself on the parapet that he would not build the wrong wall again. He did not notice that he had just finished building it, and hung the only key around his own neck, and decided the neck was safe because it was his.

So he set it down. He filed the transfer under problems worth real time, the kind he would take apart properly when the founding rush eased, a project for a quieter season, teach a man to fish, find whoever in Kaldo might have the aptitude, learn how the learning even worked. A good plan. A slow plan. The right plan for a threat that came slowly.

That night the board woke its clean small lamps for him, and for no one else, the way it would every night until the night it mattered, and he watched it do its silent competent work and felt, mostly, satisfied, and went to bed a man who had correctly diagnosed the one thing that could undo him and then, with great and careful reasoning, decided it could wait.
