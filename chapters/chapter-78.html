Chapter 78 - Which Lines Get One

The guard was sized to the land and the land was the problem, because a hundred men spread across three towns and a road were a hundred men who could not talk to each other, and an army that could not talk to itself was a hundred separate small forces pretending to be one.

Ward had said it in the scale-up and it had sat with Emeric since, the plain geography of it. If Grayrock was struck while Ward was at Wayford, Ward was a day away with every man he had, and worse than the distance was the blindness, that Ward would not even know Grayrock was struck until a rider crossed the whole holding to tell him, and by the time the rider arrived the fight was won or lost and the orders Ward sent back were answers to a battle that no longer existed. Speed of foot set the tempo of the whole war, and speed of foot was slow, and a lord who could move iron and stone and knowledge at the pace of an industrial age was still, in the one place it mattered most, moving his commands at the pace of a horse.

He had a telegraph already. That was the thing to be honest about before he built anything, that the barony had run a relay line since the defense arc, the point-to-point resonance chain that hopped a dot-and-dash pulse from post to post across the holding, and it was real and it worked and it was the cheap broad backbone he would not touch. But it was built for peace, and it showed the moment he asked it to carry a war. Its range lived and died on whichever operator happened to be staffed at each post, a strong hand extending the reach and a weak one strangling it, so the line was only ever as good as its tiredest man. It had no addressing, a pulse went along the physical chain and anyone on the chain could read it, so it was a shout down a hallway and not a word in an ear. And it was a chain, which meant it was a line of physical posts a clever enemy could cut or take or listen at, and a chain is only a chain until one link is gone.

What he wanted for the war was not more of that. It was a different thing entirely, and the different thing turned out to be already sitting in his own system waiting to be recombined, which was how the good answers always arrived. The batch rule he had used for the control runes, that two stones cut from the same origin were bonded to each other specifically and not to their type generally, was an addressing mechanism, and he had been using it as a lock and had never thought to use it as a line. Cut a pair of resonance stones from one origin and they were matched to each other and to nothing else in the world, and a signal sent on one arrived at the other directly, not hopping a chain of posts, not readable by anyone standing on a line between them, because there was no line between them, only the two matched ends. A private channel. An addressed one. A direct one. Two points that could speak to each other across the whole holding as though they stood in the same room, richer than the old pulse, a real back-and-forth in something near to time as it happened.

It was not speech, and he made himself hold that limit rather than reach past it, because reaching past it would have quietly broken a rule that held the whole magic of the world together. The wireless conduit had a low ceiling on how much it could carry and he was not going to pretend otherwise, so the paired channel solved the addressing and the directness and left the bandwidth alone, a faster and truer and private pulse-language, not a voice. And it was not free. The old relay sipped its stones with a low pulse, and the paired channel drew and sustained to hold its private line open, and sustain was a cost the same as intensity was a cost, drawn from the same well, so a line held open was a line spending, and a man could not leave every pair singing all day and expect the stones to last. Better in every way that mattered for a war, and dearer in the ways a war could afford.

He walked its necessity all the way around before he built it, because Ferra had taught him to ask whether a new thing did a job the old things could not, rather than a job they already did more cheaply. The armor's own sense network was the thing to check it against, and the check was clean, because the two did opposite jobs that a careless man would confuse. The sense network read a field. It told the men on a battlefield where the other men on that battlefield were, close, real, immediate, everyone inside one field of awareness. It could not reach a town a day away. It was never meant to. It was tactical sight, and it ended where the field ended. The telegraph did the other thing, the thing the sense network could not, which was to connect one field to another field it could not see, to move an order from a command in Kaldo to a strike team on a front, to let Ward at Wayford know Grayrock was struck in the moment it was struck rather than a day after, to bind the separated pieces of a dispersed army into one mind. The sense network made a field legible. The telegraph made the space between fields crossable. They did not overlap, and so he built it, and would not have built it if they had.

He kept the mundane floor under it the way he kept a mundane floor under everything, because a comms system that died when its magic died was a single point of failure wearing a clever coat. The baseline was the rider. It had always been the rider, a man on a horse carrying a written order, and the rider was slow and the rider survived anything, survived every stone in the barony going dark at once, survived a war that unmade every rune, and the telegraph sat on top of the rider as the fast supplement and not the replacement. If the paired lines failed, the relay chain carried the load slower. If the relay chain failed, the riders carried it slower still. Layer under layer under layer, each one cruder and more certain than the last, down to a man on a horse who needed no mana at all, so that the barony's command could be slowed but never silenced, degraded but never cut. That was the rule, and the telegraph obeyed it, a precious fast layer over a plain slow one that would outlive it.

The cost of the paired channel was the part that made it a command decision and not a utility, and he sat with Ward and Ferra to spend it, because it could not be spent twice. The pairs were finite by their nature. A matched pair came only from stones cut together at their making, and no new pair could be minted on demand to join two points that wanted joining after the fact, so the barony had exactly as many private lines as it had thought to cut pairs for, and not one more, and each pair was a single irreplaceable thing. Which meant the question was not how to build the network but which handful of lines in the whole war were worth a pair, and that was a real decision with real teeth, because a line given to one command was a line denied to every other, and the wrong choice was a silence in exactly the place the war turned.

They chose them the way they chose everything now, cold and by function. One pair bound Emeric's command to Ward directly, the two heads of the war able to reach each other from any two towns in the holding without a chain between them. One bound command to Ferra, because the forensic and courier work she ran fed the war its intelligence and could not be shouted down an open relay. One was held back, cut and matched and given to no line yet, kept in reserve for the single coordinated operation that would need a private channel more than any standing line did, a strike or an extraction where the old chain's exposure would get people killed, and holding a pair back rather than spending it was itself a discipline, the discipline of a man who had learned that the reserve you keep is the reserve that saves you. A few precious lines, each a decision, each denying itself to everywhere it did not go. He wrote them into Ferra's ledger by name and pair and holder, because a thing this scarce needed to be tracked like the strategic asset it was, and because the day one of these stones was lost or taken was a day the pair it belonged to became not a private line but a private line an enemy might now be standing inside, and he wanted to know, always, exactly where each half of each pair was.

He finished it in the quiet stretch of the buildup, the comms backbone laid under the guard he had just sized, the fast fragile precious layer set over the slow certain one, the whole nervous system of a dispersed army wired at last so that its hundred scattered men could act as one mind across a holding too large for any single field of sight. It was the last piece of the machine of war he built in peace, and he built it the way he had built the water and the streets and the school, honestly, to the real size of the thing, with the cost named and paid and the doctrine kept.

And then, the way the finished thing so often is the signal that the thing it was built for has arrived, the reports started coming up the very line he had just laid. Not a probe now. Not a merchant testing an edge or an inherited opportunist missing a deadline to see what he would let slide. Something with a shape to it, moving on the frontier, gathering the displaced and the resentful around a name that had held quiet contempt for him since a banquet years ago and had been reading his every clean street and iron pipe and hundred-man guard as a schedule the whole time he called it a rebuilding. The comms were ready. Ward was on the far end of a paired line, one town away and no longer a day. And the thing the comms were built to coordinate was, at last, coming down the road to be coordinated against.
