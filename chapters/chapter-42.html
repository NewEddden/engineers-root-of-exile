Chapter 42 - The Table

The circle had existed for eleven days before it ever sat in one room, which was, Emeric thought, exactly the kind of thing he did. He had built it the way he built everything, in pieces, in stolen hours, one forge conversation at a time, Bram at his bench and Ferra at her books and Ward with a stone in his fist swearing quietly at a doorway that would not open, each of them told his own part and none of them told the whole. Ferra had a page for it. She had headed the page with a number because a number told a reader nothing, and the number was one, and the thing had a shape and a founding cause and six people in it and had never once put those six people in the same four walls at the same hour. He had made a body and then run it as six private wires, which was the old disease wearing the new cure's coat, and he had felt the wrongness of it for days before he made himself fix it.

So he called them together. The hall was the wrong room for it, too public, too much a place where a lord sat above and men stood below, and he did not want that shape, so he took them to the long workroom off the forge where the drawings hung and the light was bad and there was one table and not enough chairs, and he let the not-enough-chairs stand, because a man who had to drag his own seat to a table sat at it differently than a man who found it waiting.

It was the first time some of them had ever been in a room together knowing what the others were. Bram and Ward knew each other, forge and yard, the two oldest fixtures of the place. But Saban had never sat down across from Armand as anything but a merchant nodding at a mage, and Imani had known Ferra only as the girl who kept the standing water order paid on time, and now here they all were, being shown the backs of each other's cards for the first time, each of them watching the others rearrange in real time from a face they half knew into a piece of a machine they had not known they were inside. He watched Saban do the arithmetic fastest, watched him look at Armand and then at Ferra and then at Imani and visibly understand that the barony he had been quietly feeding warnings to had a spine he had only ever felt one vertebra of.

"I'll say the thing I brought you here to say and then we'll do the work," Emeric told them. "Each of you holds a piece of Kaldo that would hurt to lose and would hurt worse in the wrong hands. You've each held your piece alone. That's over. From today you hold each other's, not the doing of it, Bram isn't going to smith and Ferra isn't going to keep his books, but the knowing of it, the watching of it, the answering for it when the man whose piece it is can't. No one of you is the whole thing and losing any one of you costs a limb and not the life. That's the entire design. I built a barony once that could be ended by reaching one frightened man, and a man reached it, and I am not doing that again with any of you."

Then he named them, and he did it knowing precisely what he was doing, which was reaching several rungs above anything a coastal barony had the standing to reach for. A barony had a steward and a captain and a reeve. It did not have the offices he was about to hand out. Those belonged to a king's court, or an emperor's, to the kind of house that ran a war and a treasury and a spy net as standing institutions rather than as one overworked lord doing all three by lamplight. He gave them out anyway, in a room with six people in it and a door that shut, because a title spoken where only the named can hear it is not a claim on the world, it is a statement of the size of the thing you mean to build, and he wanted the size of it standing in the room where they could all see it and decide whether they were the sort of people who wanted to build something that big or the sort who wanted to go home.

"Ward. You've read every threat that ever walked into this barony while I was still reading its manners. You take the war and the watch and the making of soldiers to a standard, the wall and the yard and the men on both. Secretary of War." He said it flat, without smiling, and Ward received it the same way, because between the two of them the joke and the earnest of it were the same thing and neither needed the other pointed out. Ward had carried his lord out of a burning boat. He did not need to be told the office was real.

"Bram. The forge, the furnace, the works, the stone. Everything Kaldo makes and everything it builds and the root under all of it. Secretary of Works." Bram wiped his hands on the same rag he always wiped them on and nodded once, the nod of a man being told his job was his job.

"Saban. The roads, the trade, the factors on the Wayford road, the quiet men in Grayrock town who heard a barony arming before it was armed. You are how Kaldo sees past its own walls, and I am not going to insult either of us by calling that spying in this room, but I am also not going to pretend I don't know what it is. Secretary of Trade." Saban's mouth did something that was not quite a smile. He understood the cover and he understood that the cover was itself a courtesy, a way of writing down a dangerous function under a boring name, and he approved of it the way he approved of a clean set of books.

"Armand." Here Emeric slowed, because Armand's was the one that mattered most and pretended to matter least. "The deep knowledge. The magic I can build past but cannot out-remember. You're the one man in the barony who can tell me when a clever new thing of mine is actually an old buried danger I'm too new here to recognize. Secretary of the Arcane, if we're keeping to the shape of it. But that's the small half. The large half is this. When I ride out of that gate, and I am going to have to ride out of it, to the capital, to be made whatever I am going to be made, the barony has answered no one since the night they carried me off, because it answered one hand and the one hand was on a horse. You are the second hand it will answer. You stay. You hold Kaldo when I can't. The office is a courtesy. The staying is the job."

Armand, who had spent a life teaching a third-born prince his letters and had not expected to end it holding the living network of an exile's barony, understood both halves, and understood that the man giving them to him had just said, in front of five witnesses, that he meant to leave and to be able to leave, which for this particular man was nearly a confession.

"Ferra." He came to her last of the offices, and this one broke the pattern on purpose. "You don't get a Secretary of anything, because you don't sit under a domain, you sit over the map of all of them. The books, the lines, the routing, the cipher, the one drawing of how every piece fits that I am specifically not allowed to hold alone anymore. If a man wants the whole shape of Kaldo he has to take you and me both and make us both willing, and we spent an evening at the forge building the reason that can't be done. You're Chancellor. You hold the map I'm not allowed to hold, and you get to tell me no, in writing, and have it stick."

Ferra took that the way she took everything, by finding the load-bearing clause first. "In writing and it sticks," she repeated. "You're giving me a no you can't overrule."

"I'm giving you the one I most need to not be able to overrule," he said. "The night I decide I know better than the books is the night the books stop being worth keeping. Yes. It sticks."

Which left Imani, and where he had made the others wait for their names he gave hers up front, plain, because hers was the one he was least worried she would turn down. "Imani. Secretary of Health. The doctoring, the wells, the sick-houses, the counting of who lives and who dies and the finding out why. All of it, credited to you, sitting at this table with the rest of them."

She took the name without a flinch, and then she told the room why she was taking it, because she had decided somewhere between the forge and this bad-lit table that she was not going to sit in a circle of people trading the backs of their cards and be the only one still holding hers face down.

"Nine men died on the western strand after I told the headman his well was clean," she said. "It wasn't clean, or it wasn't clean long enough, and I was a week early saying it, and nine men drank and swelled and went into the ground, and that was mine. Half of it was mine at the least. And this one stood up at the burning and said it was his. Said he'd moved too slow, said he should have walked the far wells himself before he let anyone drink, said it in front of every widow on that strand, and let them hate the right man for the wrong reason so not one of them would think twice about the doctor they were still going to need come winter." She looked at Emeric and did not soften a word of it. "I have worked for nobles who'd have hung nine dead men around my neck and called it justice and slept fine. This one picked up a rope that was half mine and wore the whole of it in front of the whole barony. That's why I'm at your table. Not the office. Not the water. That."

"It was mine," Emeric said. "You told them what the reading gave you. I built the reading. If it came back a week early the fault's in the thing I made, not the hand that trusted it. I'd say the same again tomorrow."

"You would," Imani said. "That's the whole of it. You'll always say that. And the day you stop, the day I watch you go looking for a hand to hang a bad reading on instead of eating it yourself, I'll know the man I signed with is gone, and I'll take my name and my doctoring and walk out the same gate you rode in through. Stay the one who eats it, and I'm yours to the wall." She pulled a chair from against it and set it at the table, having dragged it herself like the rest of them. "So. Secretary of Health. Say it once more so these five heard it and there's no unsaying it later."

He said it once more, and told her he'd rather she walked out the day he changed than stayed and let him become the kind of lord she'd have to leave quietly, and she said that was, unfortunately, exactly the sort of answer that kept her in the chair, and sat down in it.

Then they did the work, because he had promised them work and not a ceremony, and the work was the problem the whole naming had been circling. Reach. A barony that answered one hand had nearly been ended by the hand's absence, and the answer to that was rooting Armand, and rooting Armand answered who the barony listened to but not how far the barony could hear. Kaldo was about to be three territories and not one. Wayford and Grayrock were coming under the same roof, and a lord who could stand in only one of the three at a time needed a way for the other two to reach him faster than a rider could carry the news of a fire to a man already an hour too late to do anything about it.

He laid it out on the bad-lit table, and the thing he laid out was not, pointedly, magic, or not only. "We keep the runners," he said first, before anything else, because it was the rule under all his rules and he wanted it said in front of the people who would have to run the thing when he was gone. "We keep the horns and the beacon-fires and the mounted chain, all of it, every slow reliable thing this world already knows how to do. Whatever I build next sits on top of those and never in place of them, so that the day it fails, and it will fail, some night, in some weather, the barony falls back to a rider and a fire and is merely slow instead of blind. Nothing I make gets to be the only way to send a word. Say it back to me so I know you heard it."

Ward said it back. So did Ferra, drier. And then he built it for them in the air over the table, the relay chain, a line of small posts a hard ride apart, each with a flat stone and a man who knew the taps, a signal that was not speech but presence and absence, long and short, a code that could carry a warning or a number or a name down the whole length of a coast in the time it took a rider to clear the first post. The stones were nothing precious. That was the point he made hardest, because it was the point that made it Kaldo's and not his. A resonance stone drew almost nothing, carried a pattern and not a fuel, and could be cut and taught and replaced, so that the network was a standard and a drill and not a treasure, buildable by the next smith and the next, staffed by the next trained hand and the next, no single irreplaceable thing anywhere in it.

Saban saw his own roads in it before Emeric finished. The relay posts wanted to sit where his factors already sat, where the trade already stopped to water its horses, so that the spine of the fast-word network was the spine of the slow-goods network laid down over years, already staffed, already known, a thing that looked from the outside like nothing more than the merchant houses he already ran. "You want my post-houses to grow ears," he said.

"I want your post-houses to grow ears," Emeric agreed, "and I want the men with the ears to not be your men, or not only, so that no one house holds the whole line."

It was Ferra who found the flaw, which was the entire reason she held the map, and she found it in the shape of a question that was already an objection. "A post is a stone and a man who knows the taps," she said. "Take the post, you have the stone and, one knife later, the man and the taps. Now you can sit on our own line and read every word that runs down it, and worse, you can send. A captured post isn't a deaf post. It's a lying mouth in the middle of my chain."

"Which is why no post holds the whole code," Emeric said, and felt the thing he had built at the forge click into place over the thing he was building now, the two fixes meshing like they had been cut for each other, because in a way they had. "The compartments you already keep. A post knows enough to pass its own stretch and not enough to forge a word it wasn't given. The tells that say a message is true and not planted live with you and not on the line, so a taken post can garble and it can go silent, and both of those we can see, a silence is a signal same as a word, but it can't speak in our voice, because speaking in our voice needs a thing it was never allowed to hold. You keep the map of which post knows what. The line carries the word. Neither is the whole, and taking any one of them buys the buyer a corner and not the thing. It's the same lock. It's just made of distance instead of stone."

She weighed it, and did not immediately find the next flaw, which from Ferra was a longer endorsement than a yes. Bram was already sketching a post on the back of another drawing. Ward was working out, silently, in the way he worked out everything, how many men it took to hold a line that long and where the weak stretch was and what a clever enemy did to a chain first. Armand had the particular stillness of a man who had just been handed a use for a stone he had known his whole life and never once thought to string in a line. Saban was counting post-houses. Imani was watching all of them the way she watched a patient whose fever had finally turned, waiting, without much doubt now, to be sure it stayed turned.

Before he let them leave, Emeric put a second thing on the bad-lit table. A cloth roll, which he unrolled, and inside it lay six of the flat dark stones, the ones the whole empire had weighed and tested and heaped on the discard pile as dead. He had bound them himself over the last several nights, each one keyed the awkward asymmetric way the root demanded, so that it answered him at the base and would answer its holder at the surface. One for each of them. Ferra not excepted. Six people, six stones.

"Delving," he said, before anyone asked. "The thing I did in that hold, that let me read the room without eyes. The thing the network under this barony actually runs on, under the taps and the posts and everything I drew for you tonight." He turned one of the stones over between two fingers. "I learned it the slow way, a boy who slept years beside his mother's rune and never had a name for what the hours were doing to him. I can't give you the years. But I've found there's a fast road. A man who already delves can bring another across the bond, hand to hand, in a season instead of a decade. The stone in your pocket and under your pillow does the rest. Contact, worn in over time, wears the door open. That's the method, and the method is sound."

"And a guess sitting on top of the method," he went on, "which I'm calling a guess because I promised this table I'd eat my bad readings and not serve them up as certainties. I suspect, and can't yet prove, that dropping a new delver into the shared space itself, and into the dense grain of the deep sections where the mana runs thick, will force the change faster than any stone in a pocket ever could. The way cold water teaches a man to swim quicker than wading a warm shallows does. I want to try it. Wanting to try it is not the same as knowing it works, and I'll thank you to hold me to the difference."

He did not say the rest of it aloud, but he stood there over six dead stones he had taught to live and thought it plainly. This was the realm's own rite turned inside out. The crown made its knights by forcing mana into a man through a ceremony that killed some outright and left others hollowed and childless, the founder of this very barony among them, and it guarded that rite behind walls and bloodlines precisely because a thing that brutal and that costly could never be let loose to just anyone. What Emeric was doing was the same act with the cruelty engineered out of it. A change a man survived. A change he could teach the next smith and the next, that asked a season and a stone and a willing hand where the rite asked a grave. That, more than the relay line and more than the offices, was the thing he had come to Kaldo to build. He simply had not held a name for it until it was sitting on a cloth in front of him.

It was Ferra, of course, who took her stone and then found the load under it. "You'd bring us in across a bond that runs, by your own telling, base to surface. You at the base." She set the stone down and looked at him level. "Which means the six of us delve because you opened the door, through a door you're standing in. You spent the whole night pulling the barony off one hand so no knife to one man could end it. And here you are, at the root of all six of us. Is that a gift, or is it a leash you've dressed as one."

"I don't have a clean answer," he said, and meant it, because the honest thing and the safe thing were not the same thing and he had learned better than to pretend they were. "The same bond that brings you in fast is a thing I'd have to be trusted not to turn. A man asking to be handed exactly the power he spent all evening dismantling everywhere else is either the safest place in the barony to put it or the most dangerous, and I can't tell you which from inside my own skull. That's the whole reason I want you holding the map and a no in writing that sticks. If it's a leash, it runs both ways or it runs nowhere. Take the stone or don't. I'd think less of you for taking it without asking me that first, and you didn't, so." He left it there.

It was, Emeric thought, the first hour the thing had ever actually been what he'd told himself for eleven days it was. Not six wires. A table. He had built plenty of machines that ran on one hand and called them systems, and this was the first one he had built on purpose to run on six, and the strange part, the part he turned over that night and did not have a clean name for, was how much lighter it was to carry a barony you had finally admitted you could not carry alone.
