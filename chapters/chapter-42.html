Book 2, Chapter 3: The Regent

The south road into the old capital had a trench down the middle of it, and Harrold's carriage went the last half mile from the station at a walk behind a cart of pipe.

It was a quarter past ten on the Thursday. He'd taken the first train up, the half past five, because the sitting was at eleven and Amara had said there was no sense in him lying awake beside her for an hour pretending he'd go on the later one. She'd been right. She usually was about the hours he kept. He'd sat in the end compartment with the regent's case on his knee and the dispatches from the line room on top of it, and read the night's slips twice: the fleet down on the sea, all eight, a mile behind the fish, Imani's count of noses, which was four and then none. Then he'd looked out of the window at the river and thought about a white hull standing over the market at eighteen hundred feet, and about the ring of knights round it holding, on his word, and about a man in a blue coat dropping through the middle of it from the sky, also on his word, more or less.

The trench was three feet wide and five deep, shored with planks, and Weekes's gangs were in it to the shoulder. They'd come up from the river below the town in March and were halfway to the south gate now, two hundred yards a week when the ground was clay and forty when it wasn't. The cart in front stopped. Harrold's driver stopped. Between them, on the planks laid over the trench for a crossing, a man in a green coat with a silver chain on it stood with his hand up, the way a man stands who expects a carriage to stop for him whether it's a king's or not.

It was the regent's, and the man in the green coat knew it.

"Regent," he said, at the window. He had the old capital's way of talking, every word laid down separately like a coin on a counter. "A word, since you're passing. That," and he put his hand out at the trench, without looking at it, "is outside my door."

"So I see."

"It's been outside my door since Monday. My wife can't get the carriage to the step. My cellar has water in it, which it has not had in forty years. The men start at six and they sing." He let that lie. "I'm told it's for a sewer. I'm told the king wants it. I'm told by a man called Weekes, who is not a gentleman and doesn't trouble to pretend to be one, that it'll be done when it's done. I'd like to know from the regent what the king means by it, and when."

Harrold looked past him at the trench. One of Weekes's men was standing in the bottom of it with a level on a plank, frowning at the bubble, and the man beside him was eating bread.

"The king means the river," he said. "It's what the town drinks and it's where the town empties, and they're the same water, and every summer it kills children on this side of the bridge. That's what he means. When, I can't tell you. He isn't here to ask, and I'll not make it up."

The lord's mouth worked. "That's all?"

"That's all I have for the king. For myself..." Harrold considered it honestly. "Your cellar's wet because the trench drains the ground uphill of it. Weekes will know. Ask him to put a sump in, and tell him I said to. He'll do it and complain about it."

The lord stood aside. He didn't do it gracefully, and Harrold didn't need him to. The carriage went on over the planks at a walk, and the gang in the trench stopped singing to watch it go, and started again behind it.

He'd known when he took the seal that it would be like this. The man with the seal answered for what the man without it had decided, and could say nothing for him but the truth, and the truth was mostly "he isn't here". He'd said as much to Emeric, the night they drew up the seal, and Emeric had said yes, that was the price, and he was sorry. Harrold had been regent for nine days. He'd said "he isn't here" a dozen times by his own count, and he'd been glad of the count, because it was something to do with it.

And it was money. He sat back in the carriage. That was the other half of the trench, the half the lord in the green coat hadn't asked about: three feet wide, five deep, a mile and more of it, Weekes's two gangs, the pipe off the Works' kiln at so much a length, and the filter beds after it. Vantic had sent him a sheet on Monday with a figure at the foot that she'd underlined once. Under the figure she'd written, in her small hand, I have an answer to this. You won't like it. Saturday?

He'd written back Saturday, and nothing else.

The sitting went as sittings went. The envoy from Sallow wanted to know whether the king's absence changed the terms on the timber, and was told it didn't. Two lords wanted the old capital's tram, which wasn't begun, and one didn't want it at all. And the cathedral's chaplain read, into the record and very respectfully, the substance of the sermon preached on Sunday from the cathedral's pulpit, which had been on the subject of a king who sailed west into the cursed water, where no ship had ever come back from, and had left his people in the keeping of a man who had once already given his crown away.

Harrold heard it to the end. Then he said that the regent had noted it, and that the king had sailed with a mermaid who'd come out of the cursed water on her own legs and would very likely bring him back the same way, and the room laughed, which it hadn't meant to, and the chaplain went red.

He was on the train home by three. He'd have been on the half past one if Keller hadn't wanted him for the land court's list. He sat with the case on his knee and the window down, and counted the stations to Kaldo, of which there were nine, and thought that this was a week a month, every month, for a year: a week a month he'd be on a train while she was at a desk in the line room, and that he'd had three years of her back, and that he minded.

He didn't mind for the boy. He minded for her.



Vantic came up the castle stair on the Saturday at ten with a case of her own, and with Saban already at the top of it, in the Board room's doorway, where he always was.

She put four papers on the table. Harrold had sat at that table as a guest of his son's more often than he could count, and at the head of it, as regent, three times. He hadn't yet got used to being the one the papers were put in front of. There wasn't a Board to speak of. The six were a thousand miles and more out on the sea, all but one, and the one was in the doorway. There was a clerk with a minute book. There was the regent. There was the regent's signature, which bound.

"Velmoor," said Vantic. "A house you'll not have heard of, which is the point of it. They make dyes, they buy cloth, they have ships. They've written to me three times since the New Year, through my office in the Core, and once through a man who came to the counter in person and asked for me by my mother's name, which I didn't care for." She turned the first paper round. "They want lamps. Bulbs, the forty and the sixty. Glass, the plate and the bottle. They want them in quantity, a year's contract, and they'll pay a quarter over the quay price to have them."

"A quarter."

"Paid through my bank in coin, before anything leaves the quay." She folded her hands. "You'll know why they're asking me and not the Core's houses."

He knew. The six imperial houses had closed their books to Randillia on the first of January, and to Duvrenne with it, and nobody had issued any decree to say so. A merchant in Velmoor who wanted Kaldo's glass had nowhere to borrow against it but the one bank in the world that hadn't followed the others out of the room.

"They're breaking the block," he said.

"Velmoor's a free crown. It never joined the block. Its merchants are breaking their own bankers' good opinion of them, which is their business." She turned the second paper. "This is what the kingdom programme costs to midsummer. This is what the trench costs. This line is the cash I have against both, and this one is the gap."

He read the gap. It was the figure she'd underlined on Monday.

"And if I say no?"

"Then I find it elsewhere," said Vantic. "Which I can. There's a house in Solreth that'll lend it to me on the crown's paper at a rate I'd be ashamed to tell Ferra, with a clause that lets them call it the day the fleet's reported lost. The fleet won't be reported lost. They'll report it anyway, when it suits them, and then they'll call it." She said it in the same voice as everything else. "That's the other answer. I told you you wouldn't like this one. You'll like that one less."

Harrold looked at the doorway.

"Say it," he said.

Saban came in off the threshold, which he didn't often do. He didn't sit. "The lamps don't bother me," he said. "They've had lamps for years. Every pedlar on the coast road sells our lamps. The bulbs, the same. What bothers me is strangers on the quay the year the fleet's away. A Velmoor factor in Kaldo is a Velmoor factor in the customs house, and in the taverns on the quay, and in Bryda's yard asking what the big shed's for. I don't know who he drinks with when he goes home. I'd rather not open that door this year."

"And if I open it with a lock on it?"

"Then it's a door with a lock on it," said Saban. "It's still a door."

Harrold took the third paper, which was Vantic's terms, and read them through once, slowly, and then took up the pen.

He'd sat at enough tables, in thirty years, to know what a bad bargain looked like a year after it was signed. It looked like a gap somebody had walked through, and everybody saying afterwards that it had been obvious. He wanted this one, if it went wrong, to go wrong some other way. So he set it out as he wanted it, down the margin, and read each line aloud for the clerk as he wrote it.

"One year. A break clause the crown can use on a day's notice and give no cause." He wrote. "Nothing that isn't on the release ladder already. Lamps, bulbs, plate glass, bottle glass. Nothing new, nothing added by letter." He wrote. "All sales on our quay, inside the customs house. No Velmoor factor past the customs house door. If they want to see the Works they can look at the chimney from the boat." He wrote. "Every factor named to us in advance, by name and house and where he sleeps, and Saban's people to see him before he lands. Payment in full before loading. And no substitute for a named man, ever, except by the regent's written exception for cause." He looked up. "Written by me. Not by my clerk."

"They'll take it," said Vantic. "They'd take worse."

"Then they'll take this." He signed it. The seal went down beside the signature, and stood, and the clerk blotted it.

"And the objection," said Harrold. "In the minute. As he said it. Strangers on the quay the year the fleet's away, a door he'd rather not open. And my answer." He thought. "That a door with a lock and a man on it is better than no money. Put that."

The clerk put it. Saban watched him put it, and then nodded, once, to nobody in particular, which was as near as Saban came to being pleased about something he'd lost.

"You'll tell the king?" said Vantic, putting the papers away.

"I'll tell him after."

She looked at him, a banker's look, quick and entire. "Not ask."

"It's signed," said Harrold. "There's nothing to ask."

He sent it that night by the line room, in Ferra's cipher, eight lines. The answer came back on the Sunday from the K Amara, with the fleet's position, and it was from Ferra and not from his son, and it said, F.: Read. The break clause is good. Name the factors to me as well when you have them. And under it, in a different hand, sent separately: E.: Thank you for telling me.

He read the second line twice. It was very nearly a thing his son would have said to his face.



The Kaldo News, Saturday 25 April, year 17

Seen over the market. The king, who sailed with the fleet on the fourteenth, was seen over the city on Wednesday afternoon by about four hundred people, beside a Church hull that was in rather a hurry to leave. Twenty of the home knights went up round the hull first, by order of the regent. The castle says the king is with the fleet. Readers have written in with three explanations: a fast ship, a man who looks like him, or that he never went. The castle declines to choose.

Where the fleet is. West, says the castle. That's all, says the castle.

Found. A red and yellow kite, on the shoal, Thursday. Apply at the harbour office.



The lord from the capital came down on the Sunday afternoon about a mill. He had money in a paper mill on the river above Wayford and wanted to see it run, and he took a room at an inn on the Gate Avenue because it was said to be the best house in Kaldo, and at six that evening he walked down the Gate Avenue to Tamsy Brook's eating house below the castle, because that was said to be the best table.

Harrold and Amara were at the corner of the terrace, under the curtain wall, where they sat on a Sunday when he was home.

He didn't see the man come in. Amara did. She saw everything that came through a door; it was what the years of not existing had made of a woman who'd been a tower mage before that. She put her cup down, not hurriedly, and said, "Don't turn round. There's a man by the glass doors who knows my face."

He didn't turn round. "Who?"

"A lord's son. From the capital. He came to the tower with his father once when he was a boy. He's gone white." She picked up the cup again. "He's going to sit down. He's sitting down. He's not looking at us."

"Is he going to say anything?"

"Not to us. Not in here." She drank. "He'll ask somebody. Later. Somewhere he thinks is quiet."

Harrold sat with his back to the room and his hands on the table and looked at her across it. She was in a grey dress with a high collar, which was what she wore for the town, and her gloves, and her hair under a net. In the lamplight she passed. Strangers saw a tall woman with a mage's bearing and a stillness to her, and went on. To anyone who'd known the queen at the tower she was the queen's face on a woman who'd outlived her, beside a man who'd once been king and now looked thirty, and you could take your pick of what that meant, and the old court would take all of them.

"I could go over," he said.

"And say what?"

He couldn't think what.

"Eat your fish," said Amara.

The man asked Saban on the Monday morning. He found him in the customs house, of all places, where Saban was watching the clerks set out the new desk for the Velmoor factors, and he waited until the clerks had gone for the trestles and then asked it in a lowered voice, with a coin in his fingers that he didn't quite offer. Who was the lady at Tamsy Brook's, with the regent?

Saban told Harrold about it the same afternoon. He told it standing in the doorway of the long room, with his hat on.

"And you said?"

"I smiled at him," said Saban. "I'm good at it. I've practised."

"That's all?"

"That's all I ever say. If he asks her, it's hers to say what she says, and she'll say people tell her she looks like the queen, and smile, and he'll go home and tell his wife the old king's keeping company with a woman who looks like his dead wife, and his wife will tell her sister, and by Lantern Night the whole capital will think you're a sad old man with a stone in him and a fancy." He touched his hat. "Which is a scandal. A scandal's safe. People love a scandal. Nobody looks under one."

Rowena's view came by the field set that evening, at eight, as she'd promised Emeric she'd call whenever she liked. She called the line room and had herself put through to the regent, ahead of anybody but the regent, which was the arrangement, and Harrold took it at the desk in the line room with Amara three chairs off.

"I hear," said Rowena, from Emberfell, very clear down the wire, "that the old king has been seen dining with a lady."

"News travels."

"It came by his family's cook to my cook. It arrived before the mail." A pause. "Is she pretty?"

"Rowena."

"I only ask because the capital will. I've been asked twice since dinner by women who've never written to me in their lives. I've told them both that I've never seen the lady and that I'm sure she has excellent taste in men." Another pause, slightly different. "Give her my regards, Harrold. Tell her I'm glad somebody's eating with you. You were always a poor hand at eating alone."

She rang off. Harrold put the handset down.

"What did she say?" said Amara.

"She said to give you her regards."

Amara was quiet a while, with the board's lamps going green and steady behind her, every drum on the chain beating, and something in her made face moved that hadn't been made to.

"Tell her I said thank you," she said. "When you next speak. And that it's hers to say who the lady is. Not mine."



The stewards' reports came in on the Monday, as they did every Monday, from the four holdings and the Works and the yard, in their buff covers, and Harrold read them at the long table with Corbin across from him, because that was how it had settled.

Nobody had decided it. Corbin had the box. The box had papers in it every morning, and some of the papers were about the holdings, and the holdings sent their stewards' reports to the regent on Mondays; and on the first Monday Corbin had come up from the box room with a question about one of them and stayed to answer it, and on the second he'd come up with the box under his arm and the reports already sorted, and sat down without asking. He sat on the window side, in his grey coat, with a pencil behind his ear that he used to make small marks in the margins in a hand so neat it looked printed.

He was good at it. That was the thing Harrold kept coming back to. He'd watched his eldest son for twenty years be bad at a great many things, loudly, in public, and here he was being good at something quietly in a room with nobody to see but his father. He read fast. He found the line that didn't add. He knew, by the second week, that Milford's steward rounded his bushels down so that next month would look better, and that the yard's foreman wrote "on schedule" when he meant "not yet behind".

"Wayford," said Corbin. "The main's through to the mill. They want a second pump crew. They've put it as a request and costed it as a certainty."

"Do they need it?"

"Yes. They'll need it in June. They've asked in April so they can be refused once and ask again." He made one of his marks. "I'd give it them now and save the letter."

"Give it them now."

Corbin put it on the slip for the box. Harrold read the Works' report. Bram's foreman kept it in Bram's absence, and kept it, as near as Harrold could tell, the way Bram would have spoken it, in the fewest words that couldn't be complained about. Line four said that Tess had walked the Motion Shop's floor on Tuesday and Thursday without the stick, the full length, and had stopped at every bench, and that Wren had come behind her with the books and the second volume of the gearing. It said nothing else about it. It didn't need to.

The door opened on a knock and Declan came in, in his blues, with a cap under his arm.

"Regent. Sir." To Corbin, a nod, exactly the depth of the nod Corbin was owed and no deeper. "Osric's compliments. He'd like the home knights' winter drill roster from the autumn, the one Ward left, for the cold water. He means to start them on it in June, on the river, before the sea warms."

"Before the sea warms," said Harrold.

"Ward's order was that the cold drill's done in cold water, sir. Osric reads that strictly."

Corbin had already got up. He went to the press against the wall, where the knights' files were, and found it without looking long, and gave it to Declan in its folder, and Declan took it and looked at Corbin for a second as he took it, as a man looks at a door that's opened the other way from the way it always used to.

"Thank you," said Declan.

"It wants the last page," said Corbin. "Ward wrote the river times on the back of the last page. They're not on the front."

Declan turned to the last page, and read it, and nodded, and went.

Harrold watched the door close. He'd sent that man up on Wednesday with nineteen others to stand round a Church hull in the air over the market, on his own word, with the king somewhere in the sky above them. He'd sent him up a minute after the hull went under the line. He'd have sent him up a minute after that whether the king came or not. Declan had held, and come home, and today had come for a roster.

Across the table Corbin had gone back to the Wayford slip. His head was down. The bonded stone had put weight on his shoulders and colour in his face, and there was nothing round his ankle now under the grey cloth, nothing at all. Five weeks. More. And he didn't know.

Harrold knew. He'd known since the K Discovery's hold, five miles up, with Amara beside him, when his younger son had told them both about the leash, and why. He'd promised then. He'd promised Emeric that Corbin would hear it from Emeric's own mouth, with his father in the room, when Emeric was home to say it, and not before and not from anyone else. He'd promised it in front of his wife.

He sat across the table from his son and kept it.

"Milford," said Corbin. "He's rounded the bushels down again."

"Has he."

"By a sixth. I'd let him. He likes having a good May."

"Let him," said Harrold, and turned the page.



At Ostrel the officer gave his report standing, because nobody had told him to sit.

The room was high and plain and smelled of the lamp oil they used on that floor, which was not the oil they used downstairs. Six men sat at a table along one wall with the light behind them. He'd met four of them. The fifth he knew by his chain. The sixth sat a little apart, at the end, with his back to the window, and didn't move.

He told it as he'd written it. The hull had gone in along the Randillian coast on the Wednesday on the lawful lamp, as ordered. It had passed the point at three thousand and been seen by the towers; he'd felt them take it, the way you feel a man look at you across a street. It had turned along the coast to the town and gone down to eighteen hundred over the market, as ordered, and stood there. The knights had come up at once. Twenty. They'd held a ring at two hundred yards and done nothing else, very well. And then the king had come down through the ring onto the after deck and spoken to him.

"You're sure," said the man with the chain.

"I've seen him twice, at the capital. It was his face. His voice. He knew my rank without being told it. He mentioned a letter his master..." He corrected himself. "A letter he'd sent our master in February, and said he'd read it again that morning and it said what it said."

Nobody at the table looked at the end of it.

"Then he never sailed," said one of the four. "It's a story. The fleet went and the king stayed, and they put it about that he'd gone, to see who'd move. And we moved."

"Eight ships went west on the fourteenth," said another. "Our people at Sallow counted them. He was seen on the forward deck of the small grey one. By three of ours, with glasses."

"A man in a coat on a deck. They've a tailor."

"Then a fast hull. He went with them a day or two, for the pulpits, and came back on one of the new ones, in the night. They've built hulls we've never seen. We know that much."

"Or a man who looks like him. They've had three years and the king's father's money."

The officer stood and let them have it out. He'd had the voyage home to think, and these were the three he'd thought of first, too, and he'd thought each of them through and found something wrong with each, and he wasn't sure that what was wrong with them wasn't simply that he'd been frightened.

"His coat was dry," he said, when there was room.

They looked at him.

"There'd been rain on the coast that morning. Our deck was wet. His coat was dry, and his boots, and he came down from above the knights. Not from the land. Not from the sea. From above." He hesitated. "I looked up."

"And?"

"There's a thing they keep over the town. In the Band. Anybody with a good glass can see it on a clear day. A fleck. It's always been there, since the war. I saw the fleck." He stopped. "That's all I saw."

The man at the end of the table moved, then, for the first time. He didn't turn from the window. He only raised one hand a little off the arm of his chair, and the table went quiet, quite quickly, all down its length.

"The thing over the town," said the pope. He had an ordinary voice, low and level. "How high does it sit?"

The officer had the answer. He'd taken it with the sextant himself, from the after deck, while the king's back was turned. "Five miles, Holiness. A little over."

The hand went down again.

"Thank you," said the pope. "That's all."

They went out, all seven of them, the officer last. On the stair, the man with the chain caught his sleeve and said in a low voice that it had been a good report, and a careful one, and that he wasn't to think about it any further than he'd already thought; and the officer said no, of course not.

At the turn of the stair he looked back up. The door at the top was shut. Behind it, as far as he could tell, nobody had moved at all.
