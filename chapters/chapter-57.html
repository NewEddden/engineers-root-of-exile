Book 2, Chapter 18: Under Is Teeth

The watch let them swim on the Sunday afternoon, inside the ring on the gate side, between two marker floats a quarter of a mile apart, and Zale went into the water off the bottom step at a quarter past two in the Mark II suit and didn't think about anything at all for the best hour he'd had since the wall.

He'd wanted it for a month. Longer. He'd wanted it since the winter, if he was honest, since the tank on the Cradle and the first time the suit had held him under for an hour without a shiver; and before that, since the beach at South Sentinel, and a grey-white thing in the shallows that had looked up at him out of the wash with eyes he couldn't read. He'd been on the sea his whole working life. He'd never been in it. Not like this. Not with the slip panels on, so that when he asked the water to give way along his arms it gave, and he went through the green like a thrown knife; not with the warm layer off and the cool of the caldera pressing in through the rubber, clean and clear; and not with her.

Ilenne swam beside him with her tail. She didn't swim as he did. She didn't seem to swim at all. She went where she was going, and the water went round her, and he went after her as hard as he could, kicking and asking and kicking, and every so often she turned and came back and went round him once, slowly, close, with her hair out behind her like weed in a stream, and looked at him through his visor with her colour held and her eyes laughing; and went off again.

He knew he was slow. He didn't care.

The gate side of the ring was the clean side. The water was green and bright down to thirty feet and then green and dim below that, and the floor fell away under them into a blue that went down and down to the glow; and the glow came up through it, faint, gold, from the grove a long way under. He could see the inner wall going down beside them, black, with weed on it and shells, and holes in it where fish lived that were bigger than fish should be. He saw a thing like a ray go past below him the size of a cart, flat and slow, and didn't mind it. He saw Ilenne put her hand out to a shoal of small silver fish as she passed and saw the whole shoal turn after her hand like a flag in a wind. He'd seen her do it in the bay at South Sentinel and in the tank on the Cradle and he'd thought it a trick. He thought now, watching her do it at home, that it was more like a woman stopping in a street to say good morning to somebody she'd known all her life.

On the gate steps, forty yards off, his brother was sitting on the dry with his boots off and his feet in the water, watching. Zale could see him every time he came up near the top. A small figure on the black stone with his shirt sleeves rolled up. Maeve beside him, on her back on the warm step with her arm over her eyes. A watch boat lying off the nearer float with two of Taurre's people in it, also watching, also, Zale thought, bored.

He went down again. He went down to thirty feet beside the wall and hung there in the green with the cold through the suit and his bonded stone doing whatever it did, holding him, so that the press of the water above him was a thing he could feel and not a thing that hurt; and he thought that if he could stay down here he'd be quite happy never to see a deck again, and was surprised to find that he meant it.

Ilenne came past him from above and turned, and her colour moved.

Not the blue. Not anything he'd seen on her before. It went over her all at once, light-dark-light, the flicker, fast, and then slate, hard, from the throat down. Her eyes weren't on him. They were past him. Below him.

He looked down.



It came up out of the blue.

He didn't see it at first, because it was too big to see. He saw the blue go dark, under him, in a patch, and the patch get bigger, and kept thinking it was a cloud's shadow until he remembered there was no sun down here to cast one. Then the dark had edges. Then it had a shape: a long blunt shape, broad, rising, slowly, straight up toward him, and it was the size of a hull. It was the size of his own ship. It came up out of the deep water under him without hurrying, as a whale comes up, and as it came he saw that it had a mouth, and the mouth was opening.

Under is teeth. Ilenne had said it at the primer, and again on the aft deck: the third courtesy's reason. Never come up under anybody. Because that's how a hunter comes. Under is teeth.

He couldn't move. He thought later that he must have moved, because he was alive; but at the time he hung there at thirty feet in the green with the slip panels doing nothing because he'd forgotten to ask them anything, and watched it come.

On the steps, Emeric was on his feet.

Zale didn't see that. He was told afterwards. Maeve told him: that his brother had come up off the step so fast she'd felt the stone move, and stood at the water's edge with his whole body rigid and his face gone grey, staring at a place in the sea forty yards off where nothing at all was showing yet. Because he'd felt it. Through the water, through the rock, he'd felt the thing coming up. And he couldn't read it. Maeve said that afterwards too, and so did Emeric, that night, flatly, to the table: that it had come into his sense like a hill coming up under a fog, so big and so dense that it filled the whole of what he could feel in that water, and he couldn't feel past it. Couldn't feel Zale beside it. Couldn't feel whether there was anybody else down there with it, or behind it, or what it wanted, or anything but that it was there and coming up and enormous. It swamped him. He stood on the step blind with all his sense open and could do nothing at all.

Ilenne turned it.

Zale saw that. He saw her go past him downward, toward it, with her tail driving, so fast the water roared in his ears; saw her stop, twenty feet below him, between him and the mouth, with her arms out; and saw her speak.

He couldn't hear it. Nobody could have heard it. But he felt it in the water the way you feel a bell rung in a church you're standing in, through your feet: a long low sound, not a word, a sound that went down and down into the blue and that his chest heard rather than his ears. She held it. She held it and the thing came on and she held it, and her colour was going all over her, every colour, faster than he'd ever seen it, as if she were saying something with her whole body as well as her mouth.

The thing stopped.

It didn't stop at once. It came on another yard, two, the mouth still opening, and he saw into it, and wished he hadn't; and then it slowed, and hung, so close under Ilenne that its blunt snout was a yard below her hands. And it turned. Slowly. It rolled away to one side, the whole vast length of it going past under him like a wall going by, and as it went, its flank came up and caught him.

It wasn't a blow. It was the side of it, turning, slow as a barge, and he was in the way. It took him across the ribs on the left side and threw him through the water ten feet and he felt two things go in his chest like sticks, one, two, and the suit tear along his side from the armpit to the hip, and the cold come in. Then it was past. Then it was below, going down, back into the blue, getting smaller, a dark patch and then a shadow and then nothing.

Ilenne had him by the arm before he'd stopped tumbling. She came up under his shoulder, not under him, beside, and took him up through the green toward the light, and he let her, because there was nothing in his chest but a white noise of pain and he couldn't have kicked if he'd wanted to.

They came up at the bottom step.

Emeric was in the water to his waist, in his shirt, reaching. He had Zale under the arms and up onto the step before Zale could tell him he was all right, and Zale wasn't all right, so it was as well he didn't try. He lay on his back on the wet black stone with the sky going round over him and the torn suit flapping and his chest on fire, and heard his brother's voice say his name twice, quite steadily, and then Maeve's say something about Imani, and running feet.

"Abloh," said Zale, to the sky, "is going to kill me." The suit's side flapped open. Nobody laughed.

Ilenne was in the water beside the step. Her colour was still going. She had her hand on his arm, on the stone, and she didn't take it off.

"It wasn't hunting," she said.

Zale turned his head and looked at her.

"It wasn't," said Ilenne. Her voice was shaking. He'd never heard her voice shake. "I know hunting. I met one. On the way. The hungry one, that took the anchor and the rudder and broke the boy's leg. That one didn't listen. It took anything. It didn't want anything but to eat." She swallowed. "This one listened. I spoke and it listened. It stopped. A hunter doesn't stop."

"Then what was it doing?"

Ilenne didn't answer at once. She was looking down into the water, at the place where the thing had gone.

"It had a picture," she said. "They tell you. In pictures. What they're doing, what they want. Not words. This one..." She stopped. "It had a shape. A long thing. With a smell of the dry on it. A land smell. A thing that doesn't belong in the water." Her hand tightened on his arm. "It was going for that. Only that. Not me. It didn't care about me. It came up for the shape it had, and the shape was you."

The sky had stopped going round. Zale lay and looked up at it.

"That's what I felt," said Ilenne. "That's what it told me. That's what I know." Her colour had steadied a little. Not much. "And I think something else. I don't know it. I think it." She took a breath. "Animals here don't hunt a shape they never met. Never. Something gave it that one. Somebody. Somebody who talks to them." She looked at him. "And the only ones I know who talk to them are mine."

"Who?"

"I don't know," said Ilenne. "I can't say who. Nobody can." She shook her head, once. "But not a land man. Not you. Mine."

Emeric was kneeling on the step beside him with his hand flat on the torn suit over the ribs, not pressing, and Zale looked up at his face and saw that it was perfectly still, and that it was the stillest he'd ever seen it.



At dusk, with his ribs strapped by Imani on the bottom step and a shot of something bitter in him that she said would help and didn't, he went across to his ship for the post.

Imani told him not to. Emeric didn't tell him anything; he looked at him, and Zale looked back, and that was that. Ilenne rowed him across, because he couldn't pull an oar, and he climbed the ship's ladder one rung at a time and felt every rung go through the cracked ribs like a hot wire, and didn't say so.

They were all at the rail. Every one of them, watching the ladder. They'd heard. The whole gate had heard; the watch boat had seen it and the watch boat had told the gate and the gate had told Zale's ship before Imani had finished strapping him. Hesk was at the front of them. He looked at Zale coming up the ladder slowly, one rung at a time, with his arm held in against his side, and his face did something complicated.

"You're late," said Hesk.

"I was swimming," said Zale. "Read the post, Linnet."

He sat down on the hatch cover very carefully, and Linnet read the day, and his crew stood round him and didn't look at his ribs, and at the end of it he went back down the side to his boat, and Ilenne rowed him back across to the steps, because Imani wanted the station's healers to see the ribs before he went anywhere near the stages.



Imani saw the ribs properly at the station that evening, because she wanted the healers' eyes on them as well as her own, and because the station's bell had the best light in Aumere for looking at bruises.

Two of them. The seventh and eighth on the left, the same two Emeric had cracked in the Band three weeks ago, which she thought was either a coincidence or a family failing. Cracked, not broken through. Nothing shifted. Nothing in the lung. She'd felt them on the step and she felt them again on the station's table with the eldest healer's hands beside hers, and the eldest nodded slowly and said through Ilenne that the land man was lucky, and that the thing that did it must have been very big and very slow, because a thing that size moving fast would have killed him.

"It was," said Zale, from the table, with his eyes shut. "Both."

Ilenne took him through to the bell's other room when it was done, to sit with him while the healers made him drink something that smelled of kelp. And Imani was alone at the long table, washing her hands in the basin, when the deep-house woman came and stood beside her.

Orrane.

She'd been at the station all evening, as she had on the night of Haurin, carrying and fetching at the far end of the bell. Imani had seen her and not looked at her. She'd felt her watching again: the same intent look on Imani's hands, on what Imani did. Now she stood a yard off at the table's end with her own hands folded in front of her, and waited until Imani looked up.

Then she spoke.

She didn't have Kaldo. Imani didn't have her language; forty words, perhaps, from the aft deck and the primer, and the hand signs. So Orrane didn't use her language either. She used the words she could see Imani had. Slowly, one at a time, with her hands. Imani understood that a minute in: that the woman had been watching her for two evenings not only to see what kind of healer she was, but to learn which of the primer's words she knew.

A word, and a sign. Bound. Her two hands brought together, palm to palm, the binding.

Then she pointed at the door to the bell's other room, where Zale was, and Ilenne. The land man. The husband.

Then a word Imani knew from Ilenne's own page, the one Ferra had ruled in its column in the word room: dead. And the hands came apart. Opened. One of them went up, flat, and away, as you'd let a bird go.

Free.

Imani stood with her wet hands over the basin and didn't move.

Orrane went on. She did it the way, Imani thought afterwards, a woman tells a doctor how a thing is done in her house: plainly, without colour, as a set of facts the doctor needs. The hand across the eyes, slate, fear, and then the sign pushed away from her, hard: if it were done openly, the bound one would never forgive it. That was how Imani read it, and she wasn't sure. So, a finger to the lips, the sign for quiet: it's done quietly. Then the one word Imani had for the sea, and a hand tipping over, slowly, like a boat going over: a thing in the water. An accident. Then a hand flat, low, moving side to side, and a small shake of the head: most never do it. Most. Then the hand closed, slowly, on nothing, and held, and drawn in to the chest. Some do. Some who believe a person should be theirs.

She didn't point at anyone. She didn't name a house. Of herself she gave nothing at all.

When she'd finished she stood still with her hands folded, and her colour was grey and level and held, and Imani saw at the edge of it, just at the throat, something that wasn't held at all, and was gone at once.

"Why are you telling me?" said Imani.

She said it in Kaldo. Orrane didn't understand the words. She understood the question; Imani saw her understand it. She looked at the door to the other room, where the land man was sitting with his ribs strapped and the queen's daughter beside him. And she put her hand up, flat, and laid it against her own throat, lightly, which Imani had not once seen anyone here do, and took it away.

The blue. At the station, the week before, when Zale touched Ilenne's shoulder. The colour she couldn't hold for him.

Then Orrane turned and went back to the far end of the bell, and picked up a basin, and went on with her work.

Imani dried her hands. She took the card out of her coat, not Ilenne's card, a fresh one, and sat down at the end of the long table, and wrote it in her clinical hand, as she'd have written a history taken from a relative at a bedside.

24 May, the station, about 8 p.m. From Orrane (widow, a deep house; Ilenne's word). Given in signs and a handful of primer words, no interpreter. My reading of it, not checked. A bound one whose partner dies is free. Done openly, the bound one would never forgive it (my reading; her sign was the slate); so it is done quietly, in the water, as an accident. Most never do it. Some, who believe a person should be theirs, do. She would not say who, or which house, or anything of herself. She touched her own throat and looked at his door. Cannot verify. Witness had no reason I can see to lie.

She read it twice. Then she put it inside her coat, on the left, against the revolver.



She told them in the hard room at ten that night: Zale, and Ferra, and Emeric, round the table under the cold lamps, with the pumps ticking at the far end and the others asleep on their pallets along the walls. Ilenne had gone to the head of the stages to wait for Zale, as she did every night.

She told it as she'd written it. She took the card out and read it aloud, every line, including the lines that said what she didn't know, and then she put it down on the table in front of them and stopped.

Nobody spoke for a while.

Then Zale laughed. Not much. It hurt him to do it; she saw it go through the ribs.

"Widow-making," he said. "They've a custom for widow-making."

It was a joke. For about one second it was a joke, the way he said it, light and dry, a sailor's word for a sailor's thing, the kind of name you'd give a bad current or a reef that had had three ships. Then she watched it stop being one. She watched him hear what he'd said. His face didn't change, much. But the laugh went out of it and didn't come back, and he sat at the table with his hand over his strapped ribs and looked at the card.

"The beast," said Ferra quietly.

"The beast," said Zale. "With my shape on it." He was quiet. "In the water. An accident." He looked up. "That's what this afternoon was. Isn't it."

Nobody answered him.

"And the hand at the door," said Zale. "The water. That was the open road. Saying I'm nothing, so the binding's nothing. And the court. And the motion." He nodded slowly. "And now this. The quiet one."

He sat a little longer. Then he got up from the table, carefully, with his hand on its edge, and stood.

"She knew," he said.

Imani looked up at him.

"Ilenne," said Zale. "She's known all along. She must have. She grew up down there." His voice was very even. "She knew there was a quiet road, and she knew it ended at me, and she married me at a door with it in her pocket and never said."

He went to the bell door.

"Zale," said Emeric.

"I'm going down," said Zale. "I'm going to ask her." He didn't turn round. "It's my bell. It's my floor. I'm allowed."



He came up out of the last pool into the bell in the queen's house at half past ten with Ilenne's hand in his, because there was no other way down, and the first thing he did when he was standing on the glass was take his hand out of hers.

She looked at the hand, and then at him.

The two pallets were on the floor where they'd been every night, a hand's width apart. The chain went down through the middle of the floor into the glow. Outside the water door, in the open sea, Thalune was at her post, keeping her place with slow movements of her tail, her face turned to the sheet, her colour the colour of the water.

"Did you know?" said Zale.

Ilenne didn't ask what. He saw her not ask. He saw that she knew exactly what he meant, and that she'd known what he'd come down to say from the moment he came for her at the head of the stages, and possibly from the moment the thing came up out of the blue.

"Yes," she said.

"When?"

"Always." She stood on the glass in her wet dress with her arms at her sides. "A child. At court. Every child of the court knows it. Nobody tells. Nobody says. You know it from how the widows are looked at. From what the women don't talk about when the girls are in the room." She held his eyes. "Nobody taught me. Everybody knew."

"And on the ship? The whole way out. You said the house, you said you ran from a binding, you said once until one dies..." He stopped. "You never said that. That there's a way to make it end. That it's me."

"No."

"Why?"

She was quiet.

"The same as the name," she said. "The house's name. I didn't say it on the sea. Because saying it out there made it real out there. And out there was the only place it wasn't." Her voice was very low. "On the ship it was only a thing at home. Far away. If I said it, it was on the ship with us."

"It was on the ship with us anyway."

"Yes."

"And when we got here? At the door? After?" He heard his own voice going up and brought it down. "You could have told me the first night. Here. On this floor. You told me about the water, the season, the call. You told me everything about the open road. You never told me about the other."

"I thought there was time." She said it steadily. "I thought the house plays the open road first. It always plays the open road first. The court, the motion, the season. Months. I thought, I tell him when we're inside, when I can watch his back myself, when I can see the water round him." She stopped. "The beast came first."

Zale stood on the glass and looked at her.

"You didn't even tell me to watch the water," he said.

That was the one. He saw it land. He saw that it was the one she'd been waiting for, since the afternoon; that she'd had answers ready for all the others, true ones, and none for this.

"No," she said.

"Why not? Not the custom. Not the whole of it. Just, watch the water, Zale. Swim where the watch can see you. Don't go down alone." His voice cracked on the last word and he let it. "One line. You could have said one line."

Ilenne didn't look away. That was the thing. She didn't look away and she didn't hold her colour and she didn't make up a reason.

"I don't have a good answer," she said. "I don't have one. I've looked for one since the water. There isn't one." She took a breath. "I was wrong. I should have said it. At least that. I was wrong."

He believed her. That was the worst of it. He stood on the glass floor of her mother's house with his ribs on fire and believed every word, and it didn't help at all.

"You let me walk in blind," he said.

"Yes."

"I'd have walked in anyway. You know I would. If you'd told me the whole of it in the wall water I'd have walked in anyway, I'd have married you at that door anyway, I'd have..." He stopped. "That's not the point."

"No."

"That I'd have done it anyway doesn't fix that you didn't tell me."

"No," said Ilenne. "It doesn't."

Her colour broke.

He saw it go. She'd been holding it, all through, he realised, even while she said she was wrong; holding it hard, as she held it at court, as she'd held it on the steps at the call. And it went. All at once. Not the blue. Everything. It went over her from the throat to the hands in a long breaking wave, grey and dark and the blue and something he'd never seen and didn't have a name for, colour after colour, as if every one of them she'd held for a month had come up at once; and she stood there in it with her arms at her sides and her face wet and didn't try to stop it.

Outside the water door, in the open sea, Thalune watched.

Zale saw her. Over Ilenne's shoulder, through the sheet. The court's eyes, at her post, her face turned to the door, her own colour as still as the water round her. She couldn't hear a word of it. Words didn't come through water from air. But she could see everything. She could see the heir of the queen's house standing in front of her land husband with her colour broken all over her and not holding it. Not hiding it. For him.

By the next sitting, he thought, every hall in the city would have it.

He didn't go to her. He wanted to. He stood on the glass with his hands at his sides and wanted to cross the hand's width and the yard beyond it and put his arms round her, and he didn't, because the thing between them wasn't fixed and he wasn't going to pretend it was with his arms. He didn't know if that was right. He knew it was what he was doing.

After a long time her colour came down. Slowly. Not all the way.

She lay down on her pallet, on her side, facing the chain.

Zale took the blanket from the foot of the other one and went to the far side of the chain, three yards off, where he'd lain the first night, and lay down on the glass with it over him and his arm under his head and his ribs telling him exactly what they thought of it.

The first night it had been funny. Ferra had asked if the floor was level. Imani had offered him a cushion.

He lay on the glass in the glow with the chain going down past his face, and it wasn't funny at all.



Imani found Emeric at the table at midnight, still sitting where Zale had left him, with the card in front of him.

Ferra was there too. She hadn't gone to bed either. She was sitting with her book shut in front of her and her hands flat on it, not writing anything, which was how Imani knew how bad it was.

"Sit down," said Emeric.

She sat.

He said it to the two of them, quietly, with the pumps ticking. He'd been thinking it through since the step, she could tell; he said it in order, like a list, as he said things he'd made rules about saying before they happened.

"If this is the custom," he said, "then this afternoon was the first. Not the last. There'll be more. Each one an accident. Each one something nobody can put a name to. A thing in the water. A bad stretch on the stages. A dome that fails." He turned the card a little on the table with one finger. "It'll be Zale every time. Not Ilenne. Not me. Zale. Because if anybody touches Ilenne, or me, it's a war, and we've just spent a fortnight showing them what that would look like without anybody saying the word. So nobody will. They'll do it to him. A widow's free." He was quiet. "And the realer it looks between them, the more it's worth doing."

"And tonight," said Ferra, "Thalune's watching them in that bell."

"Yes."

"Who gave it the shape?" said Imani.

"I don't know." He said it flatly. "Ilenne doesn't. Nobody does. Somebody who talks to animals, and they're hers, and that's all anybody can say. I'm not going to pretend to know more than that. Not to you. Not to anybody."

He didn't say what he'd like to do to whoever it was. He sat at the table under the cold lamp with his hands still on either side of the card and his face as level as Taurre's, and didn't say it.

Imani saw it anyway. She'd seen it on the step that afternoon, kneeling beside him on the wet stone with Zale's torn suit under her hands: the thing in his face that he wasn't letting out. She saw it now, in the lamplight, behind his eyes, steady, and very cold.

She put her hand over his on the table.

He let her.
