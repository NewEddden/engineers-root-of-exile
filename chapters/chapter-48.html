Chapter 48 - The Freed Hand

The covenant ended not with a signature but with a subtraction, a single line struck through in Vantic's own hand on the schedule she had kept for a year, and Emeric watched her draw it and understood that this was the only ceremony the thing would ever have. There was no seal to break and no bell to ring. A building freeze is a clause, and a clause dies when the arithmetic it rests on changes, and the arithmetic had changed the moment two forfeited baronies came onto his books with their revenues attached, so that the debt which had sat all year at the edge of default now sat, instead, current. Vantic read the new totals twice, because Vantic read everything twice, and then she struck the line that had written a year of his life, and said, in the flat voice of a woman closing a matter rather than granting a favor, that the covenant's condition was satisfied and its restriction accordingly lapsed. She did not congratulate him. It would not have occurred to her. She had lent against a wager and the wager had come in, and a creditor does not congratulate the weather.

He had waited for this the way a man waits out a fever, and he was careful, even now, even with the line struck, to hear exactly what she had said and not the larger thing he wanted to hear. The covenant had lifted. The debt had not cleared. Those were two different sentences, and he made himself hold them apart, because Ferra had drilled into him across a hundred evenings that the most dangerous number in any ledger is the one a hopeful man rounds, and he did not intend to round this one. He still owed House Ducatis a sum that would have bought the barony he started with outright. What had changed was only that he was no longer forbidden, by the terms of owing it, from building. The leash had not been cut. It had merely, for the first time in a year, gone slack, and a slack leash and no leash feel identical to the animal until the moment it does not, which was a distinction Emeric understood in the abstract and was about to spend a great deal of money proving he did not understand in his chest.

What the freed hand reached for first was the river. He had chosen the ground months ago, standing on the bank below the rapids where the water came down fast and cold and did work for free that a hundred men could not, and he had held the whole district in his head since, unable to lay a single stone of it, turning it over on sleepless nights the way another man turns over a grudge. A foundry at the rapids, fed by the Grayrock ore now moving again on the mended road. Glasshouses downstream of it, because glass was the true wealth, not the iron, and he knew it even if no one around him yet did, spectacles and mirrors and clear panes that any lord in the kingdom would beggar himself to own. Kilns. A millrace. A row of honest houses for the people who would work them, built first and built well, because a company town that was a trap would rot from the inside and he needed this one to last past his own lifetime. He had the drawings. He had, after a year, the permission. He had, God help him, the money, or enough of it, and the having of all three at once produced in him a feeling he distrusted precisely because it felt so much like being right.

The temptation was speed, and the trap in the temptation was that speed and correctness wore, for once, the same face. Every argument for building fast was a good argument. Idle ground earned nothing and the debt did not sleep. The absorbed baronies needed work put into willing hands before the inherited opportunists filled the vacuum with something worse. Winter came whether or not his kilns were roofed. Each of these was true, and he lined them up and found them sound, and did not notice that he had assembled, out of true premises honestly held, the exact case a frightened man watching from a neighboring hall would read as its own kind of confession. He had spent the whole audit teaching those men that the lawful road to stopping him ran nowhere. Now, with the paper barely dry, he proposed to pour a year's worth of suppressed ambition into the ground all at once, in stone and iron and smoke they could see from the ridge. He read the decision as prudence. From the ridge it read as a man who had learned he could not be caught and had stopped, visibly, bothering to look cautious. Both readings used the same facts. Only one of them was standing where the danger was.

He gave the order carefully, which was the form his recklessness always took, a recklessness of scale dressed in the manners of restraint. Houses first, he told Bram, and the millrace before the foundry, so that no man could say the prince had built his furnace before his workers' roofs. Small crews, staged, each trade brought on as the last one cleared, so the district rose in a line that could be inspected at any point and found lawful, honest, and paid. It was disciplined. It was also enormous, and the discipline was in the sequence and never once in the total, and Emeric, who could hold both of those truths and admire only the first, called it a measured beginning and meant it, and was wrong about nothing except what the beginning was the beginning of.

Fletcher he took aside separately, because the ship was a different order of thing and Emeric knew it. The wood hull that had carried them through the defense had done its work and shown its ceiling, and what he wanted now, what he had wanted since he first stood on the bank doing the arithmetic of tonnage and reinforcement in his head, was a hull that did not compromise, a metal vessel of a kind this coast had never floated. He did not commission the ship. He commissioned the keel, one length of shaped iron and the yard to build it in, and told Fletcher the rest would come as the foundry came, plate by plate, as the district learned to make what the ship would need. Fletcher, who had spent a career being told what could not be done and had built it anyway out of spite and love, ran a hand along the drawing and said very little, which from Fletcher was a shout. The keel would be laid before the first snow. The ship itself was a promise Emeric was making to a later chapter of his own life, and he made it the way he made all such promises, on the assumption that he would be there, upright and free, to keep it.

Among the machines that would go into the foundry was the pantograph, and he thought about it exactly as long as a tool deserved, which is to say he thought about what it would do and not about what else it would do. It copied labor. It let one skilled hand's motion drive a dozen unskilled ones, let a master's single true cut become a hundred true cuts, and it would speed the glasswork past anything the guilds could match, and that was the whole of what he needed from it and therefore the whole of what he considered. That the thing worked by drawing the worker into long, close, daily contact with a running current of shaped mana, that a man could not stand at that bench season after season and walk away in the evening exactly the man who had arrived in the morning, was not written anywhere in his drawings, because it was not a feature he had designed and so was not a fact he had counted. He had built for the output. The output was clean. He filed the pantograph, that afternoon, under solved, and went on to the next line of the plan, and did not hear the door he had just left open, because it opened onto a room his instruments could not enter, the one room in the whole enterprise where the thing that mattered would happen not on a page but in a body, and a body is the one ledger no audit reads.

At the edges of it, off in the halls where such things are decided, the old blood of Aldany received the news of the district the way it received all news of Emeric now, which was as evidence. Roderic Aldane did not need a spy in Kaldo to tell him what a foundry at the rapids meant. He had watched a clean audit come back clean and drawn from it the one conclusion the audit had been built to prevent, and now he watched a year's leash come off and a prince pour the whole withheld strength of himself into the ground in a single season, and he did not read it as prudence and he did not read it as ambition. He read it as a schedule. A man does not build like that to sit inside his walls. A man builds like that when he means, eventually, to come out, and the only question left to a lord of Aldane's persuasion was not whether to move against him, which was settled, but only in what currency, and by what road, since the lawful ones had been so thoroughly demonstrated to run nowhere. Emeric had answered that question for him, cleanly, months ago, and was answering it again now with every wagon of ore that crossed the mended road, and did not know he was speaking.

There was no clean win in any of it, though it wore the shape of one so well that even Ferra, closing the books that night with the covenant line struck and the debt current and the district begun, permitted herself the small dry pleasure of a column that finally moved the right way. The prosperity was real. It was earned. It would feed people who had been hungry and house people who had been cold, and Emeric had built it to last past himself on purpose and at cost. And it was, in the same stone and the same smoke, the thing that would come for him, because the freedom to build fast was indistinguishable, from the ridge, from the intent to conquer, and the machine that would make him rich would also, quietly, in bodies, make him a heretic, and he could not have the one without laying the ground for the other. He had wanted, for a year, to be let off the leash. He was off it now. And he walked into the new season with a freed hand and a clear ledger and a good conscience, building, at speed, in full view, the exact evidence that two separate enemies would need, and slept, again, well, having learned nothing from the last time he had done that except how much he enjoyed it.
