Book 2, Chapter 9: The Door

The Challenger came in on the top of the water at ten past nine on the Thursday morning, slowly, behind a red and white boat with a flag on its stern, and Emeric stood in her open hatch in the king's coat and watched the wall come up in front of them.

Ned had her at the helm below. Noll had the stones. They'd taken her down at seven, a mile outside the ring, well off the gate, and brought her up again a quarter of a mile further on, out in the open where every rock on the Rim could see her come up, and run in from there on the surface with her hatch open and her lamp lit and the pilot ahead, at the pace of a man walking. Ned hadn't said a word about it. He'd said a great deal about it at six, to Noll, in the K Endeavor's cradle, and Emeric had let him.

The pilot was the same broad merman who'd taken the iron, and he hadn't stopped looking back at them since the markers. Not at the Challenger. At Emeric. At the coat.

It was a heavy coat. Abloh had made it, as he'd made the knights' jackets, in the loft over the rope store through the winter, and he'd made it to be seen: black wool, close, double-breasted, the long coat nearly to his boots and worn open on its red lining, the short gold-edged cape over his shoulders, and between the shoulders the dragon coiled inside its ring of leaves in gold thread, so that whoever saw him go saw it last. Abloh had said the queen would see a man's back last. Under it Emeric's ribs were strapped and his left forearm was splinted, and the strapping showed nowhere, which was the other thing Abloh had made it for, though he hadn't known it.

His sword was in the K Amara's strong box. The K Amara was four miles behind him, past the outer markers, on the sea, and Ferra was on her bridge deck. He could feel her along the ring, very faint at that distance, a warmth at the edge of everything. He could feel Imani too, on the K Endeavor outside the ring with the rest of the convoy, fainter still, and busy. Neither of them would be at the door. The watch's terms had seen to that. Ferra had found the gap that made the morning possible, and Imani had shut her cabin door and asked the bride in plain words if she meant it, and both of them were going to watch it from a mile and more off through a glass. He'd said so to Ferra last night on the set. She'd said it was a port order and she'd read it, and she'd see him after.

Zale's ship was off the Challenger's starboard quarter on the water, at the same walking pace, with a spare sea anchor stowed and no rudder yet, steering on her veins as if she'd been doing it all her life. Zale was at her rail. Maeve was beside him in her best, with her chin up, and her spear left on the rack below, by Ward's order and her own sense.

The wall came up.

It was the breakwater. From the ring's outside it had looked like the islands, black and broken; from close to, it was built. Black stone, dressed and fitted, each block the size of a cart, rising thirty feet out of the sea in a long curve that ran from the island on their left to the island on their right without a gap in it but one. The gap was the gate. It was an arch, high and narrow, cut through the wall, with a lamp of red glass hanging in the top of it and another of green, and under the arch the sea ran in, and steps came down to meet it.

They came down from a landing on the wall's inner face, a long flight of them, black stone worn smooth in the middle, and they went on down into the water. He could see them going down under the surface through the clear sea inside the gate, step after step, ten, twenty, getting dimmer, until they went into the green glow below and he couldn't see them any more. The city crossed the waterline here. That was what Ilenne called it: the door. Above the water was the Rim's. Below the water was hers. The steps were both.

There were people on them.

The pilot's boat went in under the arch and turned aside into a slip on the left and stopped. The Challenger came in after it, slower, and Ned put her alongside the foot of the steps on the right with a care Emeric had never seen him give anything, and held her there on the stones, level, with the water lapping the hatch coaming. Zale's ship came in under the arch behind her and lay off on the water inside the gate, ten yards out, with her lamp lit. And Emeric stood in the hatch and looked up the steps.

A dozen of them stood in the water, on the steps, from the third step under to the waterline. Merfolk, in long dark coats that shone, standing as people stand in water, quite still, with the sea round their waists and their chests; armed, with long things like bidents held upright. The twelve. At the head of them, a step higher, a tall broad man with a pale stone gorget and his colour held perfectly level, whom Emeric had seen once before, looking up at him out of the sea: the captain. Off to one side, on the dry landing, alone, Taurre, with his slate.

And above all of them, on the last step before the water, waist-deep, with nobody beside her, the queen.

She was older than he'd thought she'd be, and smaller. She was Ilenne's height. She had Ilenne's face, under a good many more years and a band of white stone across the brow, and her hair was cut short as the guards' was cut, and she wore no coat at all. Her shoulders were bare. Her colour was still: not held, as Ilenne held hers, with the effort showing, but still, as deep water is still. She stood in the sea at her own door and looked down the steps at the land ship come alongside them, and at the man standing in its hatch, and he couldn't read anything in her at all.

Then Ilenne came up out of the hatch behind him.



She'd been below since the markers, in the Challenger's after cabin, with the door shut. He'd felt her there the whole way in, brighter and brighter, and then not. She'd been holding it. She came up past him now in the dress Abloh had made her for going home, pearl and shell off the Isle's beds and the sheer stuff that didn't mind the sea, with her hair down and her feet bare, and stepped from the hatch onto the coaming and from the coaming onto the first dry step, on her own two legs; and she didn't look back at him.

She walked up the steps.

Not into the water. Up, on the dry part, past the guards' heads, step by step, slowly. Every one of the twelve turned to watch her go up past them. He felt Zale, out on his ship, stop breathing.

The queen's colour slipped.

He saw it. Everyone on those steps saw it. For the space of one breath, at the sight of her daughter walking up the stair of her own door out of a land ship's hatch, the queen went gold from the collarbone to the brow, the whole of her at once, a warm deep gold that lit the water round her waist. Then it was gone. She'd put it away. She stood in the sea at the head of her guards with her colour still and level as before, and if anyone on those steps had missed it, they hadn't missed that she'd needed to put it away.

She didn't go to her.

Ilenne stopped on the step above the queen's head, on the dry, three yards off, and stood there with her hands at her sides, and didn't go to her mother either. She waited. She looked down at the water and waited.

It was Taurre who spoke. He stepped to the edge of the landing with his slate and said, in a level clerk's voice, a good many words in the merfolk tongue that Emeric didn't understand; and he didn't need to understand them. He knew what they were. The two land ships at the gate. Six hulls outside the ring, where they lay, and the one past the markers. The pilot's fee, paid, eight bars. A man in a coat at the queen's door, and what he was. Taurre read it off the slate into the morning, all of it, while the queen stood in the water and heard it, and her daughter stood on the step above her and heard it, and neither of them moved; and Emeric understood that this was how it went, here, even for her, even today. A land ship had come to her door. The forms came first. For her as for anyone.

When Taurre had finished, the queen turned her head and looked at Emeric.

He'd been taught it on the K Amara's aft deck every second evening for a fortnight. He came up out of the hatch onto the coaming and down onto the first step, the dry one, so that he stood a little above the water and a little below her; and then he went down one more, into the sea, so that it came up over his boots to the knee and he was level with her. Not above. Not below. The wool of the coat's skirts went dark at the hem. He bowed: the guest's bow, from the waist, level, slow, hands open at his sides. Rank to rank.

She bowed back. The same bow, exactly, to the same depth, from the water.

Then her eyes went to his, and stayed there.

"Suvarre," she said.

It was the captain's word. The captain had said it once, out of the sea, looking at him. She said it the same way. She added nothing to it, and she didn't explain, and he didn't ask.



"Ilenne," he said, without looking round. "I need her ear. Apart. Before anything else."

Ilenne came down a step, to the edge of the water, and said something to her mother, quietly.

The queen didn't answer at once. She looked at him again, and then at her daughter, standing there on the step above her with her bare feet on the stone, waiting to be received, and not received, and asking this first. Then she moved her hand a very little, and the captain and the guards stepped back down the stair a step each, and another, into deeper water. They held their places there. All but one. One guard on the left end of the line, a lean grey man with a hard narrow face, went back the two steps and no further, which left him nearer than the rest, and near enough. Emeric marked him. The queen didn't send him further, and he didn't know whether she'd noticed or chosen.

He went up one step, out of the water, to stand beside Ilenne on the edge of the dry; and the queen came up one, so that the sea was at her hips; and they were three, close, at the waterline, with Ilenne between.

"Tell her," he said, "that the ship past the markers is the K Amara. The big one. The burned one. That in her hold, in a room of iron, there's a stone that makes a dead zone. A thing that puts out every stone round it for a long way, and keeps them out for a day. That it's there because my knight keeps it for war, and we've been at war, and it came with us because everything came with us. That I told you in March she'd hear it from me before we came in." He kept his voice low and level. "And that it's there now, past her markers, on the sea, under Ward's hand. And it doesn't move unless she says."

Ilenne told her. She told it slowly, in her own tongue, and he watched the queen's face while she did, and saw nothing in it, and then, at one word that he guessed was dead, saw her colour go from still to stiller. That was all. It didn't change. It went down, as a lamp goes down when you turn the wick.

She didn't answer him for a long time. She looked at him. He let her. He didn't look away and he didn't say any more, because there wasn't any more, and anything else he said would have been asking for something.

Then she turned her head and said one word, not loud, and Taurre came down from the landing to the waterline with his slate.

The queen spoke to Taurre. Ilenne put it into Kaldo as she spoke, very quietly.

"She says: the watch's line for that ship is right. She says: and more. That ship stays beyond the outer markers. It does not come back inside them, and it does not move from where it lies, at all, not a ship's length, without her word. Her word, not the watch's." Ilenne's voice was flat with care. "She says write it."

Taurre wrote it.

It wasn't an I will. Emeric heard it not be one. It was a port order, given by the crown to the crown's own watch and written on the watch's slate, and it bound his ship and nobody's tongue. She'd tightened what Taurre had set, and she hadn't promised him anything, and she'd done it standing in the sea at her own door with a hard-faced guard two steps down the stair who'd heard every word of the translation.

She looked at Emeric once more, long and level.

Then she turned from him and went up the steps to her daughter.



He didn't watch it. He went back down a step into the water and stood with his eyes on the arch and the red lamp, and let the two of them have it; and what he saw anyway, at the edge of his sight, was enough. The queen came up out of the sea onto the dry step, on legs, slowly, with the water running off her, and stood in front of her daughter, and put one hand up flat against the side of Ilenne's face, as Rowena had put hers flat on Zale's chest at the yard; and her colour went. All of it. She didn't stop it this time. It went up through her and through Ilenne at once, the same gold, so that the two of them on that step in the grey morning were lit like a window, and the guards in the water below them dropped their eyes, every one, to the sea.

Out on Zale's ship, nobody moved.

It went on for a while. He didn't know how long. Long enough for Ilenne to say something into her mother's shoulder and her mother to answer, once. Then the queen took her hand away, and her colour came down again, slowly, and she was a queen at her door once more, and she turned and looked at Zale's ship lying on the water inside the gate.

Ilenne said something to her. Quite short.

The queen looked at her daughter. Then at Zale's ship. Then at Emeric, standing knee-deep on the step below with his coat skirts dark with sea; and then back at her daughter; and her colour did nothing at all, and she inclined her head.



Zale came in from his ship in the boat, rowing himself, with Maeve in the stern, and put the boat alongside the Challenger at the foot of the steps, and got out into the water.

He went in to the waist. He'd known he would. He'd told Emeric on the ship that morning, in the cabin, with his good coat laid out on the bunk and not put on, that you didn't do this in a coat; you did it in the water in your shirt, as she'd do it in her dress, because the water was the point. So he stood in the sea at the foot of the queen's door in his shirtsleeves, with his feet on a step he couldn't see, and the swell coming in under the arch and lifting him a little and setting him down. Maeve came up onto the dry landing at the top of the steps and stood there on the threshold, by Taurre, with her hands behind her back.

Ilenne came down to him. Down the steps, into the water, until it was at her waist too, on the step beside his. Her legs went as the water reached her knees. He saw it happen through the clear sea: the grey dress floating out round her, and under it the long pale shimmer of her, and her tail moving once to hold her where she was. She was the same height as him in the water. She turned so that they faced each other.

The captain came up out of the deep water to stand on the step below them, on one side. Taurre came down from the landing, into the sea in his long coat as if it were nothing, and stood on the step below them on the other side.

Two witnesses. One the queen's man, and one nobody's.

Ilenne said it first. She said it in her own tongue, in a clear voice, not loud, with her eyes on Zale's face, and Emeric didn't know the words and didn't need to. It was short. It had a shape to it, a beginning and an end, and in the middle of it, he could hear, the one word that mattered, said once and not hurried. Her colour didn't move while she said it. She held it perfectly still, all of it, so that there'd be nothing in the saying but the saying.

Then Zale said it.

He said it in her tongue first, as she'd taught him on his own deck at night all that week with the old one asleep under the keel, every sound of it. He got one wrong. Emeric heard it go, a sound at the back of the throat that came out at the front. And Ilenne said the sound again, softly, the right way, and Zale said it again after her, right, and went on, and finished.

Then he said it in Kaldo. So that it was said in both.

"I will," said Zale.

The sea came in under the arch and lifted them both a little, and set them down.

Then, by the custom, they named their houses. Ilenne named hers. It was long, and it ended in her mother's name, and when she said that the queen's colour moved once, at the throat, and stilled. Yssaure.

Zale named his in her tongue, which he'd also learned that week and also said badly; and Ilenne corrected him on one sound again, and he said it again. Emeric didn't know the merfolk words for any of it. He knew what Zale was saying, because Zale had told him in the cabin. His father, who was king of the land. His brother, who was king now. His brother, standing three steps away in the water in a coat with a dragon on it.

It landed on the steps hard.

The captain went pale. Not white: the pale colour, the one Ilenne had taught them on the aft deck, the colour you went before a rank higher than yours. It went over him from the throat down and he held it, and his head came down a fraction. Down the stair, in the deeper water, most of the twelve went with him, one after another, pale, eyes down. It was deference. Emeric knew it was deference. It wasn't agreement. Ilenne had said so on the aft deck, twice: pale is rank, not yes.

One of them didn't go pale.

The guard on the left end of the line, the lean one, the one who'd stood nearer than he should have to the translation, went slate. Dark, hard, blue-grey, all over, the warning colour, at once; and he held it. He stood in the water with the bident upright in his hand and his eyes on Zale and the slate on him like a coat, and didn't let it go.

Taurre's colour didn't move at all. Not up, not down, not anything. He stood on his step in the sea with his slate under his arm and his face as still as the wall behind him, and wrote nothing, and showed nothing, and somehow that was the loudest thing on the steps.

On the threshold, Maeve let out her breath.

"Well," she said, to nobody. "That's out, then."



He felt the other one coming a full minute before anyone else did.

It came up from Below. He'd had his sense open all morning, wide, as he always had it, and the water inside the gate was crowded with notes, the twelve's and the queen's and Ilenne's, and past them, at the edge of what the thick water let him have, a blur that might have been many more. One note came up out of the blur. A strong one. Much stronger than the guards'. It came up slow and straight from below, not toward the gate, toward the steps, toward the exact place where Zale and Ilenne stood in the water; and it didn't hurry, and it didn't turn aside.

He kept still. He'd been taught on the aft deck what it meant to come up from under, and he could feel what this was doing, and he could feel that it wanted him to move first. So he stood knee-deep on his step with his hands at his sides and the coat's skirts floating round his boots, and watched the place in the water where it was going to come up, and waited.

It came up under Zale.

The water broke a yard from Zale's back, from straight below, and a merman came up out of it to the chest, close enough to put a hand on Zale's shoulder, and didn't. He was big, as big as the captain, younger, dark-skinned, with long hair bound back and a collar of worked red stone round his throat that the sea ran off. He came up under, at the queen's own door, in front of her guards and her watch and her daughter, the way Ilenne had said on the K Amara's platform a hunter comes, the way you never come. Every merfolk on those steps saw it. Emeric felt it go through them like cold water.

Ilenne went colourless.

It happened at once, the moment he broke the surface. Everything went out of her: not pale, not slate, not any colour at all. Flat. The flat dead skin of the first courtesy, the colourless thing that she'd told them on the aft deck was what something in the deep says to you just before it eats you. She turned it on him full, in front of her mother. He saw the queen see it.

The merman didn't look at her. He looked at Zale.

He put one hand out, low, across his own body at the waist, and moved it sideways, flat, slowly, the way you'd wipe a table. Emeric knew that one too. Everybody on that stair knew it. A man whose word is water. The worst thing you could call anybody, Ilenne had said: worse than coward, worse than thief. And he'd said it to a man who'd just said his one I will in the water before witnesses, which made it a thing said about the binding, as well as about the man. That it was worth nothing. That it was water.

Then he turned his head and looked at Emeric, and said a word.

It was Suvarre. The same word the captain had said once, looking at him, and the queen ten minutes since. But he said it differently. He said it longer, with something in the middle of it that the captain's hadn't had and a sound at the end like a door closing, the old way, the way Emeric guessed it was said on a stone somewhere, under a carving of something in the sky. Gold eyes, come back. With ships.

His eyes went once to the coat. To the dragon across Emeric's back, where it showed over his shoulder. They rested there and came away. He let it lie.

Zale's hand went to his knife.

It had always been going to. Emeric had known it would, since the merman came up, and he was already moving. He had Zale's wrist before the hand got there. Not hard. He just put his own hand round his brother's wrist, in the water, below the surface, where nobody on the stair could see it, and held it, and Zale's hand stopped an inch from the hilt and stayed stopped.

He felt Zale understand. It went through the wrist. A land man's raised hand at the queen's door. That was what this was for. That was what the merman had come up from the bottom of the sea for, slowly, in front of everyone: not to stop the binding, because nobody could stop a binding said in the water and he must have known it, but to be struck. By a land man. Here.

Zale took his hand off the knife.

And then he did the only thing Emeric would never have thought of. He turned round in the water, slowly, to face the merman who'd come up under him, and he bowed.

The full guest's bow. Ilenne's. The one she'd taught on the K Amara's aft deck and made them do again and again until they did it right, level, from the waist, hands open, slow, all the way down and all the way up again. He did it perfectly. He did it standing waist-deep in the sea at the foot of his wife's mother's door, to the man who'd just called him water, and he did it better than Emeric had ever seen anybody do it, and when he came up again his face had nothing in it at all.

The merman's colour moved.

It was small. It was a flush, a dark red, at the throat, under the red stone collar, there and gone. But Emeric saw it, and the guards saw it, and the captain saw it, and Taurre saw it. A guest's correctness at the host's door. A lord's rudeness answered with a courtesy, in the open, in front of the watch. It had landed, and everyone on the stair had watched it land.

Emeric went down into the water.

He went down off his step to the next, and the next, until he was waist-deep beside his brother, level, in the coat. The sea came up through the wool and soaked it to the shoulders in a breath, cold and heavy, and the dragon on his back went dark, and he didn't look at it. He stood beside Zale in the water and looked at the merman.

He didn't say anything.

He was the only man on that stair whose word in the water was his country's. Every one of them knew it. Ilenne had taught it every second evening for a fortnight; the queen had watched him climb out of the sea to say his one thing to her; and the merman knew it too, Emeric could see him know it. One sentence from him, here, in the sea, before witnesses, and it was Randillia's. So he said nothing. He looked at the merman with the eyes the merman had just named, steadily, without any expression at all, the coat heavy on him and the water cold through it and his ribs aching under the strapping; and he kept looking.

The guards' colours were everywhere. Pale and slate and something in between, going and coming, all down the stair.

The merman looked back for a long breath. Then another.

Then he looked away.

The queen spoke.

She said one thing. One sentence, to the merman, from the step above, not loud, in the water. Ilenne didn't translate it. She didn't need to; Emeric caught the merman's name at the front of it, Ushar, and the rest he could read in the way it was said and the way the merman took it. You came up under. At my door. That was all it said. She couldn't say more, not to him, here, at a gate in the Rim's breakwater, where whatever he was he had a right to be; and both of them knew it, and she said exactly as much as she could and stopped.

Taurre, on his step, took his slate from under his arm and wrote. It was the first thing he'd written since the binding.

Ushar went down the way he'd come. Slowly. He went down into the water until only his head was out, and then not that, and Emeric felt the strong note go down and down through the hum of the city toward the glow, taking its time about it, and then lose itself among all the others.

Nothing was settled. Emeric could feel that too. A man had been called water at the queen's door before witnesses, on his wedding day, and he'd answered it with a bow, and a bow wasn't an answer to that, not here. Ilenne had said once on the aft deck what happened to a name like that if it wasn't answered before witnesses. It stuck.

Zale was looking at the place where Ushar had gone down. His face still had nothing in it.

"Right," he said, to the water. "I'll have to think of something."



The queen came down the steps to the waterline.

The water was still settling where Ushar had gone, a long slow ring of it spreading out across the gate and lapping at the stair. She stood on the last step above it, on her legs, with the sea round her ankles, and she looked at the three of them in the water below her: her daughter, with her colour still flat and coming back into her slowly from the edges; the land man her daughter had just bound herself to, in his wet shirt, with his hands empty; and the land king, waist-deep in his drowned coat, who had stood in her sea at her door through all of that and not said one word.

She'd noticed. He could see she'd noticed. She'd noticed it at the beginning, when he came out of the hatch, and she noticed it again now.

She bowed. The same bow as before. Level, slow.

And then she brought her right hand up from her side, open, and turned it over, palm up, toward them. Toward the steps going up behind her into her city. And held it there.

Come in. My house. My word.

Ilenne had taught them that one early. Only a host does it, she'd said. It says what it says: my house, by my word.

Emeric bowed back, in the water, with the sea running off the dragon on his shoulders, and kept his eyes down, and didn't look up.
