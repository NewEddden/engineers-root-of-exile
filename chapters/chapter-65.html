Book 2, Chapter 26: Breath

The second charge went off a minute after the first, low on the far side, and Zale felt the whole bell lurch on its chain and swing, and settle, and heard the glass over the pallets go.

It didn't crack. It went. A section of the shell the size of a door, over Ilenne's pallet, where she'd thrown the suit, simply wasn't there; there was a hole in the bell's side full of black water, and the black water came in.

It came in like a wall falling over. He saw it come, in the lamplight, a solid grey-green slab of the sea tipping in through the hole onto Ilenne's pallet; and the pallet went, and the suit on it went, the black rubber and the belt and the weights and the gill pack in its harness, all of it, gone in one swallow, out and down into the dark, before he'd done more than lift his head.

Then the water stopped.

It stopped in the air a yard from him. It hung there, a curved grey face of sea standing in the hole and bulging into the bell, trembling all over, held. He could see light through it. He could see a shape outside, beyond it, in the dark, moving.

Ilenne was on her feet in the middle of the bell with both hands out toward it, palms flat, and her face set.

He'd seen her hold water before. He'd seen her hold a sheet of it up off a ship's deck in the wall water to show a knight it could be done, laughing. This wasn't that. She wasn't laughing. She was holding the sea out of a hole in the side of a bell hundreds of feet down, with all of it pressing in at once, and he could see what it was costing her in the set of her shoulders.

The third charge went.

The other side. Low, by the anchor chain, where the crack had come; the glass there went in a spray of fragments and the water came in through that too, a second face of it, and Ilenne turned her head and put out one hand to it without taking the other off the first, and it stopped.

Two holes. Two faces of sea, standing in the bell's sides, held.

The lamps went out in the next one. The fourth charge was overhead, at the crown, and when it went the lamp stones in the glass up there went with it, all of them, into the sea, and the dark came down; only the two at the floor were left, dim, green, and by them he saw the crown of the bell open like an egg.

The sea came down on him.

And stopped.

He lay on his back on his pallet with his hand on the cloth on his side and looked up, and the sea was over him, a hand's breadth from his face. Grey-green and black, enormous, pressing. Curved. He could see the curve of it going away on every side of him, down to the floor, round. He was lying in a bubble. A bubble of air the size of a cart, with him in it, and Ilenne in it, standing over him, and the whole of the sea outside it pressing in on every side to get at him.

And outside the bubble, past its skin, there was something else. Another layer. He couldn't see it. He could see what it did: a dark shape came at the bubble out of the black, fast, a man, with a spear, and drove it at the curve over Zale's chest; and the spear went into the water outside the bubble and stopped as if it had gone into mud, and slowed, and turned aside, and the man went past with it, tumbling. Something bright came after him, a light, a cast, a line of white straight at the bubble; it went into that outer dark and spread out and went dull, like a coal dropped in a bucket.

A pocket of something thick. Outside the air, round all of it. Turning everything they threw.

She was holding both.

He understood that, lying there. She was holding the sea off him with one thing and holding them off with another, both at once, all round, everything at once, and she couldn't do anything else at all. She couldn't move. She couldn't turn her head. She stood over him in the middle of the bubble in the dim green of the two last lamps with her hands out and her feet apart and her eyes open, and her lips moving, and he saw a line of dark come out of her nose and run down over her lip and her chin and drip onto her dress.

Outside, beyond the curve, beyond the outer dark, very dimly, he saw Thalune.

She'd come back. She was in the water past the bell's broken water door, keeping her place with her tail, alone. No healers behind her; no guards; nobody. She'd gone for the queen's healers, and come back without any; whatever she'd found up there, it had been nobody. Her colour was going in great dark waves, her face turned to the bubble, and she couldn't hear a word of anything in it. She could only see.

The shapes outside came again. Three of them now. Spears and light, and another charge, somewhere out of sight; he felt it through the floor, and the bubble shuddered, all of it, the sea over his face bulging down toward him a hand's breadth and pushed back.

Ilenne made a sound. Not a word. The blood was coming faster.



He knew two things.

He knew them the way he knew where the deck was under his feet in the dark. He didn't have to think them. They were there.

She was going to die before she let go. That was the first. He'd known her more than a year and he knew it as surely as he knew anything about anyone. She'd stand there with her hands out holding the sea off him until whatever it was in her that held it ran out, and then she'd stand there a bit longer, and then she'd fall down, and the sea would come in on both of them. She wouldn't let go first. Not for anything. Not if he asked her. He could see her not letting go now, in the set of her mouth with the blood on it.

And he was dying anyway. That was the second. He'd known it since the breakwater. The hole in his side was a hole and he'd been holding a rag on it for a quarter of an hour, and he could feel what was under the rag and how it had gone cold and wet and slow, and his hands were cold, and his feet were somewhere a long way off. The bracing held him against the deep, as it always had. It kept the sea's weight off his bones and out of his ears. It did nothing at all about breathing. When the bubble went, it would go, and the sea would be in him, and bracing wouldn't do a thing about that. He'd drown, braced. That was the joke of it.

Ushar had said it on the steps. He didn't speak in the water to a man who couldn't breathe it.

Two things. She'd die first. He'd die anyway.

So she might as well not.

He knew she wouldn't listen. He couldn't reach her; she was a yard off and he couldn't get up, and if he touched her she wouldn't feel it, and if he spoke she wouldn't hear it. She was gone into the holding, all of her. There was only one thing he had that she couldn't not hear.

He'd been whistling since he was a boy. Rowena had taught him, years ago, with the door shut, how to put a breath through his teeth and put something on it so that the air went where he told it. He'd kept it quiet for years, as she'd told him to, and only lately let anyone see it. It was the only casting he had. And he'd never once used it on anyone he loved.

He wet his lips. They were cold and they didn't want to move.

He whistled.

Short and hard, through his teeth, with everything he had left on it. The air went out of him straight past her face, a crack, a flat slap of it, close enough to her ear to move her hair. In the bubble, in that small round space with the sea all round, it was as loud as a shot.

Ilenne flinched.

Only that. Her head jerked, an inch, toward the sound. Her eyes came off the sea for the first time in ten minutes and found him on his pallet, looking up at her, with his lips still pursed; and he saw her understand what he'd done; and he saw her hold go.

The sea came down.



It was cold, and it was everything, and it was in him.

He'd been under water all his life. He'd swum since before he could walk, in the cold grey sea off Emberfell and in the harbour at Kaldo and in the wall water and in this one, in the Mark II and out of it. He knew what it was to be under. You held your breath. That was what being under was. You held it, and you came up.

He didn't hold it. He didn't have it to hold. The whistle had taken the last of it, and the sea came down on him all at once, the whole weight of it, and went into his mouth and his nose and down his throat into his chest before he could close anything, cold, salt, enormous, filling him.

He drowned.

He felt it start. He'd heard men say it was easy at the end, and it wasn't, it was the worst thing that had ever happened to him; his chest heaved against the water in it and his whole body bucked and his hands came up and clawed at nothing. The two green lamps at the floor were gone in a cloud of glass and grit. It was black. He was in the black with the sea in his lungs and the blood going out of his side, and the last of his mind said, quite calmly, there, that's it, then.

And his body answered.

It didn't ask him. It went ahead without him, the way your hand comes up before you've seen the stone. Something under his ears opened. Both sides of his neck, at once, a pulling, a tearing, as if somebody had cut him there with a knife and the cuts had come open in the water and kept opening; and the water that was in his chest went out through them. Through. Not back up his throat. Through, out of the sides of his neck, in a long cold rush; and more came in at his mouth and went through after it; and with it, through it, out of it, something went into his blood that wasn't air and did what air did.

He breathed.

He was breathing the sea.

His hands hurt. He was aware of it as he was aware of the hole in his side: a long way off, important, later. His fingers were pulling apart from each other and something was coming between them, thin and tough. His skin was going hard down his arms, from the wrist up, in plates. His back was on fire across both shoulder blades, two long lines of fire, and something was coming out of them, out of his back, folding, heavy, and he felt it fold and lie flat against him and didn't know what it was. His jaw ached. His skull ached, above the ears, two points. Something long and heavy was coming down from the base of his spine and he could feel the water on it.

And the dark turned into shapes.

It did it all at once, as if someone had lit a lamp that wasn't light. He could see. Not see. He could feel the water round him all the way out, in every direction, and in it everything that was in it, the broken glass falling slowly, the anchor chain, the floor, the rock of the wall, a man ten yards off with a spear, another behind him, a third further off with something bright in his hands; and Ilenne.

Ilenne, a yard from him, falling.

She'd been standing in air and the air was gone and the sea had come down on her too, and she'd gone limp in it, legs still, not changing, her hair spreading out round her head, blood going out of her nose in a dark thread that drifted, and her eyes half open, falling slowly through the water toward the floor of the broken bell with the whole weight of the sea that she'd held up for ten minutes coming down on her.

He didn't think about it.

He put out his hand toward the sea that was on her, and it moved.

It went where he told it. He didn't know how he told it. The water round her went still, and then it went back. Away from her, off her, all round her, in a slow round push, as if he'd put his two hands on either side of her and made a space; it held off her the way she'd held it off him. Not air. Water, still water, a pocket of quiet in the middle of the churned black, with her in the middle of it. And he felt it from inside, all of it, as you feel your own arm. It was his. All of it. The water he'd never had, coming up in him from somewhere he'd never known was there, and it was enormous, and it was his.

He held it off her.

She opened her eyes.



The house's men came in to finish it, and found him.

He didn't count them. Five, six. They came in through the hole where the crown had been, in a rush, spears first, men going into a room where they've heard a man drowning, to make sure; and they came into the dark of the broken bell and found something in it that wasn't drowning.

He was bigger than he'd been. He knew that from the way they looked at him, before anything else. He was crouched over Ilenne on the bell's floor with his wings folded tight against his back in the wreck, scaled, clawed, with a tail he could feel lashing the broken glass behind him, and in the dark he could see their faces change.

The first one came anyway. Brave, or too frightened to stop. He came straight down at Zale with his spear and Zale went up off the floor to meet him and took the spear on his forearm, on the plates there, and it skidded, and his other hand closed on the man's shoulder and threw him. Into the glass. Hard. The man didn't come back.

The second came from the side, low, at Ilenne.

Zale turned on him and put out his hand, the clawed one, the webbed one, to stop the spear. He didn't strike. He meant to catch it. And the water in front of his claws went hard.

He felt it go. A thin flat line of water in front of his hand, for an instant, hard as a blade and thinner than paper; and the spear went into it and came out the other side in two pieces. The head went one way, spinning. The shaft went on into his palm, blunt, and stopped.

He stared at it. So did the man.

Then the man let go of half a spear and went, backwards, up, out through the hole, as fast as a tail could take him.

The rest of it was short. He was bleeding through all of it; he could feel the hole in his side in the water now, the blood going out of it in a dark cloud that followed him when he moved. He didn't care. A light came at him, a cast, and went into the water and he felt the water take it and spread it and it was nothing. Another spear. His tail did something he didn't tell it to. Somebody screamed in the water, a sound that went right through him. And then the rest of them were going, all of them, up and out through the broken crown into the open sea, fast, as far from what was in the bell as they could get.

They'd say a beast. He knew that, watching them go. They'd go back to whoever had sent them and say a beast came up out of the deep into the broken bell, and they'd believe it.

Two of them didn't go anywhere. He didn't look at them.

He picked Ilenne up.

She was awake. She was limp in his arms, her head against his chest, against the hard plates of it, her nose still bleeding a thin dark thread into the water; and she was looking up at him, at his face, whatever his face was now, with her eyes wide open and her colour doing something he'd never seen it do, all of it at once.

He went up out of the bell through the broken crown with her in his arms, into the open sea. At the broken water door below him Thalune hung in the water, alone, and watched him go: a shape out of the dark going up past her with the queen's daughter in its arms. He felt her colour go, all of it, through the water.



He swam.

That was the thing. He'd not known until he did it. He'd been swimming all his life with his arms and his legs, kicking, the way land men swam; even in the Mark II with the slip panels on and the water giving way along his arms, it had been that. This was a tail. It went down and up and the sea went past him, and he was going, up the face of the caldera wall in the dark with the queen's house's lights falling away below, faster than he'd ever gone in his life, with Ilenne held against his chest and the water pouring past them both.

He could feel the whole of it. All the way out. The wall, the domes below, the shafts going up through the rock, the bells; and up ahead and to his left, high on the wall under the Rim, the place where the sea stood against the opening into the furnaces' chamber. Where the seal had been. He could feel that it was gone: a round black mouth in the rock with the sea gone into it and stopped, up against something hard and flat and heavy, a door shut in the stone. And past it, above, a wrongness in the water, a dead flat patch near the Rim's foot where everything that should have been there wasn't, like a hole in a sound.

He went round it. Wide. Up the outer face of the wall in the open sea, away from it, toward the gate.

Ilenne said something against his chest. He felt it more than heard it. He didn't stop.

The gate came over him: the great black arch of the breakwater, and the water under it, and inside it the lights of his own ship. Floods. Somebody had the floods on, white, hard, lighting the gate steps. He came up out of the dark under them toward the steps and the hull with Ilenne in his arms, and there were people on the steps, so he let the change go as the water got shallow; it went easily, out of him like a breath out, the wings and the tail and the plates pulling back in, and his own legs under him on the bottom steps, and his own two hands round her with the webbing going last.

He came up out of the water onto the gate steps with her in his arms.

There were people on the steps. Hesk, in the water to his waist, reaching. Linnet at the top of the steps with the set. His crew, half a dozen of them, running down. And Imani coming along the breakwater from the Rim at a dead run with her bag bouncing on her back and her hair coming down.

Ilenne got her feet on the step. She stood. She swayed, and stood. She didn't let go of his arm.

"Inside," she said. Hard, flat, to Hesk, to Linnet, to all of them. "Inside. Down. Not up. Nobody sees." She looked along the breakwater toward the Rim's lit street and the smoke and the people, and back. "Nobody says he lived."

Hesk looked at her.

"Nobody says," said Ilenne. "Not to anybody. Not yes. Not no. Nothing." Her nose was still bleeding. She didn't wipe it. "My word. On these steps." She'd been bound on them. "Nobody says he lived."

They got him into the hull through the low door at the waterline, not up the gangway in the light; Hesk and two of the crew and Linnet, half carrying, half dragging, down into the dark of the lower deck and along it and into a cabin, onto a bunk, out of sight of anything on the Rim; and Imani came in through the low door behind them two minutes later, out of breath, out of the zone, and her hands were on his side before she'd taken her bag off.

He felt it. The warmth. Her palms on the hole, and something going into him out of them that hadn't been able to an hour ago, and the cold in his side backing off a little.

"You stupid," said Imani, working. "You stupid, stupid man."

"I know."

"Hold still."

Ilenne was in the doorway. She stood there with her hand on the frame and the blood drying on her lip and chin, and looked at him on the bunk with Imani's hands in his side, and her colour was going dark and light and dark.

"You whistled," she said.

"I did."

"At me. In my ear."

"You'd have died."

"You'd have died!"

"I was going to anyway," said Zale. "I'd rather it was one of us."

She looked at him with her colour going and going, and he thought she was going to hit him. She came in and sat down on the edge of the bunk beside Imani and took his hand in both of hers, with the webbing still going slowly out of it, and held it, hard, and didn't say anything. She was furious. He could feel it in her hands. And then she wasn't; and then, a moment later, she was again, worse.

Neither of them said a word about the night of the beast, or the floor of the bell, or any of it.



Ward had had Ferra's call through Linnet at nine minutes past the shot, standing at the K Amara's board with his tea gone cold beside him.

He'd had the shot at two past. Linnet's voice, flat, fast: Zale shot on the breakwater, Ilenne's taken him down, Arthur says bad and alive, Mira's marked it. He'd stood there with the set in his hand and thought about it for as long as it took, which wasn't long. A man shot. Gone down below where nobody could reach him. Nothing for a warship thirty miles off to do about that but wait. He'd put the set down and called the officer of the watch and told him to have the engine room warm her up, which they'd do in any case, and nothing else.

Then the second call. Dead zone over the big kiln yard. Hatch shut, shutter down. People on the stair. Imani, maybe.

That was different.

"Station," said Ward.

The officer of the watch went to the rail with the loud-hailer. Ward went with him. The Grey Mark stood up out of the dusk a cable off, a grey pillar on its reef with the watch's lamp lit on its ledge, and the station officer was there by the lamp in his dark coat, as he was every evening.

Ward took the hailer himself.

"Station. K Amara." He said it slowly, for a man whose Kaldo was careful. "A dead zone at the Rim. Over the big kiln yard. People trapped on the yard stair. I ask the queen's leave to come in. To come in past the markers and take them off."

The officer on the ledge stood very still. Then he called back, cupping his hands, carefully: he had no word from the ring. He would send. A swimmer would go now, to the watch house, for the queen's word. He would send.

"How long?" said Ward.

The officer didn't answer that. He turned and said something to someone below him on the ledge, and a shape went off the reef into the dark water and was gone, west to east, toward the ring. Thirty miles. A swimmer. A fast one, Ward supposed. He'd seen what they could do.

He stood at the rail and looked at his watch.

Eleven minutes since the shot. Twelve. He had the board read to him: Linnet again, from the gate. Nothing new. Ferra had gone off the set. Fourteen.

He hailed again.

"Station. K Amara. I ask again. People are trapped. I ask the queen's leave."

The officer on the ledge called back that he'd sent. That he had no word. That he was sorry.

Ward put the hailer down.

He'd done this sum the day they came out here. He'd done it on the Thursday, on the bridge deck at the after rail, with Emeric standing next to him saying it; and every night since, on his back in his berth, with the deck plates ticking. Outside the markers, to save a life, he went and told the watch. Inside, it was a breach of the queen's order, and of what Emeric had said in her hall. Unless her word came first. And if it didn't, and somebody was dying at the ring, and he was thirty miles off with three quarters of the knights: his judgment. Emeric's to answer for.

A swimmer thirty miles to the ring. The watch house to the queen, who was at the bottom of the sea. The queen's word back up, and thirty miles back. Hours. And people on a stair under a barred hatch in the smoke, now.

He knew what it would do to Emeric's word. He knew exactly. He'd said it to his face on Thursday: you've given her something to stand on, and me a reason to break it.

"Anchor up," said Ward. "All of it. Lift. Then the burn."

The officer of the watch looked at him.

"The burn," said Ward. "Every can. Now." He picked the hailer back up. "Station. K Amara. I'm coming in. On my judgment, to take people off the yard stair at the Rim. Escort three will hold at the outer markers. Tell the watch. Tell them I said so." He put the hailer down. "Set to escort three. Follow on her own burn. Hold at the outer markers. Not one yard past. One hull breaches tonight, not two."

The stone was in the hold, in its iron room, two decks under his feet. He didn't look at the deck. He didn't need to.

The K Amara came up off the sea.



The cans lit at nineteen minutes after the shot.

He'd heard them on the test stand at home with the doors open. He'd never stood on her deck when they lit in earnest. The whole hull shook. The sound came up through the plates and through his boots and his teeth and his skull, a roar that went up and up until it wasn't a sound any more but a weight; and the K Amara put her nose east and went, and the Grey Mark and its lamp and its officer on the ledge with his hands over his ears were gone behind her in the dusk before Ward had got his hand off the rail.

Escort three came up behind her, a mile back, lit, roaring.

Ferra would have his times. He knew them without asking, counted from the shot: the anchor up at sixteen, lifting, the burn at nineteen, the ring coming up out of the dark ahead at twenty-four, the islands and the lights and the breakwater and, at the far end of the Rim, a great dark smear on the night where the lights weren't, with a red glow under it.

The zone. He saw it from three miles off. A patch of the Rim with no lights at all, not one lamp stone, round the big kiln yard, sixty yards across, perhaps; and in the middle of it the fire still burning, red, and smoke.

He couldn't fly into it. Nothing could. A zone dropped anything that flew on stones; he'd drop his ship on the Rim's roofs. He took her in to its edge, low, fifty feet over the water at the Rim's foot below the kiln yard, and held her there, and had the floods put on.

They came on white and hard over the black water and the rock and the yard's walls, and lit everything. He saw merfolk on the water stairs below the yard turn their faces away from them, as they always did. Then turn back, and look up into them, and stay looking.

The knights' night glass was no use. The light-stone grids in their visors would be dead the moment they crossed the zone's edge, and the heat layer with them; and the yard was all warm stone and fire anyway. The floods weren't stones. They ran off the ship's own dynamo, and they lit the yard like noon.

"Flight knights out," said Ward. "Land outside the edge. Walk in. Take your rifles. You'll have your legs and your hands and nothing else in there, and you'll walk."

They went. He watched them go off the aprons in pairs and drop toward the dark edge of the Rim and land on the stones just outside it, light, as they always did; and walk in. Heavy, ordinary, on their feet, into the dark yard, in their kit, like men walking into a barn. A zone dropped fliers and left knights everything their bodies had. They'd been drilled on it all winter.

There were people in the yard already, working. He saw them in the floods: three figures in Kaldo blue among the furnace hands at the hatch mouth and the furnace sheds, carrying. Arthur. Mira. Bren. They'd come from the breakwater and gone straight into the zone on foot without being told, and they were carrying men out of the smoke of the sheds and up from the yard stair, where some fool had gone back down for an old man who couldn't climb.

His knights took them from there. Out of the yard, across the zone's edge, down the water stairs below it to where the watch's skiffs were coming in out of the dark to take people off. A line of them. He could see it from the bridge deck, plain as a drawing: the yard, the edge, the stairs, the skiffs, the water.



The skiff went over at twenty-six minutes after the shot.

He saw it happen and couldn't do anything. The K Amara was holding fifty feet over the water at the zone's edge on her lift, and her downwash went out from under her flat across the sea, a great ring of it, flattening the water white; and the watch's skiffs on the evacuation line were in it. Small boats. Open boats, rowed. He saw one of them heel in the blast, and heel further, and go over, and the people in it go into the white water.

"Up," said Ward. "Fifty feet more. Now."

She went up. The downwash eased. The water under her went from white to grey.

His knights on the stairs went into the water. He watched them do it in the floods, three of them, off the bottom step, in their kit, after the people in the sea; and the watch's other skiffs came round, and the merfolk on the stairs went in too, on their tails, faster than anybody. They had them out in a minute. All of them. He counted.

A woman was the last. A knight brought her up the stairs over his shoulder, a merwoman on her legs in a skiff-hand's jerkin, limp, with water coming out of her mouth, and laid her on the step in the floods, and a watch officer knelt by her, and she coughed, and coughed, and was sick, and lived.

She sat up. She held on to the knight's arm. She held it with both hands and said something to him, over and over, that Ward could see was thanks even from fifty feet up and three hundred yards off. The knight didn't understand a word and stayed where he was and let her hold on.

Then she let go of him, and turned on the step, and looked up into the floods at the K Amara hanging over her in the dark, enormous, roaring; and she put both hands up at it, open, shaking, and screamed. At the ship. Ward didn't need it put into Kaldo.

A young man came running down the stairs from the yard. A furnace hand, in an apron, with soot all over him. One of the ones who'd come up off the yard stair and stayed in the yard to carry. He came down the steps three at a time into the floods and threw himself down on the wet stone beside the woman and took hold of her.

Ward watched him do it. He didn't look away.

That was what it cost to come at the burn. He'd known there'd be a cost. He'd not known it would be a woman in a skiff, and that her son would be the one off the stair.



Linnet came on the set at half past eight.

"Ward. Linnet. Zale's alive."

He stood at the board.

"He's aboard. Here. He came up out of the sea at the gate steps with Ilenne. He's hurt. Imani's got him." A pause. Her voice changed. "He's... he's not right. His hands. I'll tell you. Not on this." Another pause. "And Ilenne said a thing. On the steps. She said it's her word. Nobody says he lived. Nobody. Not to anyone. Nothing, either way." Linnet stopped. "I'm telling you because you're the only other set that knows. Nobody else heard it from me."

Ward thought about it.

"Then nobody hears it from us," he said. "I'll tell the bridge deck. Nobody on this ship says a word about him. Not yes. Not no." He looked out at the Rim. "Tell her I've got it."

"I'll tell her."

He hung the set up and turned to the officer of the watch and the two men at the helm and the signaller at the board, the only people on the bridge deck who'd heard it, and told them; and watched each of them understand.

The watch's boat came out to him at twenty to nine.

A long boat, eight oars, with a lamp at its bow and a watch officer standing in the stern with a slate, and it came in under the K Amara through the last of the downwash and hailed. Ward took the hailer.

The officer called up, in careful Kaldo, slowly, so that there'd be no mistaking it. The woman from the skiff was alive, and at the station; the watch had her name, Esseth, of the skiff hands. And the watch's order. The land's ship would go out past the markers. At once. To the Grey Mark, as the queen had ordered. Now.

"When the last of my men are off that yard," said Ward.

The officer called back that he understood.

Ward waited. He watched the last of his knights come out of the yard across the edge, the last of the furnace hands go down the water stairs to the skiffs, Arthur and Mira and Bren walking out of the dark into the floods with their faces black and their rifles on their backs. He counted his knights. All of them. He counted them again.

Then he took her out.

On the lift, not the burn; there was no need now for anything but going. Out over the ring's black water with the Rim falling away under her and the dark patch of the zone and the red of the fire behind it, out past the gate with Zale's ship below it lit up in her own floods, out past the inner markers and the outer, where escort three was waiting for her on the sea with her lamps lit and her people at her rail. And west. Back to the Grey Mark, thirty miles, in the dark, with the stone in its iron room under his feet the whole way, and the watch's boat getting smaller behind them on the black water with its one lamp.

Ward stood at the after rail of the bridge deck and watched it go.

He'd done what he'd decided to do. He'd do it again. He thought about the woman on the step with her two hands up at his ship, screaming, and her son with his arms round her; and he thought about Emeric, at the bottom of the sea with no set and no sense of any of it, who'd said in front of the whole court that the stone wouldn't come in, and that if it did it was his.

It had come in. It was his now.

Ward didn't know yet what that was going to cost. He thought it would be a lot.
