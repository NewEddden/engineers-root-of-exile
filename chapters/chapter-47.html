Chapter 47 - Due Diligence

The request came worded so reasonably that Emeric admired it before he distrusted it, and then, because the wording gave him nothing to distrust, stopped distrusting it, which was the first move in a game he did not know he was playing. A man who takes two baronies in a season, the letters said in the round courteous phrasing that regional lords used when they meant something with teeth, invites questions, and questions honorably raised are best honorably answered, and so the neighboring holdings proposed, in a spirit of mutual confidence and no accusation whatsoever, an informal review of the recent transfers, that all might be reassured the thing had been done as lawfully as it appeared. It was framed as due diligence. It was, on its face, entirely fair. Emeric had absorbed two territories at a speed that would make any careful neighbor sit up, and careful neighbors sitting up and asking to see the paper was exactly the behavior a lawful order was supposed to produce. He read it as fair because it was fair, and he made the error there, at the very start, of believing that a fair question could have only a fair thing standing behind it.

He was, if he was honest, pleased. An audit was a paper contest, and paper was the ground he stood most firmly on, the one field in the world where he had never yet been beaten by anyone in this age, and the invitation to be inspected landed in him the way a challenge lands in a man who has spent a year quietly building the exact defense the challenge will test. Let them come. Ferra's books were not merely honest, they were legible, cross-ruled, and reconcilable to the coin, kept by a woman who filed a ledger the way a mason lays a course. The escheat instrument had already survived Keller and the crown's own lawyers. And the appointments, the tender place, the one seam where a hostile reader might have hoped to find a prince's faction wearing a steward's chain, were clean too, because he had made them clean at a cost he could still feel, had turned a loyal man out of a door he had earned so that no auditor walking these books would ever find a body in a chair that fitness had not put there. He had paid for this audit before it was ever called. He simply had not known, paying, that this was the bill it was settling.

Paxton came as the face of it, which Emeric read as a courtesy and which was, in fact, the sharpest edge of the whole thing. Milford was the near neighbor and the ally, the one lord in reach who had chosen Emeric's side when choosing it cost something, and sending Paxton to conduct the review was the group's way of softening a hard errand, an unpleasant necessity carried out by a friendly hand rather than a hostile one. So Emeric read it. He was glad of it, even. He would rather open his books to Paxton than to a stranger with a grievance, and he told himself the choice of messenger was a kindness, and never turned the reading over to look at its other face, which was that an ally sent to measure you is still, whatever else he is, a man sent to measure you, and that the thing Paxton had come to measure was not on any page Ferra could hand him.

The review took four days and found nothing, because there was nothing, and the finding of nothing was thorough. Paxton's people walked the granaries and read the toll-books and traced the ore-counts and set the crown's escheat language beside the actual transfers and found the two agreed. They talked to the new stewards and found them competent and unrelated to the prince by blood, favor, or debt. They even, at Emeric's own suggestion, spoke to Vantic, because a resident creditor who had sat in the barony with a lawful eye on every coin for a year and had every mercenary reason to report a weakness was the single most devastating witness a clean man could offer, and Vantic, asked whether the prince's house was solvent and its books true, said that it was solvent on her terms and that its books were, to her considerable inconvenience, exactly what they claimed to be. There was no thread. Emeric had built it so there would be no thread, and Paxton's people pulled at every place a thread might be and came away with clean hands, and on the fourth evening Paxton closed the last ledger and sat with it under his palm for a moment and said the thing that Emeric filed, that night, under the wrong heading entirely.

"There's nothing here," Paxton said.

He said it quietly, and he was not smiling, and Emeric heard in it the sound of a friend reassured, an ally who had been sent to find rot and was glad, on Emeric's behalf, to have found none, a man laying down a suspicion he had never wanted to carry. That was what Emeric heard. He heard relief. He did not hear the other thing living in the flatness of it, the thing a man says when he has come a long way hoping to find the one lever that would let him sleep at night and has discovered, going home, that the lever does not exist. There was nothing here. Not nothing wrong. Nothing to use. Nothing to catch, nothing to ruin, nothing a careful enemy could take to a magistrate or a council or a court and say, there, that is the crack, that is where he breaks. A dirty lord frightens no one for long, because a dirty lord can be undone the ordinary lawful way, the way Emeric himself had just undone two of them. It is the clean one who cannot be reached, and a clean lord rising this fast, holding two new baronies on paper that will not tear, is a far worse problem to a frightened neighbor than a thief ever was, because the only tools that work on him are the ones no one is supposed to reach for.

Emeric felt the small wrongness at the edge of it, the way he always did, the faint mismatch between the words and the weight under them. And he did with it what he had trained himself, painfully, in a hold and on a bench, not to do with a reading that had a shape, except that the training had a hole in it exactly the shape of his life. He had learned to distrust a clean fit in data. He had learned it hard, had beaten into himself the habit of turning over any set of numbers that agreed too neatly, and he had spent that whole hard-won suspicion, these four days, in the right place by his lights and the wrong place by the world's, checking and rechecking the books for a trap in the arithmetic, hunting the hidden error a hostile auditor might have planted or found, guarding with everything he had the one door he knew how to guard. He never turned the same suspicion on the premise underneath the door. He never asked whether the books were what they had come for. He had tested the answer to exhaustion and never once tested the question, because a room full of frightened men reading his competence as a threat was not, to him, a claim that could be true or false and weighed. It was not a system. It did not have a shape he could put on a table. So he did not put it on a table, and the wrongness, having no column to sit beside, got swept off the ledger the way he swept every reading that came to him bare, and he let Paxton go home, and told himself the matter was closed.

He thought it had been about impropriety. That was the whole of his mistake, laid flat: he thought the neighbors feared that he had cheated, and that a proof he had not cheated was therefore the answer to their fear, and that having furnished the proof, cleanly, exhaustively, in front of an allied witness and a hostile creditor both, he had furnished the answer and could file the account paid. He had solved impropriety. Nobody had been worried about impropriety. Impropriety was the polite dress they had put on the thing so that it could be raised at all, the way due diligence was the polite dress on a delegation come to take the measure of a man they had begun to be afraid of, and the measure they took was not comforting. They had come to learn whether he could be handled the ordinary way. They had learned that he could not. He had shown them, with four days of flawless books and his own eager pride in the flawlessness, that the lawful road to stopping him was a road with nothing on it, and a frightened man who is shown that the safe road is closed does not stop being frightened. He goes looking for the other road.

And Emeric, who had spent a real and irrecoverable thing to make those books clean, who had closed a door on Merrin and men like Merrin precisely so that no page in his house would ever give an enemy a handle, sat that night with the quiet satisfaction of a machine that had met its test and held, and did not understand that the cleanness itself had been the danger all along, that a handle at least is a thing an enemy reaches for in the daylight where you can see the hand, and that by leaving them no handle he had not made himself safe. He had only made certain that whatever came for him next would have to come from somewhere the books did not reach, in a currency no audit measured, on the far colder logic of men like the old blood of Aldany, who had watched the review from a distance and drawn from its clean result the single conclusion Emeric had worked all year to prevent anyone from being able to prove and had now, instead, proven for them past any doubt: that this one could not be caught, and would therefore, in the end, have to be broken.

He filed the audit under wins. It was the most expensive one he ever recorded, and he recorded it as free, and he slept well, which he would later count among the things he was least able to forgive himself.
