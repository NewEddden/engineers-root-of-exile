Book 2, Chapter 8: The Ring

They had the argument about the stone at two in the afternoon on the Wednesday, at the K Amara's table, with the ring four hours off and nobody yet able to see it.

Ferra had known it was coming since the wall water. She'd known since March, if she was honest; since the wall water, the night Ilenne brought her mother's answer, and Emeric made Ilenne a promise, and Ferra's pencil stopped on the page and didn't go on. She'd left a space in the book that night. She'd never filled it.

There were four of them. Emeric, in the chair with the cushion behind his ribs, because Imani had let him across on condition that he sat in it. Ward. Armand. Ferra. Nobody else, and the door shut, and the K Amara's second on the bridge outside with orders that nobody came in.

"It's in the hold," said Ward. "It's been in the hold since Kaldo, in the iron room, under my hand. Nobody aboard but the four of us and Bram knows what it is. It's no danger to anybody unless somebody wants it to be." He had his hands flat on the table. "And you want to tell their queen about it."

"First thing," said Emeric. "At the door. Before anything else is said."

"Why?"

"Because I told Ilenne I would." Emeric didn't raise his voice. "At the wall water, in March. Her mother hears it from me, before we go in."

"It's a promise to a girl," said Ward. "Her mother's never heard it. Nobody would know if it went unkept."

"It's a promise," said Emeric.

Ward held his eyes. Ferra watched them both.

"Then I'll say what it costs," said Ward, "and you can pay it knowing. A stone that makes a dead zone is the one thing on earth that city fears more than us, because a dead zone is what sank them. You tell their queen we've got one, she does the only thing a queen can do. She bars the hull that carries it. The K Amara. My strongest ship, three hundred aboard, the knights, the hospital beds we'll want if this goes wrong, and Ferra." He didn't look at Ferra. "You'll have the king's wife and the fleet's teeth parked outside a foreign city on the far side of their markers, where I can't reach the door in less than an hour, while you walk in with your brother and a mermaid and no sword. That's what telling her buys you. That's the bill."

"I know."

"And not telling her?"

"Nobody would know," said Emeric. "It's in an iron room. No sense they've got would read it through iron. Ilenne didn't. She sailed with it from Kaldo and never felt it." He looked at the table. "That's why I have to tell her. If they could find it, it wouldn't matter whether I said. They can't. So the only thing between that stone and her mother's city is whether I keep my word."

Armand had been quiet. He spoke now, from his chair, slowly.

"He's right, Ward. And you're right. That's the trouble with it." He turned his cane in his hands. "If we're found to have brought it in secret, later, by anyone, we're the people who carried the thing that killed their city to their door and smiled. Nothing we say after that is believed. Not the binding. Not anything."

"And if we're found to have brought it at all?"

"Then we're the people who told them," said Armand. "It's not much. It's the difference between a guest and a thief."

Ward sat back. He looked at Emeric, and then at Ferra, as if she might help him, and she didn't.

"Then do it," he said. "I've said it. I'll take her out past their markers myself, the minute their officer tells us where the markers are, and I'll keep her there till their queen says otherwise." He stood up. "And when the door goes wrong, as it will, I'll be an hour away, and you'll know why."

He went out. The door shut behind him.

Emeric looked at Ferra. She'd felt him look at her before he did it, along the ring, the question in it.

"Write it?" he said.

"No," said Ferra.

She closed the book.

"Not a line," she said. "Not in this book, not in the ship's log, not on a slip to Kaldo. A line about that stone is a thing someone can read. It can be copied. It can be taken, or found in a drawer, or sent home in the wrong cipher, or opened by somebody whose colour goes slate. The only safe record of it is none." She put her hand flat on the cover. "Ward knows. Bram knows. Armand. You. Me. Ilenne, by tomorrow, and her mother. That's the record."

"You wrote the rifle."

"The rifle's at the bottom of the sea," said Ferra. "Five miles down, in the dark, in a case, with nothing in it that can hurt anyone. It's lost. That stone isn't lost. It's in our hold." She stood up. "That's the difference. One of them's gone and the other one's ours."



The ring came up out of the sea at a quarter past five.

She was on the bridge deck at the forward glass when it did. First a smudge on the horizon that she took for weather, dark and low, with a smear above it the colour of a coal fire's smoke. Then it was smoke. Then it was a line of black islands under the smoke, very low, broken, with gaps of sea between them, curving away north and south out of sight in both directions, as if somebody had drawn a great circle on the water with a burnt stick and the stick had skipped.

The smoke came off the islands themselves. As they came closer she saw why: chimneys. Not one or two. Dozens. Stone chimneys, short and thick, on the black rock, with the smoke going up from them in grey and white columns that leaned over together in the wind, and at the feet of the chimneys long low buildings with their roofs smoking too, and fires showing through doors. Furnaces. Kilns. There were cranes on the islands, wooden cranes, three and four storeys high, swinging. There were causeways from island to island, low, of the same black stone, with the sea slapping at them, and things moving on the causeways that she couldn't make out yet.

And inside the ring, through the gaps between the islands, the water was lit.

It was the last of the day and the light was going, and inside the ring the sea had a glow in it that came from underneath. Faint, at first. Then plain. A greenish-gold light, down in the water, in patches, as though a town's windows had been sunk a fathom deep and left lit.

"Below," said Ilenne, behind her.

Ferra hadn't heard her come up. She stood at Ferra's shoulder with her hood back and her hands on the rail, and she wasn't holding anything at all. She was the colour of the glow in the water, more or less, all over.

"That's the city," said Ferra.

"That is Below. The city is all of it. Up there is the Rim, the fires, the work, the rock." Ilenne pointed at the smoking islands. "Down there is Below. My mother is down there. Very far down. Where the light stops." She was quiet. "I can feel it from here. All of it. I didn't think I would cry."

"Are you crying?"

"No," said Ilenne. "I'm only saying."

Out beyond the K Discovery the long smooth place on the sea had stopped.

It stopped dead, a mile short of the nearest island, and lay there, and didn't move. The K Discovery stopped behind it. The convoy came up into its order behind the K Discovery and stopped, and on the bridge deck the K Amara's second said "All stop" quietly to the helm, as if it might hear.

"It goes no further," said Ilenne. "There are the markers. You see? There, and there." Two posts on reefs, a mile apart, the same grey reef-stone as the pillar at the wall, with the same coloured glass at the top, catching the last of the sun. Further in, more of them, a line of them, curving round. "Outside the markers is the sea. Inside is the city's water. It does not go inside. It is not allowed. It waits for my mother to tell it go home." She smiled, a little. "It is very glad."

"Birds," said Sorrel's voice on the set, from the K Discovery. "On the outer rocks. On the cliffs on the north island. I'd call them birds. They're too big to be birds."

Ferra took the glass. The north island had a cliff on its seaward side, black, high, streaked white, and there were shapes on the ledges of it, standing, hunched, as cormorants stand. Then one of them opened its wings to settle them, and the wings were wider than a man was tall, and it wasn't holding them the way a bird holds wings. It held them the way a man holds a cloak.

It turned its head toward the convoy. All of them had turned their heads toward the convoy. She counted eleven before the light went off the cliff.

"Ilenne?"

"Winged," said Ilenne. "The winged people. They live on the outside rocks. They watch. They always watch. They are watching you come in, and you have a black ship." She looked along the K Amara's port side, scorched from the rack bank to the keel. "They will tell everybody about the black ship by tomorrow."

"Tell them what?"

"That something burned you," said Ilenne. "And you came anyway."



The watch boat came out at a quarter to six, from a gap between two islands to the south, and Ferra watched it come all the way.

It was a long boat, low, black, sharp at both ends, with a short mast and a dark sail furled on it, and it came on without the sail, fast, with no oars and no engine that she could hear or see, cutting a clean wake across the evening water. There were six in it. Five sat. One stood in the bow.

The convoy came down onto the sea to meet it, because Ilenne said that was the first thing: on the water, not in the air. "In the air you are a thing over them," she said. "Nobody likes a thing over them. On the water you are a ship. Ships they know." All eight came down. The K Amara settled onto the swell with her burned side to the setting sun, and Ward had the boarding platform let down on the starboard side to the waterline, the low one the tenders used, so that whoever came could stand at their own level and not be looked down on; and Emeric went down to it, slowly, with his arm strapped and Imani's eye on him from the rail above, and stood on it, and Ilenne went down beside him, and Ferra went down after them with the book under her arm.

The boat came alongside the platform and stopped. It didn't touch. It stopped a yard off, exactly, and held there on the swell without a line, and the one standing in the bow looked at them.

He was merfolk. She'd seen Ilenne often enough by now to know it. He stood on two legs in the boat's bow, in a long dark close-fitted coat that shone faintly as if it were wet, and he was tall, grey-skinned, with his hair cut short and something at his throat like a gorget of pale stone. He had a long slate in the crook of one arm, the size of a ledger. His colour, at the throat, was a still even pale grey, and stayed there, and didn't move at all. He looked at Emeric for some time, and at Ilenne a moment longer than at Emeric, and his colour didn't move then either.

Then he spoke, and Ilenne spoke after him.

"He is Taurre," she said. "He keeps the watch. The caldera watch, for this side, for tonight." A pause while he went on. "He says: eight ships. He says: one burned. He writes it." The man had a stylus and was writing on the slate, without looking down. "He says, which of you speaks for them?"

"I do," said Emeric. "Emeric. King of Randillia."

Ilenne put it into her own tongue. Taurre wrote. He said something else.

"He says, the king, then. He gives you the port orders." Ilenne's voice had gone formal, flattened, the way it went when she was saying the words exactly and not making them into Kaldo. "One. The ships stand off. Outside the ring, on the sea. Not in the air. Never in the air over the ring, over the Rim, over Below. Two. Nobody of the dry land goes into the city's water except where the watch says, with a pilot in front, a Rim pilot, always. Three. Guests of the dry land go only into the hard domes, the dry ones, the bells, with a Keeper beside them and a pilot. Never into the wet. Four. The Breath is shut to you." She stopped, and asked him something, and he answered. "The Breath is a place. Down. You don't go. He says it is not for you and it does not change."

"Understood," said Emeric. "Tell him. All four."

She told him. Taurre wrote.

"And tell him," said Emeric, "that one of our ships carries a thing your mother must hear of before that ship comes any closer. I'll tell her myself, at the door. Until she's heard it, I'd ask him to say where that ship may lie, and it'll lie there."

Ilenne put that into her tongue too, slowly. Ferra watched Taurre while she did. For the first time his colour did something: not much, a flicker at the throat from pale grey to a darker grey and back, gone in the time it took to see it. He asked a question.

"Which ship," said Ilenne.

"This one," said Emeric.

Taurre looked up at the K Amara. At the whole black length of her port side, at the knights at her apron rails, at the necks along her upper deck, at the scorched planking above the after store. Then back down at Emeric. He said four words.

"The outer markers," said Ilenne. "Beyond them. Outside. This ship lies past the outer markers, and does not come back inside them until the queen's word says. The others, outside the ring, on the sea, where he says." She listened. "The little ship that goes under, and Zale's ship, they may come in. To the gate. Tomorrow, in the light, with the watch's boat beside and a Rim pilot in front." She listened again. "Because there is nothing on them that breaks a rule."

"Thank him," said Emeric.

Taurre wrote for some time. Then he looked up again, and this time he didn't look at Emeric. He looked at the burned side of the K Amara, and at Ferra, and asked a question in a different voice, a plainer one, the voice of a clerk.

"He asks what burned," said Ilenne. "And if anything of yours is in his water."

Ferra stepped forward to the edge of the platform. She'd known this was coming too. She'd read the merfolk file at the K Amara's table three times on the way, and she had the page open.

"Tell him," she said, "a dragon's fire burned it. Four days ago. In the high air, a day east of here. Fourteen of ours burned. Nobody died." She waited while Ilenne put it over, and Taurre wrote. "And tell him one thing of ours went into his sea. A weapon. A long gun, in a wooden case, that fell over the side in the high air when the fire came. It fell into the outer water. This is where." She read the position off the page, the latitude and the longitude as Sorrel had fixed them at half past five that morning, slowly, figure by figure, and Ilenne put each figure over and Taurre wrote each one. "Its mark is this." She read the mark off the page as it was stamped on the stock. "It's miles down. It can't be brought up. It can't hurt anyone where it is. But it's ours, and it's in his water, and he should have it written down."

Taurre stopped writing. He looked at her over the slate.

Then he said something, quite short, and his colour did a thing she didn't know, a faint warm shift across the grey, and Ilenne made a small sound that might have been a laugh.

"He says," said Ilenne, "that is how a guest should come into a house. Saying what she dropped on the step." She glanced at Ferra sideways. "He says he wishes the dry land had been so careful the last time."

Taurre wrote the last of it, and drew a line under it on the slate with the stylus, and Ferra recognised the gesture so exactly that something in her chest went warm and odd for a moment, as if she'd seen her own hand on a stranger's arm.



The pilot boat came out behind the watch's, smaller and quicker and painted, red and white, with a flag on a staff at its stern; and it didn't stop a yard off. It came right up to the platform with a thump, and the merman in its stern, who was short and broad and grinning, stood up and said a good deal very fast.

Ilenne's colour went flat while she listened. Ferra had learned that one in the lessons: the courtesy colour, the polite nothing.

"He is a pilot of the Rim," she said. "He says: no ship of the dry land comes to the docks without a Rim pilot before it. That is the Rim's right. It was the Rim's right before the war, it is the Rim's right now. He says the queen's old one brought you to the markers, and that is very nice, and it does not matter. He says from the markers to the gate is his water, and there is a fee." She paused. "He says the fee is the fee. He says it like everybody knows the fee."

"What's the fee?" said Ferra.

Ilenne asked. The pilot told her. Ilenne's mouth moved.

"It's a lot," she said. "For two little ships. It is what they ask a big trader. He thinks you don't know."

Taurre, in his boat a yard off, hadn't moved. He was watching the pilot with no colour at all. The queen's watch had brought them to the gate, and the Rim's pilot was charging them for the walk, and the watch wasn't going to say a word about it, because the Rim's right was the Rim's right; Ferra could see that much in how still he stood.

She'd read the file. The Rim was a ring of furnaces on rock in the middle of the sea. It made glass and stoneware and fired clay and a hundred other things out of what the rock gave it; and it was short of iron. The rock didn't give it. The file said so in one line, and she'd underlined it.

"Tell him," she said, "we'll pay in iron."

Ilenne looked at her. Then she put it over.

The pilot stopped grinning.

Ferra had sent to the K Amara's hold an hour before for what she thought she'd need, and it was on the platform behind her in a crate. She opened it. It was bar iron from the Works, in the plain flat bars Bram's mill rolled for the yard's smiths, each one stamped with the Works' mark, twenty to the crate, grey and clean in the last light. She took one out and held it out across the gap.

The pilot took it. He held it in both hands, and turned it over, and weighed it, and his colour went through something she couldn't follow, fast, all up his throat and into his face and down his arms, so plainly that the men in the K Amara's boarding party behind her shifted their feet.

"How many?" said Ferra.

The pilot said a number. He said it much more quietly than he'd said the fee.

"Eight," said Ilenne. "He says eight. For the two little ships, there and back." She looked at the crate, and at the pilot, who couldn't take his eyes off it. "Ferra. That is a quarter of the fee he asked. And he is ashamed he asked less." She hesitated. "And he will tell everybody. Tonight. Everybody on the Rim. The dry people have iron. Bar iron, clean, like this, in boxes."

"I know," said Ferra. She counted eight bars into the pilot's boat herself, one at a time, and the pilot watched every one go in as though it might change its mind. "That's partly why."



Taurre didn't go at once. Ferra had thought he would. He stayed in his boat a yard off the platform while the pilot rowed away with the iron, and the light went, and lamps came up along the Rim's black islands in rows and clusters, and the glow in the water inside the ring came up brighter as the sky went down.

"Ask him," said Ferra, "about the silver ones."

Inside the ring, down through the clear water where the light came from, she could see them now that the sun was gone: the domes. They went down the inside of the ring's wall in tiers, like seats in a theatre, one below another below another, into the dark. Most of them were green-gold and dim, lit from inside, and she could see shapes moving in them, swimming. They were full of sea. But here and there among them was a dome that was different. Brighter, harder, silver, a dome with a skin on it like a drop of mercury, and nothing swimming in it at all.

Ilenne asked. Taurre looked where Ferra was pointing and answered as though it were the plainest thing in the world.

"Bells," said Ilenne. "He says those are bells. Dry. Air in them, pressed as hard as the sea presses, so the sea does not come in. For things water spoils. Records. Dried food. Bolts of the cloth from the south." She listened. "And for guests. For guests who breathe air. When the city hosts people of the dry land. Every hard dome has a bell beyond its door for that." She smiled faintly. "He says the dwarves, mostly. The dwarves come to trade, and they cannot swim, they sink like their own anvils, so they stay in the bells and complain. Two live in my mother's top city all the year round. He says: others, when they come."

"Others," said Ferra.

Taurre said something more. It went on longer, in a lower voice, and Ilenne's colour went quiet while she listened.

"He says it like a thing everybody knows," she said. "He says: once we floated. Our cities. On the water, on stones, the greatest of them on top of the sea. Then the war came over the water, and the dead ground came with it, and the stones went out, and the First City went down with everybody on it." She stopped. "And he says: the rock holds us now. That's all he says. Like a saying." She looked at the black ring with its chimneys. "It is a saying. A city may rise above the sea. The rock must hold it."

Ferra felt Emeric, beside her on the platform, go still.

She didn't look at him. She knew what he was thinking about. She was thinking about it too: an iron room in the K Amara's hold, two decks under their feet, and the thing in it, and a city that had gone down with everyone on it when the stones went out.

Emeric asked the next one himself.

"Ask him," he said, "if there are humans out here. People like us. Living past the wall."

Ilenne asked. Taurre answered at once, without any interest, the way a man tells you the time.

"Yes," said Ilenne. "Some. Not many. Here and there. They live with other peoples, mostly, in other peoples' towns." She listened. "And one people of their own, far, far to the west. Many. They keep to themselves. Nobody goes there much." She paused, and asked him something, and he said a word and shrugged. "He doesn't know their name. He says it like I would say: oh, yes, them."

Ferra looked at Emeric's face then, because she couldn't not.

It was the thing the fleet had sailed on. Every sheet she'd drawn for this voyage had had it somewhere at the bottom, unwritten: that past the wall there might be humans who'd gone with the old peoples willingly, a long time ago, and that eight ships of humans might be taken for them, and not for the others. It had been a hope with no shape. Now a watch officer in a black boat had given it a shape in four sentences and a shrug.

And he'd given it without any doubt at all about where they'd come from. He knew. He'd known before the boat left the gap. The merfolk knew which side of the wall these ships had sailed from, and they'd never once been fooled, and wouldn't be.

Emeric said thank you, through Ilenne. His face gave nothing. Through the ring she felt him put it away somewhere careful, for later, and close the door on it.

"Ask her one more," said Armand.

He'd come down to the platform while they talked, slowly, with his cane, and was standing behind Ferra with his rug over his arm. Taurre had looked at him once when he came, at the stick and then at the young face above it, and his colour had done the small warm thing again: respect, Ferra thought, for the stick, and a question about the face.

"Not him," said Armand. "Her." He looked at Ilenne. "Your court. Tomorrow and after. Eight armed hulls at their gate, one of them burned and barred. What will they make of us? Not your mother. The old men."

Ilenne didn't ask Taurre. She answered herself, after a moment, and her colour went the grey-blue of a thing being turned over.

"The old men," she said, "will talk about a promise."

"What promise?"

"An old one. Between my people, all the old peoples, and the dry land. From after the war. A long time." She frowned. "Something about who stays where. I don't know the words. I told you. Nobody I know has read it. Nobody alive, maybe. The old men know there is one. They will say: these ships break it. They won't say how, because they don't know how. They will just say it, again and again, in the hall, until it is true."

Armand nodded slowly. He didn't say anything. Ferra watched him take it and turn it over and put it somewhere in the long patient place where he kept things to read later, and she thought she'd see it again.

The set on the platform's post clicked, then, and a voice came out of it that she knew: Ned, from the K Endeavor.

"Challenger to the king. If I might. For tomorrow." He sounded pleased with himself. "We've rehearsed it. We go under outside the ring, quiet, well off the gate, run in deep on the stones, and come up right at the steps. No fuss. No pilot in front. Nobody sees us till we're there. It's what she's built for, and it's the quiet way in, and..."

"No," said Ilenne.

She'd turned from the platform's edge so fast her hair swung.

"No," she said again, into the set. "Ned. No. Not up from under." Her colour had gone a hard bright white at the throat. "Up from under is how a hunter comes. Up from below, at the door, is teeth. Everybody at that door sees you come up under them and every one of them is ready to kill you before your hatch opens. That is what it means. Always. Since before the war. You never come up under anybody."

The set was silent.

"Level," said Ilenne. "Never under. I teach it every lesson. I teach Ned too. He forgets, because it is a boat." She took a breath. "We go down outside. Then we come up, outside, far off. Then we come in on the top of the water. With the pilot. Slowly. Where everybody sees. Level."

"Level," said Ned's voice, after a moment, rather small. "Right. Sorry. On the top. With the pilot."

"Is fine," said Ilenne. "Now you know."

Taurre had watched all of this from his boat without moving. He couldn't have understood a word of the Kaldo. Ferra saw him look at Ilenne's throat, at the hard white there, and then at the set on its post that the voice had come out of, and then, for the first time, at Ilenne herself, properly, the queen's daughter on a land ship's platform telling a land ship's crew how to come to her mother's door. His colour moved, very slightly. He bent his head to her, a fraction. Then he gave an order to the five in the boat, and it went away into the dark toward the gap it had come from, fast, with no oars and no engine, leaving a clean wake.



Maeve went across to Zale's ship at eight, before the K Amara stood out, in the K Amara's boat with her kit bag and her spear.

It had been settled at the table at two, after the stone, in a sentence: the K Amara would lie past the markers, and anyone who was meant to be at the door had better not be on the K Amara when she went. Emeric would go in in the Challenger, with Ilenne, and Ned and Noll to sail her; Zale in his own ship beside her. And Maeve, with Zale, because she was Zale's sister and the king's, and because she'd asked, and because Ward had looked at her and said a sister at the door was a thing their court would understand.

Mira saw her down the ladder. Ferra saw that from the bridge deck: Mira at the head of the boarding ladder, not saying anything, and Maeve on the third rung down looking up at her, and then going on down, and the boat pulling off. Mira stood at the head of the ladder until the boat reached Zale's ship. Then she went back to the apron.

At half past eight Ward came up onto the bridge deck and said, "Now."

The K Amara lifted off the sea. Not far, a few feet, just enough to move, because the port orders said on the water and Ward meant to keep them to the letter, and she went out through the convoy on the lowest veins she had, past escort one and hull four and the K Endeavor with the Challenger slung under her, past the K Discovery, past Zale's ship sitting on the swell with its lamp lit, out toward the line of glass-topped posts at the edge of the dark. Ferra stood at the after glass and watched the convoy go small behind them. The lights of the Rim behind it. The glow in the water behind that.

They passed the outer markers at nine. Ward took her a mile beyond them, as Taurre had said, and set her down on the sea, and had the sea anchors put out, and stood for a while at the forward glass with his hands behind his back, looking at the ring.

It was a long way off now. A circle of small fires on the black sea, low down, with a faint green glow inside it, as if someone had dropped a lamp into a well.

"An hour," said Ward, to the glass. "If I go flat out. If they let me."

Ferra came and stood beside him.

"You were right," she said. "About what it costs."

"I know I was right." He didn't turn round. "He was right too. That's the worst of it." He was quiet. "I've the knights on this ship and the best guns in the fleet, and the king's going in tomorrow through a door in a wall with his brother and a mermaid and his hands empty. And I'll be here. Watching a lamp in a well."

She didn't say anything. There wasn't anything to say that he didn't know.

After a while she opened the book, on the shelf under the glass, by the lamp, and found the page with the space on it that she'd left in March. She looked at the space for some time.

Then she closed the book again, with the space still in it.
