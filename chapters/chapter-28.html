Book 3, Batch 9: With

Chapters 77–85 | June – Saturday 8 December, Year 20

The Seed Act is law and the first fruit has fallen: about seven hundred seeds counted, the replant done close by close, and about five hundred trees now standing. Reuben Orley walks between the bars. Elected reeves command the small towns' watches. The Titles Act waits for the end of the hereditary council's year in late September. The research pen at the Claim holds one live pup, and a sparrow at Wayford's close neither answered Runa nor refused her. Gethin is out of Turnstone. Corrick and Kezia come out on 14 July.

This batch closes the long argument between the king and the man who once burned the houses of the powerful for the powerless. Corrick comes out of prison and learns to use the slow law: a docket number, an appeal, a wait. In the Assembly a political enemy reads aloud the first proof of where the king is going, that he means one day to end the crown, and the king, asked straight out, does not deny it. Then the king comes to Cinderhale as an applicant, waits outside the League's hall, and asks them to work with him. Corrick notices the word.

Around them the new order produces results its makers dislike. The Titles Act passes with seats the king thinks unfair, and he signs. The first widened election goes to a duke and a mill lawyer. A man lowers his hand halfway at a League vote because the king is in the room, and Maudie asks the king to leave, and he goes. In between, a pumpkin patch opens at Milford, the royal family goes on a ticket like everyone else's, and the king gets honestly lost in a maze.

Chapter 77: Out

June is quiet. The new pairs root at every close, the pup in the research pen grows to its measured mark, and Reuben Orley climbs the chapel stairs on his stick.

On Saturday 14 July, early, at Turnstone's gate, through Kezia's eyes, she is out, one year to the day. Maudie is at the spur halt again, this time with two coats, and Gethin with her, because he said he would come and then nearly did not. Kezia has her drawings in a satchel, and the warder who liked them hands her the last one through the gate. Corrick walks out behind her carrying nothing and does not make a speech, which Kezia notices, because he used to.

On Thursday 19 July, at the League's door in Cinderhale in the evening, through Corrick's eyes, the night watchman from the Hearth's demolition comes. He does not ask whether the cause was worthy. He wants to hear Corrick say that the man standing in the house was never asked whether it should come down. He says Corrick asked him to walk out, and did not ask him whether he wanted the house gone. No, Corrick says. The watchman asks whether he knows the difference now. Corrick says he knows it mattered, and he did not know it enough then. Corrick offers him money for the disrupted night. The watchman takes none; his wages were never interrupted. He neither forgives Corrick nor condemns everything the League has done, and he leaves when he chooses. Corrick stands in the doorway after him a long time and does not follow.

On Friday 27 July, at the Motion Shop, through Ada Lovell's eyes, a scrap brood core, a first-stage pup's core bought cracked from the Saltreach chandlers' house at the formula price, sits in a cradle wired to a steel arm on the bench, a one-finger gripper and an elbow of Tess's. Ada wears the cap herself, because she wrote the tables and wants to feel whether they are right. She thinks about closing her right hand. The arm closes, slow and jerky, on nothing, and the whole shop goes quiet. Then Wren laughs out loud. The rule holds: the core reads the cap's signals and drives the arm, and nothing goes back into Ada. Imani checks her afterwards and finds a tired, delighted woman and nothing else.

Chapter 78: Docket

On Monday 23 July, at the hours inspectorate's county office, through Corrick's eyes, he reports a thirty-two-hour violation at a Cinderhale dye works, where a night shift was kept on through a day. The clerk writes it up and gives him a docket number. He asks how long. The clerk tells him the truth, which is that nobody knows.

Because the king has no heir yet, and a kingdom that has lived through a succession war wants the rule in print, a Penhallow member moves that the Assembly write the succession into statute, and the debate takes written submissions from the public. On Wednesday 8 August, through Ferra's eyes, Tullio's submission is read by his counsel, and printed the same morning by Tullio's own press from his letters out of prison. The Bank Article lets the Assembly appoint and remove the Board's Holder, so the crown has already given up the money, and a king who gives up the money means to give up the crown. A Tarrowmede member asks the king from the floor whether it is true. Emeric does not deny the broad intention. He says the crown should end when there are laws strong enough to carry the country without it, that there are not yet, and that he will not hurry it. Ferra, who told him years ago when he first said where he meant to go that they would kill him for it, hears that line in her head the whole time he is speaking. Nobody kills him. Two members walk out, and one of them comes back. Harrold, in the gallery, has known for seven years, and his face says so.

On Friday 10 August, at the castle's back gate, through Emeric's eyes, Corrick comes, and he is angry first, because a political enemy printed the first usable evidence of the king's real goal. You knew where you were going, Corrick says. Yes. Then why did Tullio have to tell the country? Because he feared what announcing it would do before there was a law to carry it. Corrick says that may explain his silence, and it does not give those years back. Emeric accepts the distinction. Corrick leaves without a handshake, and Emeric accepts that he is owed none.

Chapter 79: One Seed

On Tuesday 14 August, in the vault, through Osric's eyes, the first quarterly reconciliation under two signatures comes up one keel short: the Inspector of the Groves' book and Osric's agree on the count at the drop, and disagree with the vault by one. Lowell Tilley, a picket at Milford's third close, a good one with gambling debts nobody knew about, confesses before lunch. He palmed a seed from a tin at the drop, before the count, and sold it to a Fairhythe chandler for a year's pay. The seed is recovered from the chandler's strongbox that night and goes back on the Roll under its keel. The buyer, who was to collect it at the month's end, never comes, and is never found; the chandler can name only a letter with no name and a sum paid in old coin.

On Tuesday 21 August, in Keller's court, through Ferra's eyes, Tilley gets two years at Turnstone for trading a blank seed, and the chandler one year for holding an unrolled blank. The heaviest charge, selling abroad, is not brought, because nobody can prove where the seed was going. Osric visits Tilley at Turnstone once, because he hired him. On Monday 27 August the Mage Corps breaks ground for the Grand Library beside the Grand Museum and the campus, and Ferra signs the first draw on its budget line and writes "paper and film" in the margin, because someone on the committee asked about stones.

On Saturday 1 September, in Ferra's office, through Emeric's eyes, Ferra's new margin holds eleven Watcher sightings since May, sparrows, a thrush, two crows and a hare at Milford, all at grove closes and none answering the pickets' sea-talker or Runa, and her air column has risen in the same months in the same shape. She lays one sheet over the other and holds them to the window. Privately he thinks the sentence he does not say yet: every good thing they do is loud. The Isle's guard woke to digging.

From Monday 20 to Friday 24 August the king keeps a promise made in Book 2: Ilenne's price. The K Endeavor carries the Challenger, with its new slick coat under the slip plating, to Aumere, takes eleven of the old families of the shallows aboard in mana-held cabins on Imani's dosed water, and flies them across the wall to the outer western water off Ostrellin. On the descent to the wreck the stones die in a dead patch in the thin water, the same thing that took the city; the latch lets the drop weights go, she rises on her own buoyancy, and the motor, the floods and the battery lamps carry on, because none of them is magic. Ilenne says that this is how it happened to them, and nobody makes it a metaphor. The second descent goes down on ballast and the motor alone. The families look on the First City through the glass nose for as long as the dose allows, and do not speak, and nobody from Kaldo describes it to anyone afterwards.

Chapter 80: Applicant

On Monday 3 September the Assembly's committee publishes its criteria and picks Cinderhale, Wayford and Saltreach for pilot local institutions. On Saturday 15 September, through Gethin's eyes, the king walks up from the station to the League's hall in Cinderhale on his own and waits outside while the League decides whether to hear him. Gethin, who still will not sit in a room with the king, waits outside too. He says half the south road calls him a demon king, and he walked up from the station on his own; how does he walk about like a normal man? Emeric tells him plainly: he keeps a skin shield up all the time, thinner than his coat and fed from his core, with padding under it that does not drop in a dead zone, and his sense is never off. He demonstrates a little: he tells Gethin where Maudie is standing in the hall behind them, that the boy on the roof across the lane has been there since the station, and that a dog is under the third cart. Then he nods at the pebble in Gethin's hand. Gethin flicks it at his cheek, and it stops a finger's width short and drops. He does not oversell it: the shield stops the pebble, not the push, a dead zone takes it off, and a knight's charged edge goes through it. Gethin says, well, isn't magic nice. Yes, it is, Emeric says, and in a couple of months he plans to make this more readily available to all of them; he just has to work through what could go wrong first. Gethin puts his hands in his pockets, regrown fingers and all, and says nothing grateful. Then Maudie opens the door.

Inside, through Maudie's eyes, Emeric says he came to ask them to work with us. Corrick says: you said with. He did. Corbin, Corrick says, offered a place working for you. Emeric says Corbin offered the job he once would have offered, and it was the wrong one. Corrick says the king will need them to say no to him. Yes, publicly, when the evidence calls for it. Maudie adds: and sometimes when we simply disagree. That too. He reads out the proposed town institutions: a removable elected reeve, protected assembly, inspectable accounts with privacy safeguards, lay justices for limited local matters, appeal to the circuit, protected nominations and petitions, secret ballots, and a register held by the district beyond the reeve's control. On firearms the same principle holds: no civic militia, and public watches only under statute, with independent inventories and reviewable licences. Corrick dislikes it and says so. Emeric defends the safeguards and does not claim his own government can never abuse them.

On Monday 17 September Corrick's complaint is eight weeks old and unresolved. On Wednesday 19 September, in the circuit's listing room, through Corrick's eyes, he appeals the delay through the circuit, the court he asked the League to boycott in December. He does not call the five together; he does not know where they are, and does not try. The court wants dates, the inspectorate's published backlog, and a remedy it can lawfully give. He is listed for November and adjourned once. Nothing is won.

On Saturday 22 September, in the League's hall, through Maudie's eyes, the League sets its terms. Corrick has three conditions: the right to criticise without reprisal, the right to withdraw, and no king in the room while the League votes. Maudie adds a fourth: Corrick speaks only for himself unless the League authorises him. The League votes on limited participation without Emeric in the room, and carries it. Corrick stays outside office. The minute states the scope of the League's consent, so nobody can later call it loyalty.

Chapter 81: A Stranger's Claim

On Thursday 27 September, at Wayford's county office on its weekly redemption day, through the eyes of Nan Wicks, a carter's widow, she collects what she is owed. Her husband drove for a carrying firm that kept him on the road thirty-six hours before the axle went over. The inspector found the logbook, and the circuit awarded. She collects forty-two silver in new notes at the counter, signs for it, counts it twice, and goes to buy shoes for her boys. No king, no Corrick, no Maudie. The clerk who pays her does not know her name until she signs it. The absence of a rescuer is the victory.

The hereditary council's year ends at midnight that Thursday. On Saturday 29 September Kaldo holds its harvest festival. On Wednesday 3 October, through Corbin's eyes, the Titles Act passes its final vote by a wider margin than the first, the March's members voting for it now that their concession is in. Hereditary jurisdiction ends. Civil courts pass district by district to elected ones. Lawful tolls are compensated over twenty years. The March reform committee gets its guaranteed seats and a referendum on its civil franchises, set for next spring. The first widening of the Assembly, with new seats for the eastern hills, the south road and the March, is set for election on Saturday 10 November. Agatha does not clap. On Thursday, at the castle, Emeric tells Corbin once that he dislikes the unequal seats, guaranteed to one region by right and not by number. He signs. The council refiles its lawsuit on Monday 8 October now that the Act is law, and on 30 November Keller dismisses it: compensation by statute, at assessed value, over a stated term with interest, is not a taking at a discount. The hereditary council has nothing left to do and stops meeting. On Wednesday 10 October the beast line is renewed for two years, to 13 October Year 22, with the quarterly reports and the one pen; the July report's line about the pup in the pen is read into the record, nobody objects, and the crews' man, who had prepared a speech, is surprised.

On Friday 12 October, at Turnstone's gate, Elwin Gorran walks out to a wheel that is still turning. The miller still does not know why. Vantic hears it from her clerk and says nothing.

The Titles Act also carries an election clause: no private money in any campaign for a seat. Each candidate's costs come from a public allowance, and a gift to a candidate is a crime for the one who gives it and the one who takes it. Corbin signs it with the rest and thinks it's the only part the old houses will really fear.

Chapter 82: The Patch

The orc valleys' pumpkin seed first showed green in Milford's glass houses in March two years ago. The first year's crop was a seed-increase strip, and Hollis kept most of last year's for seed and selection, three rows in and never from the field edge, his grandmother's rule. This year, through Hollis's eyes, Milford's upland grows one whole standard block for the first time, about a hundred acres and about a hundred thousand fruit. Ferra's rule is one pumpkin free per registered household, on the card like the ration, and more at the weighing table at a posted price. Nobody has to come: the crews cut most of the block, and the farm line carries the households' pumpkins to every ration hall in the four states the week before Hollow Night. About ten acres beside the farm line's halt are left on the vine for people who want to pick their own. Ferra splits the picking over six days on three weekends, capped at about four thousand a day by the patch trains, a half-hour run, and the road, so the line is not swamped: Milford's own growers first on Saturday 13 October, Kaldo's city in two halves on the Sundays, Grayrock and Wayford on the 20th, the neighbours from the south road on visitor cards on the 27th, and a sweep on the 28th. There is a hay wagon behind a Grayrock tractor, a maize maze cut through a block of feed maize, pumpkin bread and pie with Aumere's sugar, hot cider from Milford's orchards, a weighing table with a chalk board for the day's heaviest pick, and the sun house's glass ribs visible from the halt. The pickets at Milford's closes, about a mile off, are doubled on patch days and do not come down for pie. On Kaldo's first Sunday, Tess and Osric come and Tess walks the rows, and Isolde and Sir Jabari come too, while Paxton stands at the gate, still sore about the days.

On Sunday 21 October, through Emeric's eyes, the royal family goes on Kaldo's second Sunday, and Ferra issues the king a ticket like everyone else's with the day printed on it. He does not get a closed field; he gets Kaldo's second half of the wards around him. Emeric, Ferra and Imani have a date. Ferra checks the day's count at the gate and Imani walks the aid tent, and when he tries to pull Ferra off the count she tells him work first. After noon a radio call comes from the castle; he takes it, and is back in two minutes. In the maze they make him promise to close his sense, which he never does, and he gets honestly lost. Imani walks up behind him and kisses him, the first time anybody has sneaked up on him in years, and only because he promised. Ferra finds them both by sitting at the exit with the cutting map.

Harrold brings both his wives. Amara comes in her golem, which could carry a cart of pumpkins and is not allowed to. Rowena came in on Friday at his proper letter. Each picks one, and he carries both, refuses help, and is a knight about it all afternoon. Corbin brings his mother, Sabina, at Amara's asking; she comes with her son, not her husband, and that is her choice. She picks the most correct pumpkin in the field, and Corbin carries it. She tells Harrold it will turn cold before the thirty-first. Harrold says she wrote. It is the first time she has said the weather to his face instead of in her one letter a year. Neither says more. Runa decides a mother counts as a date. Waylon and Alina come, and Alina asks Hollis how many acres it takes and what the Isle would need to grow them; Ferra says spring, at her price. Zale brings Ilenne up from Aumere. She has never seen food that grows lying in a field, and knocks on one like a door to hear it. Zale lifts the day's heaviest and goes on the board, with Runa second, who picks by nose and is right every time, which Hollis takes personally. In the picking rows, in the Sunday crowd, eighteen days after the Titles Act ended hereditary jurisdiction, Mira takes Maeve's hand. Rowena sees it and says about time. The crowd makes nothing of it except the girl at the weighing table, who asks which of them is paying. Emeric finally has the overprotective brother's glare ready and nobody to aim it at, so he aims it at Bren, out of habit. "Still?" Ward runs the day's security with Arthur and Bren, says he does not want a pumpkin, and leaves with three. Emeric thinks privately of a farm off a highway in a life before, a wagon of hay, too much cider. Ferra asks him quietly whether it is like home. Almost. Wait for the thirty-first.

Through Paxton's eyes, on the neighbours' day a Vennor woman asks where she can buy the seed, and Paxton tells her spring, at the posted price, and that the pumpkin is the easy part and the drains are the hard part. The neighbours stay quiet about Kaldo, because each of them wants it for themselves. On Sunday 28 October the count in Paxton's farm book is about twenty-one thousand four hundred through the gate over six days, no day over its cap. Paxton writes "next year: two blocks" in the farm book and phones Ferra to tell her the days worked, which he hates doing. Kaldo News prints the count the next week, and at the bottom one line saying that the princess and Mira Holt of the Crestless picked together. That is all it says.

Ilenne brings the Keepers' seed and the method of its deep steep to the royal day, as Ressar promised. Ferra enters it under the Seed Act with the Keepers' conditions, and the steep starts at the Cradle under Tess and Armand.

Chapter 83: Loud

On Tuesday 30 October, at the inspectorate's examination hall at Kestle, through Kezia's eyes, she sits the open safety examination, written papers and a mill walk-through, open to anyone, which is the point. She passes on her drawings. The examiner has seen her Stannary warning in Treloar's trial record and says nothing about it. Gethin walks his first lawful safety round the same month with the Cinderhale reeve's watch.

On Wednesday 31 October, Hollow Night, through Imani's eyes, sweet or story goes round by pumpkin light, and for the first time there is a pumpkin at nearly every door, the one each household brought from Milford, while the children carry small ones from house to house. The turnips stay on the old families' steps, Bram's still the best in the street, and at the stone for the people with no graves, where the children still leave turnips. Maren's sweets this year are pumpkin bread. Cardboard rescue golems are everywhere after the dock fire, and one child goes as a sparrow and will not answer anybody who asks it anything, and the adults who know laugh a beat late. September comes down the pear again. Emeric, at her shoulder, says quietly that it finally looks like Halloween, the Earth word, said only to her. She asks what was missing. Orange. On Monday 12 November the Stone Hearing's autumn sitting will grant nine.

On Sunday 4 November, at the castle kitchen, the six sit at their table, and through Emeric's eyes he says it aloud to them at last: the groves are the gentlest thing they have done, and the Watchers came to them, the way the Isle's guard came to the digging. Every good thing they do is loud. Saban asks whether they should stop. Emeric keeps planting, and says why once: hiding the world is what the Church did for two thousand years, and it cost every grove there was. Ward and Ferra want the curve slower, and the racks' cap stays where Ward set it in May. Nobody knows what the birds are.

Chapter 84: The Count

On Saturday 10 November, in an ironworks town in the eastern hills, through Wystan's eyes, the first widening's election is held. Wystan's case to the hills is the furnaces: they employ the hills, their suppliers feed the hills, and the hills' future is tied to them. An ironworker votes for him in a curtained booth with a clean, secret ballot, freely, and afraid for his job; the page labels him neither bought nor foolish nor free of pressure. He votes freely under unequal conditions. Wystan wins. Through Agatha Vell's eyes, the March's guaranteed seats are filled, and she wins one of them.

On Sunday 11 November, in the League's back room, through Corrick's eyes, the south road's count comes in: the League's candidate, the dyer who lost the reeveship in February, loses by under two hundred votes to a lawyer the mills backed. Corrick refused office and did not stand. A young supporter wants to call the count stolen and lean on the register clerk. Corrick asks what evidence he has. There is none. The young man says they followed the rules and the others still won, so the rules failed. Corrick says a fair rule can give them a loss; that is why it is a rule. A worker leaves before Corrick finishes, and nobody applauds. Corrick has to live with knowing that some of the people who followed him wanted victory more than a system that would also protect their enemies.

Chapter 85: The Step

On Monday 26 November the Kindling and the Returning Current's warnings go out in print to the pilot towns' clinics, before any method. On Wednesday 28 November, through Maudie's eyes as she reads the broadsheet, Wystan's motion carries in the Assembly: a two-year exemption for the eastern hills' ironworks from the hours inspectorate, legal only there, until the state inspectorate's furnace branch exists. Ironworkers wrote letters both ways and the broadsheet prints both. It does not cover Cinderhale's mill. Maudie underlines that sentence. On 30 November Keller dismisses the council's refiled suit.

On Saturday 8 December, in the League's hall in Cinderhale, Cinderhale's mill still runs sixty-hour weeks. The reeve, the old mill lord's cousin, sits by the door. The mill lord cites Wystan's exemption, and Maudie reads him the sentence she underlined. Corrick moves an immediate strike and loses; the members choose a staged approach. The king is on the back bench, because the League invited him to hear the pilot's first quarter. The district clerk leans over and tells Maudie quietly that hands are going down because the king can see them. She sees a man raise his hand halfway and lower it. She asks the king to leave. He goes.

Through Emeric's eyes, on the step, in the cold, with the door shut, he hears the motion read through it: Bryony's shifts, forty-one Silver Rand in a tin for a strike fund, three delegates, and their own lawyer, instructed to sue the crown's inspectorate over its backlog. He hears it and does not stop it. Then the mill whistle. Then no chairs moving. Then the count. He does not hear the number. He walks back to the station.

Inside, afterward, through Pim Barrow's eyes, someone asks Corrick whether he trusts the king now. He says he trusts the League enough to let it vote without him. Pim, who learned his letters at Maudie's night table, signs the roll with his name, in letters. There is no speech.

On Wednesday 28 November, in the same sitting that grants Wystan's exemption, the widened Assembly re-enacts Harrold's Year 17 order as the Prisons Act. Turnstone and Stillwater now stand under statute, the Final Review is written into law, and there are still no life sentences. Nobody asks Emeric, and he doesn't speak.

Where the Story Stands After Chapter 85

The Titles Act is law. Hereditary jurisdiction has ended, civil courts are passing district by district to elected ones, and tolls are being bought out over twenty years. The March has its guaranteed seats, Agatha Vell among its members, and a referendum on its civil franchises next spring. The first widened Assembly is seated: Wystan for the eastern hills, now holding a two-year exemption for their ironworks, and a mills' lawyer for the south road. The hereditary council's suits are dismissed and it has stopped meeting. The beast line runs to October Year 22, with one pen and its pup kept below the second stage. The king has said in public that the crown should end when the laws can carry the country, and not before.

The League has joined the pilot towns on its own written terms, without loyalty. It voted for a staged approach at the mill, a strike fund of forty-one Silver Rand, three delegates and its own lawyer suing the crown's inspectorate, and it voted without the king in the room after Maudie asked him to leave. Corrick is out, holds no office, has a docket number and an adjourned appeal, and has told his own supporters that a fair rule can give them a loss. Kezia is an examined safety inspector, Gethin walks lawful safety rounds, Nan Wicks collected her forty-two silver without meeting any of them, and Pim has signed his name in letters. Lowell Tilley and the Fairhythe chandler are at Turnstone for one blank seed, and its buyer was never found.

The bench arm closed on a thought, read and never written. The groves stand at about five hundred trees, the first pumpkin block fed every household in four states, and Maeve and Mira are known in public. Ferra's margin now holds nineteen Watcher sightings rising with her air column, and the king has said aloud that every good thing they do is loud, and keeps planting. The young are unchanged, the mother unseen, the count at day 806 with five sales, and the switch holds.

This batch completes the turn the whole of Book 3 has been building toward: from a king who acts and then explains, to one who applies, waits outside, and leaves when he is asked. The institutions he built now give results he dislikes and decline to need him, and the people he once ruled are learning to use them, including against him.
