Book 2, Chapter 31: Named

Ilenne dressed for the court at the first bell of the morning on the Thursday, in her mother's house, in the room she'd slept in as a girl, and the whole time she was doing it she knew exactly where his ship was.

Up. Five hundred feet up through the black water, and a mile across it, and through the gate in the breakwater, lying against the gate steps with its floods dark and its crew keeping to the hold. She couldn't feel it from here. Nobody could feel anything from here; the Breath was too near, and it filled the water with its slow deep note until every other note in the city was a voice in a crowd. She knew where it was the way you know where a door is in a dark room. She'd had no word from it in thirteen days. She'd asked for none. She'd told Linnet Shaw, on the gate steps on the night of the fire, with his blood still on her hands and the sea still running out of her hair, that if anything came down the stages from that ship for her she'd send it back unread; and nothing had come, and she'd hated every hour of the nothing, and it had been right.

She put on the grey coat. Not the court colours; the plain long grey of a woman of the house who isn't speaking for it, close at the throat, oiled so that it lay flat in the water and didn't lift. She'd chosen it on the Tuesday. Her mother had seen it laid out and said nothing, which was how her mother said yes.

On the shelf by the door, in its oilcloth, lay Ferra's sheet.

She didn't need it. She'd learned it on the Tuesday and the Wednesday, sitting on this floor with the lamp stone turned low, line by line, as she'd once learned the stones: the attempt, the piece, who held it, the word. She'd said it over aloud until she could say it with her eyes shut and her colour held, and then said it over again with her colour given, all of it, and found that was much harder. She could have left it on the shelf.

She picked it up and held it flat against her chest in both hands.

Thalune was at the door.

She was always at the door. She'd been drawn by lot from the great houses on the night of the binding to watch a bound heir's door for the heir's first season and say to the court what she saw, and she'd done it: on the stair, at the bell, in the open sea outside the water door with her tail moving slowly to hold her place. Ilenne knew what she'd said. She'd said it in the court on the Saturday after the fire, standing, in a voice with no more in it than a slate has: that the bell had broken in the night, and the husband had been in it, and the heir had been carried up out of it by something from the deep. Every house had heard it by nightfall.

Thalune looked at the oilcloth against Ilenne's chest and at the grey coat, and her colour stayed the colour of the water, and she fell in a pace behind.



The hall was full by the second bell.

She'd been coming into it since she could walk. She'd sat on the floor at the back of it with the other children of the deep houses on feast days and watched the lords go to their benches in their long dark coats with their hair lifting round their heads, and the stones at their throats catching the light, and every one of them walking, slowly, pressed down, because nobody swam in a king's hall. She'd thought, at six, that it was the most beautiful room in the world, and she still thought so.

The benches went up both sides, tier on tier, to the ribs. Every place was taken and people stood behind the top tier against the glass. The great houses on the right, the deep ones nearest the seat. The old families of the shallows on the left, further back, where they'd always been put. The Keepers against the long left wall in their plain grey, apart from the benches, standing, with Ressar at their front. Taurre on his stool below the step with his slate across his knees and two of the watch behind him.

The seat was on its step of black rock at the end. Her mother was on it.

Yssaure sat as she'd sat since Ilenne's father died: straight, still, her hands on the arms of the seat, her colour so level that people who didn't know her thought she had none. Ilenne knew her. She could see the effort at the corners of her mother's mouth. A regent for a dead husband, with the court's patience going out like a tide and every lord on those benches counting the days to the binding that would put a man on that seat. Ilenne had run from that binding into the thin water and nearly died there. Her mother had stayed and held the seat.

The house of the Rim had its benches on the right, the third tier, near the seat. Ushar's place at their front was empty. Sevrin sat beside it, narrow-faced and young in the house's coat, with his hands folded, as he'd sat all through the sittings on the fire, speaking for a man the watch had in a closed bell under its house and told nothing. Ilenne didn't look at him. She'd decided on the Tuesday not to look at him once, all day, and she didn't.

The guests stood where guests stood, on the left of the floor below the seat, a little back.

The land king was in his coat. Heavy and dark, the one the Rim called the king's coat, over the grey suit with its wide-visored helm, no sword, his hands at his sides; standing as he'd stood every day of the sittings, as the asked stood, though nobody had asked him anything today. Ferra beside him in her own suit and helm, and Imani on his other side in hers, both of them braced against the water the way the land eaters braced, so that they stood on the polished floor as steadily as anyone born to it. The old Keeper of the land, Armand, a pace behind them with his hands folded under his coat.

Emeric caught her eye through his glass. He didn't do anything with his face. She was grateful.

She went to the floor and stood below the seat, alone, and looked up at her mother, as the asked looked up.



Ressar opened it.

He came off the Keepers' wall with the stone held flat against his chest in both hands, as he always held a stone, as if somebody might try to take it, and walked out onto the floor until he was level with her, a few yards off. He didn't look at her. She'd never once in all the years of her childhood looked at his face; she'd watched the stones. She looked at it now. He was old. He'd been old when she was six. His colour was the grey of the Keepers' coats and it didn't move.

"The court went into recess for the stones," he said, "at the close of the sitting on the fire, so that the Keepers might read an older line. The line has been read."

His voice was flat and dry and carried to the top of the hall without his raising it. It had been a woman's voice once, the old ones said, a singer's; it had never sung since the change, and he said everything now the way he said this, laid down level, word by word, like stones on a wall.

"The line is cut on the ninth stone of the third wall," said Ressar. "It is old. It is older than the First City's fall. It says: a custom is named by one it binds." He turned, slowly, to the Keepers against the long wall. "I read it so. Do the Keepers read it so?"

And the Keepers showed.

She'd never seen it done. She'd heard of it. Seven old men and women in grey against a wall, and each of them in turn let a colour come up at the throat, slowly, so that the whole hall could watch it come: not warm, not slate, a pale clear even blue-grey, the colour of water over sand, that meant I have read it and it says this. One. Two. The old woman at the end, who'd kept the children quiet at the stones when Ilenne was small, in her turn. All seven. And Ressar's own, which came up last of all, and held.

"The Keepers read it so," said Ressar. "It is the court's reading from this sitting. Any custom of the court that binds a person, and has no name, may be named in this hall by one it binds. A thing named may be judged."

He went back to the wall. Taurre wrote.

Her mother spoke from the seat. The old words, the ones the regent was allowed, that put a question to the floor without asking anything: whether any here bound by a custom without a name would name it.

Ilenne said, "I am."



She said it all. In the water, with her mouth, with her colour given, so that every word went out of her wet and was hers for ever.

She said the custom first, because it had to be said before anything could be laid against it. That a binding made at a door, in the water, before witnesses, was once and until one of the two died. That the one who lived could be bound again. That this was a good custom and an old one, and kept a house from being bound twice to the same breath.

And then she said the rest of it.

That in some houses it had been taken to mean a thing that was never cut on any stone. That if a bound heir's husband was a man a house didn't want beside her, there was a road round him that the custom left open. That the one who lived could be bound again. That a widow was free.

"And so," said Ilenne, in her own voice, in her own tongue, level, to the whole court of Aumere, "a house that wants a bound heir for its own son, and finds her bound to another, kills the husband. To make her a widow. To make her free. To bind her again to the one it wanted." She held her colour exactly where she wanted it, a plain clear grey with nothing hidden in it. "That is the custom. Widow-making. It has never been said in this hall. I say it."

The hall made no sound. Merfolk didn't, much, in a hall; there was no air to sigh with. But she felt it go through the water like a cold current in the open sea: a hundred people changing colour at once, and holding it, and trying not to.

Ressar's colour didn't move.

On the second tier of the right side a deep lord she knew by his coat, the heavy one who'd asked the land king for a wet word on the first day, went slate from the throat down, all at once, and then, slowly, pale.

She laid out the attempts.

In Ferra's order. She'd thought, on the Tuesday, about putting them in another, the worst first, or the one with the most proof; and she'd known before she finished thinking it that she wouldn't, because Ferra's order was the order they'd happened in, and anything else was an argument. She said each one as it was on the sheet. The attempt; the piece; who held it; and the word.

In her own tongue they were short words. Kaldo's five, in the Rim's speech. Seen. Measured. Remembered. Guessed. Blank. She'd been afraid on the Wednesday that they'd sound small in that hall, little dry land words for counting nails. They didn't. Said wet, each one meant exactly what it said for ever, and there was nothing smaller or bigger about any of them than that.

The sliver under the back strap of her husband's harness, in her mother's house, in the bell. The drain stone, with the mark of the old families' yard on its face. Seen. The cutter of the shallows who sold blanks with that mark to a furnace steward of the Rim, Maudrec, for iron. Remembered, by the cutter, to the watch. The iron itself, three bars of the land's own, paid by the land to a pilot of the Rim for passage, and gone from that pilot to Maudrec to the cutter. Measured, by the land's weights. The cut on the stone, that the cutter said wasn't his, set beside the point of the drain-stone bident that went into her back on the land's south island years ago, when she ran, by the land's engineer at home, over the set, with the watch on the line. Seen. Same hand.

The hand that put the sliver under the strap. Blank.

The fish in her husband's dish. The taster of the house of the Rim, who ate it. Seen.

The knife in the market, for hire, by a man nobody on the stairs knew. Seen. Who hired him. Blank.

The shot from the roof above the kiln yard. The land's gun, declared lost in the watch's own log, raised by a salvager of the Rim, carried by night to Maudrec's yard, bought for the house. Remembered, by the salvager and the carrier. Seen, on the salvager's tally. The man who fired it, a mage of the Rim, dead on the kelp racks with it in his hands. Seen.

The fire stones in the big kiln yard laid to go one into the next. Seen. The charges that broke the bell, cast in the house's own furnace and written down as net weights, out of a cracked mould. Seen, and measured. The shutter at the foot of the yard stair. Guessed, by the land engineer who drew it before it fell, and left as guessed because it was what he knew when he drew it. The bell broken with the husband in it. Remembered, by the court's own watcher at the door. The house's men who came out of it alive. Remembered, by their own account. The zone logged at the watch house a quarter of an hour before the land's ship could be heard. Measured.

Maudrec's word, when the watch held him, whose orders he'd taken. Remembered.

She didn't say the name. She didn't need to. Everyone in the hall had known for twelve days whose bell was under the watch house.

When she'd finished she stood with Ferra's sheet against her chest in its oilcloth and her colour held, and the hall was so quiet that she could hear Taurre's stylus.



Taurre stood up from his stool and walked out onto the floor beside her, and confirmed it.

Piece by piece. That was what the watch's seal was for, and he did it without hurrying, from his slate, in his slow level voice, his colour held to a plain record's grey. The sliver: in the watch's keeping, sealed, since the day it was cut out of the harness. The cuff off the carrier's wrist: sealed. The three bars, in their cloth: sealed. The stone from the land's south island, the bident's point, kept by the land's engineer at home in a box with two locks and laid against the sliver's drawing there, over the set, with a watch officer on the line: the match heard by the watch, and the engineer's drawing of it received. The dishes: sealed. The gun, with its mark and its eight rounds in their box: in the watch's keeping since the morning on the kelp racks. The casings from the bell: raised by the watch's own divers, and the mould's vein read before a watch officer. The salvager's tally, the carrier's account, the cutter's, Maudrec's: on the watch's slates, in the words they were given.

"Each piece," said Taurre, "is as the queen's daughter says it is. The watch's seal is on each."

He went back to his stool.

And nobody asked her whether her husband was dead.

She'd known nobody would. She'd stood in front of the shelf in her room on the Tuesday night and thought it through, over and over, until she was sure: that in a court where everything said wet was said for ever, there was one question no lord would put to a woman whose husband had been carried out of a broken bell, because it was the cruellest question a court could ask, and every lord in the hall had a daughter or a sister or a mother. And she'd held her colour all morning so that nobody would need to. Plain grey. Not a widow's dark. Not anything.

The lean old lord on the second tier who always asked, who'd asked the land king about his brother, sat with his hands on his knees and looked at her a long time, and didn't stand up.



Sevrin stood up after the noon bell, and ran the defence a house would run.

She'd expected it. Ferra had expected it; Ferra had said, on the Monday night in the hard room, that a house as old as that one didn't have nothing to say, and that the first thing it would say would be the steward. And it was.

He spoke well. He was young and narrow and fluent, and he'd sat in his cousin's empty place for twelve days and learned the hall. He didn't deny a single piece. He stood below the seat beside her, at the proper distance, with his colour a steady sober dark that meant grief, and said that the house of the Rim had been shamed by a servant. That Maudrec had been a steward of its furnaces for most of his life, and since the spring a bitter and a sick one, with a private hatred of the land and of the husband the land had brought; that he'd done all of this in the house's name and with the house's iron and its yard, and the house would answer for every piece of it. That it would pay. He named a sum. From its own holdings. To the old families of the shallows, for their burned furnace hands, every one of whom the house would carry until they could work again.

And then he turned his head, a little, toward the left side of the hall, where the old families sat, and let his colour go warm for them.

"And let the court remember," said Sevrin, "what crossed the markers on the night of the fire. The thing that sank the First City. In our water, at the ring. Whoever laid the stones in that yard, the land brought that." He held the warmth for them a moment longer. "The shallows and the Rim have one enemy on that night. It isn't each other."

He sat down.

It nearly worked. She watched it nearly work. The old families' head, on the left, the old man who'd stood at the floor on the Saturday after the fire with the boy at his shoulder and said the stone came in, held his colour all through the afternoon's first bell, level, a thinking grey; and two of the women behind him had gone warm when Sevrin named the sum, and the young men behind them had gone dark when he named the stone, and stayed dark. The shallows had been put at the back of that hall for longer than the stones remembered. They'd been offered money and an enemy in one breath, by a great house, in front of everyone, and a great house had never offered them either.

Dessaun, standing behind the old man, didn't move at all.

Her mother, on the seat, asked whether the queen's daughter would answer.

"Yes," said Ilenne.

She didn't make a speech. Ferra had told her not to; Ferra had said that the sheet answered the steward, and that if she argued it the court would hear an argument and if she only read it the court would hear the sheet. She turned to the old families' benches and gave them one line of it again, the one Sevrin had stepped round.

"The cut on the stone in my husband's harness," she said, "is the cut on the bident that went into my back on the land's south island. The land's own engineer laid them side by side, with the watch on the line. Same hand. Years apart." She let her colour come up, a little, cold and clear. "Maudrec's year of madness began this spring. That bident was thrown at me before the land had a fleet. Before anybody on the Rim had heard of my husband. A steward's grudge doesn't send men with drain stones to the thin water after a runaway heir. A house does."

The old man's colour moved. Very slightly. A darkening at the throat, there and gone.

"And the taster," said Ilenne. "Ask her who sent her."

Her mother asked.

Sarrou came out from the back of the hall where the watch had brought her, walking slowly, on her legs, because she was still weak and because in the hall everyone walked. She was thin. Her colour was the pale amber Ilenne had seen in the market once, years ago, behind the house's stall, a servant's colour, that a great house's servants wore like a livery. She came to the floor and stood, as the asked stood, and looked up at the seat.

She held her colour. Ilenne watched her hold it. It cost her. Everybody in the hall could see what it cost; the amber shook at the edges and she held it anyway, steady at the centre, while she spoke.

She said she'd been the house's taster since she was a child. That a taster was sent from the house's own hall, by the house's table, as every taster was, to eat from every dish before the guest's people, so that nobody could say afterwards that the dish hadn't been tasted. That she'd been sent down to the land's guests on the morning of the fish by the house's table, and told which dish was the husband's. That she'd known what was in it. That she'd eaten it, because she'd been sent to.

"A steward of furnaces doesn't send a taster," said Sarrou. "The table sends. The house sends." Her colour shook and held. "The house sent me."

She stood a moment longer. Then she turned, and walked back to the watch at the end of the hall, slowly.

The old families' head stood up.



The court judged what it could now name.

Her mother put it. The old words for a finding, the ones only the seat could say, laid out by Ressar's office in the morning so that the question was exactly the question: that the killing of a bound person to free the one who lives, which the queen's daughter has named in this hall, is a crime against the binding and against the court; and that the attempts the queen's daughter has laid out, under the watch's seal, are the house of the Rim's.

And the houses showed.

The old families first. She'd known they'd have to be first, if they came at all, and that if they came it would be for their own reasons and not hers. They came for two. Their furnace hands, burned on that night in a yard they'd been made to work, carried off a stair by land knights out of a fire the house had laid. And the drain stones, cut with their own yard's mark on them, so that whoever found the sliver would find the shallows. The house had tried to kill her husband and hang it on them. The old man's colour came up warm, slowly, a deep red-gold like the inside of a furnace door, and he held it up where the whole hall could see; and behind him Dessaun, who'd stood at her door on the night of the binding and done his slate alone three weeks ago, let his own come up the same. The whole left side came up with them, though only the old man's colour was the shallows' voice.

Two of the deep houses followed. She saw the first one go and couldn't believe it, and then the second, the heavy lord's house, his colour going from pale to a slow reluctant warm as if he were being made to drink something, and holding.

The Keepers' voice, for. Ressar's colour, warm, once, against the grey wall, and then back to grey.

The Rim's house against. Sevrin's colour went slate from the throat down, and every cousin on the benches beside him went slate with him. Two more of the deep houses went slate with them, the two whose sons had been spoken of for her before the Rim's was.

One house held its colour. A plain level nothing. No voice.

Her mother counted. She counted as she'd been counting all Ilenne's life, without moving her head, without moving anything but her eyes. Then she said the words that made a finding.

It carried.



And then her mother put the other question.

Ilenne had known it would be today. Not tomorrow; not at the sitting on the person. Today, the same sitting, because her mother had decided it would be and had told her so on the Wednesday night in three words, in the doorway of the room, and gone: I'll put it. The court had given the regent until the binding. The binding was made. A regency was a bridge, the lords said, and bridges ended; and there was a husband, or there wasn't, and either way there was a question that had been waiting since the old king's death to be asked in that hall.

Yssaure asked it. Who would sit the seat.

Ilenne answered it wet.

"You can have my husband," she said. "Or the change I've refused twice. Or me."

She said it in the order Ferra had laid it out on the table in the hard room, three places on the wood. She said it level, with her colour plain. She didn't add anything. There wasn't anything to add; it was all their own custom, laid out by their own law, the only three roads the stones gave to that seat, and she'd only said them aloud.

And the hall heard a widow.

She felt it hear one. It went through the water like the cold current had in the morning. You can have my husband. A woman whose husband had been carried out of a broken bell by something from the deep, standing in grey in front of the court that had let it happen, offering them the one thing she didn't have. They heard the bitterness in it. They heard a widow throwing it in their faces. She saw it in their colours, all down the right side: a darkening, a turning away, and on some of them, on the old women at the back, something that was very like shame.

She meant every word.

That was what none of them knew. Every word of it was true, said wet, for ever. They could have her husband. He was alive on a ship at the gate. He was the old king of the land's son, and the new king's brother, with a whole kingdom behind him and a fleet at the Grey Mark, and the law gave him the seat if they wanted him on it. They could have the change, which she'd refused once already and would refuse again here. Or they could have her. She'd laid all three on the floor of the hall and she'd keep to whichever they picked, for the rest of her life, because she'd said it in the water.

They didn't want a land man on that seat. They couldn't make her take the change. There was one road left.

The old families showed first again, warm. Then the deep houses, more of them this time, one after another down the right side, one of the two that had gone slate for the finding among them. The Keepers. Then, last, very slowly, the heavy lord's, warm and sullen. The Rim's house, slate. One deep house held, as it had before.

Her mother counted.

Her mother looked down at her from the seat for a moment, without moving.

Then Yssaure stood up.

She took her hands off the arms of the seat and stood, and walked down off the step of black rock to the floor, and stood on it beside her daughter, level, where the asked stood. It was how a regency ended. Ilenne had read it on the stones as a child and never seen it, and never thought she would. The regent stepped down before the court, and stood on the floor, and the seat was empty.

Her mother didn't look at her. She looked straight ahead at the empty seat with her colour held so level it might have been carved.

Ilenne walked up the step and sat down.



The heavy lord stood up before the hall had finished showing, and asked what the court was given for it.

He asked it courteously. He was entitled to ask; a house that showed for a thing it didn't like had the right to ask its price, and he'd made himself drink it and wanted to know what it had cost. Ilenne had known that too. She'd sat with Ferra in the hard room on the Monday night until the lamp was low, and Ferra had asked her, at the end, what she'd give the great houses when they asked, because they'd ask.

"Review," said Ilenne, from the seat. "Of any heir's binding to any house. From this sitting. Before it is made, the great houses see it, and may speak against it in this hall." She let that settle. "Review. Not a veto. And over nothing else the seat does. Only the binding of heirs, because that is where the killing came from."

The heavy lord stood a moment. Then he inclined his head, which was as near to warm as he was going to go, and sat.

And Ressar came off the wall again.

He walked out onto the floor, below her now, and stood, and spoke to the whole hall in his flat dry voice, and she understood as he began why he'd come, and that he was going to say it so that nobody could say afterwards that it hadn't been said.

"The court's reading of the ninth stone," said Ressar, "is general. It is not for one heir. Any heir of any house, bound by any custom of this court that has no name, may stand in this hall and name it, and it may be judged." He turned his head, slowly, and looked up at her on the seat. "Any heir. Of any house. Including the house that sits there."

She held his eyes from the seat, and he looked back.

A thing she'd done today, alone, in front of everyone, any girl of any house could do in twenty years to whoever sat this seat, and might, and might do it to her. She'd seen that on the Tuesday, learning the sheet, and done it anyway. She let him see that she knew.

His colour moved, for her: a warm, very faint, once, at the throat.

Then he went back to the wall.



The land king hadn't said a word all day.

She looked at him once from the seat when the court had begun to go down its shafts, the deep lords first, the old families after, the Keepers last with Ressar's stone between them. He was still on the floor on the left, where guests stood, in the king's coat, with his wives on either side of him in their suits and the old Keeper behind. He hadn't moved since the morning. He hadn't lifted his slate or taken his helm off or so much as turned his head when the house named the stone. He'd stood there all day like a post in a tide, and let it go past him.

Ferra, beside him, was looking up at her.

Ilenne looked back. Ferra's face behind her glass did nothing at all. Ferra's order had been in Ilenne's mouth all morning, and Ferra's three places on the wood all afternoon, and not one lord in that hall would ever know that any of it had been a land woman's at a table in a hard room, and that was how Ferra had wanted it. Ilenne understood that she'd been given a thing that couldn't be thanked for in public, and that the land woman would be offended if she tried.

She let her colour go warm, once, at the throat. For Ferra. Only that.

Ferra inclined her head behind her glass, very slightly, and looked away.



She went up to the gate alone that night, for the first time since the fire.

The pilots weren't on them at that hour. She didn't need a pilot. She'd been going up and down the stages since she could swim, and she went up through the black water in the dark with her eyes open and the city's glow going down under her into the deep, bell by bell, the lamp stones on the line going past one every twenty yards; and nobody stopped her, because nobody stopped a queen. She hadn't thought of that until she was halfway up. Nobody would ever stop her again in this water. She didn't know yet whether she liked it.

She came up into the head and through the cold chamber and out through the Rim's streets in the dark on her legs, and along the breakwater to the gate, and down the gate steps to where his ship lay with her floods out and her crew below. Linnet Shaw was sitting on a coil of rope at the top of the gangway with a shuttered lamp. She stood up when she saw who it was. Then she sat down again, very carefully, as if she'd decided that was the safest thing, and looked at the sea.

Ilenne went up the gangway.

He was on deck. Under the awning aft, in the dark, on a folded sail with his back against the deckhouse and a blanket over his knees and his side strapped under his shirt, where nobody on the breakwater could see him. He'd heard her on the gangway. He'd known her step; she saw that he had, in the way he hadn't moved.

She sat down beside him on the sail. Not touching. A hand's width off.

The sea lapped at the steps below them. Somewhere along the breakwater a watch boat's lamp went by and was gone.

"I'm queen," said Ilenne.

He didn't say anything for a while. She felt him not say it. Then he turned his head and looked at her in the dark, and she couldn't see his face, and didn't need to.

"I've been told worse things on this deck," said Zale.

She laughed. She hadn't meant to. It came out of her short and rough and surprised.

They sat on the folded sail in the dark with a hand's width between them, and the sea going up and down the gate steps, and neither of them said the other thing.
