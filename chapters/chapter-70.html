Chapter 70 - A Traveling Cleric

The cleric came through Kaldo on the road to somewhere else, which was how nearly everyone of his order came through anywhere, the church being a thing of circuits and visitations rather than of place, its men forever passing between the seats of its power carrying its rites and its accounts and its quiet observations from one hall to the next. He was not important. Emeric, had he marked the man at all, would have marked him as a low functionary of the delving church, one of the many who moved along the roads administering the rites the church administered and reporting, dutifully, upward, on the state of the districts they passed through, because the church survived on information the way it survived on tithe, and a passing cleric was, among his other functions, an eye. This one had eyes that worked. That was the whole of the trouble, and the trouble was so small, that season, that no one in the barony felt it as trouble at all.

He heard a thing in Kaldo that did not sit right with what he knew, and what he knew was the rite, because the rite was his trade. The delving church had, at the center of its practice, a working it performed on certain of the faithful, an old and guarded thing, and one of the plain facts every cleric of his rank carried about that working was who it killed. The rite had a threshold. Bodies of a certain constitution survived it and were counted blessed, and bodies of another constitution did not survive it and were counted, in the church's cold theology, as having been found wanting, and every cleric knew the rough shape of which was which the way a physician knows which fevers take the old, from long grim tally. It was settled knowledge. It was, to a man of his order, simply how the world and the working were made.

And in Kaldo he heard, in the ordinary talk of the ordinary roads, that the factory hands were changed. He did not hear it as heresy, because no one said it as heresy, no one in Kaldo had the least idea it touched the church at all. He heard it as men speak of a district's oddities, that the folk who worked the fine crafts down at the prince's works were a hardy lot, that they took sickness less than they used to, that a man who went into the ink-rooms or the fine-line shops seemed to come out of a year of it somehow sounder than he went in, the way a district invents small proud legends about itself. To the men who said it, it was a boast about clean water and good wages and the physician's careful protocols, all of which was, as far as it went, true, and none of which was the thing the cleric heard. What the cleric heard, against the settled tally in his head, was a description of a change in the constitution of ordinary laboring men, and he heard it the way a man hears a wrong note in a tune he has known all his life, not knowing yet what the note means but knowing, with a trained certainty, that it does not belong.

He did not investigate. That was not his office and not his instinct, and there was nothing in Kaldo to investigate that would have shown him anything, the change being real and invisible and locked inside bodies no passing cleric had the means or the cause to read. He did the one thing his order trained him to do with a note that did not belong, which was to write it down. He filed a report, as clerics filed reports, a line in a visitation account among a hundred other lines about tithe and attendance and the condition of the roads, and the line said, in the flat administrative language of such things, that the district of Kaldo bore watching on a small point, that the laboring population there was said to be altered in its health in a manner the writer could not account for, and that the seat might wish to note it. Then he moved on, to the somewhere else his circuit was taking him, and forgot it more or less entirely, because a cleric who filed a line in every district he passed did not carry the lines with him, he set them down and let the seat decide which mattered, and to him this one was no larger than the state of the roads.

The line went up the church's slow circuits toward the seat, and would arrive, and would sit in an account with a hundred others, and would very likely be read by no one who understood it for a long time, because the man who could have connected an altered laboring population to the rite's own threshold was not the man who logged visitation accounts, and the church, like every large old body, kept its knowing in separate rooms that did not talk to one another as often as its enemies feared. But it was written now. That was the whole of what the chapter of that season did, in the deep structure of the thing Emeric could not see. Before the cleric passed, the change in the ink-workers was a fact known to no one and suspected by one idle tower mage who had filed it nowhere. After the cleric passed, it was a line in the church's own records, a data point lodged in the one institution in the world that had both a settled theory the change contradicted and a settled machinery for acting on contradictions of its theory. The fuse had not been lit by the cleric. The fuse had been burning in the workers' bodies for a year. The cleric had merely carried the first spark of it into the powder room, and set it down, and walked away, not knowing there was powder, not knowing he carried a spark, thinking only that he had done his small duty and noted an oddity for men wiser than himself to weigh.

Emeric registered none of it. He had, if the cleric crossed his notice at all, the vague awareness a busy lord has of a church functionary passing through, a rite performed for those who wanted it, a meal given, a bed, a departure. His apparatus, so fine at catching a barrel meant for his road, was blind to this entirely, because the apparatus was built to catch men who meant him harm and the cleric meant him none, had no plot, carried no barrel, wished the prince nothing but the ordinary blessings of his order, and had done, in perfect innocence, the single most dangerous thing anyone had done to Kaldo all year. The danger wore the face of a good outcome. The cleric had heard men praise their own health and had reported it upward as an oddity, and the praise was true and the health was real and the reporting of it was, to every human eye in Kaldo including the prince's, a thing of no weight at all, a passing man noting a district's small pride. Emeric could not see the danger because there was, in the plain human sense, no danger in the room. There was only a healthy population, a passing cleric, and a line written in a book he would never read, in a hand he would never know, filed in a seat that had, sleeping in its old records, exactly the theory that would one day wake and call his greatest kindness a crime.
