Book 2, Batch 2: Terms

Chapters 10–19 | About 7 May – 29 May, Year 17

The fleet has reached Aumere and come in as guests. Zale and Ilenne were bound on the lowest steps of the door, before the captain of the twelve and Taurre of the caldera watch, and in the same hour Ushar of the Rim's house came up beneath Zale at the queen's own gate and named him a man whose word is water. Zale answered with a guest's perfect bow, Emeric stood silent in the water in his king's coat, and the queen turned up her hand: come in, my house, my word. The K Amara, with the stone she carries, waits beyond the outer markers on the queen's order. The other hulls stand off outside the ring.

This batch makes the fleet live under the terms that came with that invitation. Aumere has five separate powers. The queen invites, receives and gives port orders. The caldera watch holds safety, closures and the hazard log. The Keepers hold the stones, the grove and the songs. The Rim's house holds the certifying bench, the pilots and the furnaces. The court judges, but it cannot judge what it will not name. Every chapter asks what Kaldo is allowed to do rather than what it is able to do, and the answer keeps shrinking. Emeric keeps accepting it, because the alternative is becoming the thing the merfolk's stones remember.

Against them, the Rim's house takes two roads at once: the public road through the court, and the quiet road through the water. The batch turns when the court finds that the binding was made but refuses to say whether it can be ended, and from there the house's only remaining route runs underwater.

Chapter 10: Held Messages

On the evening of the door, Imani is aboard Zale's ship to check Ilenne before she goes Below, and the captain of the twelve is aboard to escort the couple down. Linnet Shaw reads the day's traffic aloud on deck at the evening set, a habit from the run west. Saban's reply to the wedding has arrived, and he addressed it to the whole ship rather than to Zale, his idea of a joke for the crew who were with him on South Sentinel: "I was a yard off. Her neck went blue." Zale, Imani, Linnet and the crew understand it at once. The captain does not, until Ilenne, after a year of translating everything, puts it into her own language without thinking. Now the captain has it from a land witness that her colour went blue for Zale on the first day. A sham marriage could be waited out. A love match has to be ended. His colour flickers before he holds it still, and he looks at Zale differently afterward. Zale laughs, assuming the joke is about him being slow.

Ferra fills in the last blank line in her book, the name the queen sent in the warning colour that nobody translated. She does not ask Ilenne. She works it out from the door, writes in the Rim's house herself, and shows Ilenne the page. Ilenne nods once.

The guests go down into the hard rooms beneath the Rim. At the water door the Keeper logs the revolvers on Ferra's and Imani's belts and asks what they are for. Imani says, "For when the lights go out." The Keeper takes it as a proverb and approves. They descend a stair cut in the rock, pass through a water door into a bell, and then into a hard dome: ribs of held glass in small spans, the pumps that emptied it over a full day still ticking, a Keeper and a pilot beside them, and a Keeper's word for implosion that he refuses to say indoors. Emeric listens to the ribs the way he listens to a hull and does not say what he hears. Imani sees him do it.

At the gate Imani checks Ilenne as her doctor. Home water has done in a day what a year on South Sentinel could not: a pearl sheen, full gills, colour held so still that Imani has to ask her to stop holding it so she can read it. Imani marks a notch on her card that has never been used before: home. Ilenne tells her what the insult demands of Zale. He must answer it before witnesses within the season, or the name becomes his.

Imani walks the couple down to the first stage. Her field set thins on the stair and dies at the first wet shaft, because radio does not pass through seawater. She climbs back a step at a time until it answers again and marks the place, then writes on her card: Below, nothing past the head of the stages. It reads as a nurse being thorough.

Below, in the queen's house, the couple's bell hangs on its anchor chain with two pallets laid out by someone who knew exactly what that said. Outside the water door waits Thalune, the court's watcher. By custom the court posts one at a bound heir's door for her first season, drawn by lot from the great houses and reporting to the court, not to her own house. Through the water sheet she sees colour well and hears almost nothing. Before they sleep, Ilenne gives Zale her house's guard charm, a sliver of shard on a cord, the custom for a bound husband's first season. It catches a fast thing by its speed. She says it is for the knife you do not see. He puts it on under his shirt and says he has never owned jewellery. Thalune sees it given, and by the next sitting so does every hall. Zale takes the floor without being asked. Ilenne lies awake and does not hold her colour, because there is nobody to hold it for.

In the morning Zale comes up the stages after a bad night on a glass floor, and Ferra and Imani are merciless at breakfast. Ferra asks whether the floor was level. Imani offers to prescribe a cushion. Emeric eats. He has spent the early hours steaming the king's coat dry with his own fire, a hand's heat at a time so the wool will not scorch, and the hard room is fogged; the Keeper is unhappy about water in his air. Ferra tells Emeric how many hours of work are in the back of that coat. Zale announces he will come up every dusk for the post, because his crew are alone at the gate and he is their captain. Imani notes that it will be the one time each day he is within reach.

Chapter 11: Say It in the Water

On 9 May the queen sends Ressar, Keeper of the Stones, to teach the land man the forms. Ressar taught Ilenne as a child, and she never once looked at his face. Born a daughter of a minor house, he has lived as a man for forty years, and the court calls him he. His changed voice is the price: as a girl he sang the stones' songs, and now he cannot. He arrives in the hard rooms with a teaching stone and no patience for anyone who has not done the reading.

He gives Zale the form plainly. The sideways sign is not a word, which is why the custom lets the named man answer it. Within the season, the named man returns to the place, before the same witnesses, and calls the namer to say it in words, in the water. If the namer refuses, the witnesses record it and the sign is returned: void against the named man, forbidden to the namer before witnesses again, and the disgrace of an unmade charge falls on the one who made it. If the namer does say it in the water, the charge becomes a wet word, and he must prove the named man's word broken before the court within the season or carry the name himself forever. Nobody sane does that about a vow made the week before. The stones carry a saying for it, say it wet, show it wet, and Ressar can name one old case of a man who did, and carried the name to his own grave.

Zale asks Ilenne first, since it is her door and her mother's name. She says yes in her colour before she says it in words. She does not go down the steps with him; this one is his. At the turn of the tide, before the captain and Taurre, with Ressar there to state the form, Zale calls Ushar in his rough sailor's version of her language. He does not say "I will." He asks a question.

Ushar comes level this time. He cannot come up under twice in front of the watch. He also does not say it. He refuses in the way that saves the most face: he will not speak in the water to a man who cannot breathe it. The captain and Taurre certify that he was called and did not speak, Ressar states that the sign is returned, and Taurre records it. The sign is void against Zale, Ushar can never make it at him again before witnesses, and the steps watch the disgrace pass to Ushar. But the line he chose stays with Zale, because it is true. Emeric watches from the top step and does nothing, and it is the hardest thing in the chapter for him.

That afternoon the Rim's house goes to the court. Notice is laid in the dry hall that the house holds the binding at the door was never made, on three grounds: a land man cannot be bound, the witnesses were the queen's, and a binding at the door, outside the city, does not count.

Afterward Zale and Ilenne sit on the steps, his legs in the water and hers not. She teaches him the colour sign for "that was well done," and he gets it wrong into something rude. Maeve, who came down to watch, learns the rude one first. Armand asks Ressar how the teaching stones are read. Ressar asks whether he has done the reading. Armand has, all of it, in the wrong language. Ressar goes back down without answering, which Armand takes as a beginning. At dusk Zale goes up the stages for the post, as he said he would.

Chapter 12: The Dry Hall

On 11 and 12 May the court comes up to receive the guests. The full court sits at the floor, Below, near the Breath, where everyone walks and swimming in the queen's hall is an insult. For strangers it meets in a dry hall, a hard dome under the Rim pumped out for the purpose, and some of the deep lords make sure the strangers know what that cost them. Armand watches. The reason for the dry hall matters: a word said in the water before witnesses is forever, while a word said on the dry binds the way a land promise does. Breaking it costs standing and allies, but it does not bind a name forever. Ferra notices that this is also why Ushar can be free with charges here.

A deep lord objects that women are not heard in this hall. Ilenne answers that a binding is a home matter, and homes, healing and the teaching songs are spheres where women do speak. Ressar rules for her: his office keeps the songs, and the songs of binding are home songs. After that Ferra speaks through Ilenne, and the hall notices whose words they are. The three objections fall in turn. Asked what the stones say, Ressar gives the conditions and nothing more: the water, witnesses, both say it, once. The stones do not say who. Against the claim that the witnesses were the queen's, Ferra points out that Taurre's watch is drawn from the Rim and Below together and answers to the crown as an office, and that the Rim's own pilot was in the water at the gate and saw it said. She asks whether the house's pilot is lying. The house does not answer. As for the door being outside the city, it is a gate in the Rim's own breakwater, and if it is outside the city, Ferra says, then the fee she paid was for a road outside the city and she would like her iron back. Someone on the deep benches laughs before he can stop himself.

The court shows its colour. One voice per great house and one for the Keepers; warm for, slate against; a held colour counts as no voice. The Rim's house is against, two deep houses hold their colour, and the rest, with the Keepers, are for. The queen counts aloud and pronounces: the binding was made, and the objections fail. Ushar then moves for the other thing, an order setting the binding aside notwithstanding, for the good of the throne, while both of them live. The hall goes still, because everyone knows what the question touches and nobody will say it in front of strangers. The court cannot answer it without saying the custom aloud. The queen defers the remedy to the season. The binding stands; the motion waits. Ushar never tries the succession road, and Ferra, watching him not try it, writes down that he is cleverer than the house he speaks for. Armand notices the shape of what nobody is saying and writes down that he does not know what it is.

A deep lord asks how eight hulls stand in the air. Ressar answers with the stone: the First City, floated on stones, sank with everyone aboard when the war's dead ground crossed the water. That is attested history. Ushar's addition is rhetoric: the land's ships hang from stones in the same way. Armand sees the move and notes it, and privately notes that the merfolk learned not to float a city without ever learning why it sank. He does not say so. An elder of the old families of the shallows tells of a party that went back early and whose stones went dark. It could have been thin water. Nobody can say. They read it as a breach of the old promise that nearly came due.

Then Ushar lays the charge. The human side once promised to leave the old peoples alone. Eight armed hulls under a gold-eyed king past the wall is that promise broken, and the queen's invitation reaches no further than her own word. Then he points at the coat: the land's king comes to the queen's hall wearing on his back the beast that burned his own ship. Emeric does not answer. Nobody connects it to any name. The hall hears it as rhetoric, and it works anyway. Nobody on Kaldo's side can answer the charge, because the text of the old promise is not kept in Aumere. Emeric asks one question, on the dry: what does it say? Ressar replies that it was sealed where all the peoples' seals went, and it is not here. Armand files that away and underlines it.

The winged ones trade storm warnings and messages with the Rim for fishing rights on its cliffs, so the Rim's news is their trade goods. Sorrel, who keeps reporting birds that are too big, reports that two of them left the outer cliffs at dusk, flying in different directions.

Chapter 13: Sael

From 13 to 15 May, Bram walks the Rim, which its people call Sael, the dry. It is a working craft city with no palace: kilns, the rope-walk, kelp drying in long racks, and the geyser beside the kiln yard with its heat piped into the furnaces. The crafters work on their legs, rank a step below the court, and are not interested in being reminded of it.

The glassmakers have seen the Challenger's stacked glass nose at the gate and recognise it as a cruder version of their own held glass, and they tell him so kindly. Bram did not build it. Tess did, and she stayed home, so he carries her questions in a notebook and asks every one, including the stupid ones she told him to ask. They answer the stupid ones best. On the salvage quay, a freelance salvager named Oddrin and his animal haul a load of old bronze out of the caldera: a seal-like back the size of a cart, a line to a harness, Oddrin talking to it in a voice that is not quite speech. The animal goes where no diver can. Bram watches the whole haul and thinks only that he would like one. Beside the quay, on the pilots' hall, hangs the watch's hazard board, which he cannot read.

In the foreign quarter two smith factors look at Bram's beard, hands and height and go quiet in a way Bram cannot read and Emeric, behind him, very much can. Then they ask what the iron cost. Nobody tells them. At the certifying bench, a joint merfolk-and-smith institution run by the Rim's house, Ferra brings a bar of Kaldo steel. The clerk will not test it. It carries no hold's mark, and without a mark there is nothing to certify. Ferra asks for the test, not the mark. The clerk has no answer for that today. The bench's books sign off everything built on the Rim, including a six-year-old seal near a vent.

Behind the kilns, in a glaze-and-soap yard, works Neris, a Sael-born craftswoman without the words for what she knows. Ordinary soap barely lathers in seawater, because the sea's minerals turn it into scum. Neris's soap lathers in the sea because she blends in the boiled froth of one particular sea cucumber from the caldera's reef, which she calls sorvesh and Kaldo calls the froth-slug. She found it by watching what foamed on the rocks after storms and trying every slug she could reach. She can repeat the method and cannot explain it. The froth stings skin until she learned to work it in sharkskin gloves, and it stuns fish, so she pours her rinse water on the rocks above the tide and the fishermen hold it against her anyway. Emeric knows why it works and does not say. He asks her one question about her lye. She shows him, and then, unasked, shows him something else it does: left in a dish in a kiln cellar, it drinks the bad air. Her grievance comes out sideways. A Rim guild shut its door on her, and a rival took her soap and sells it under the guild's mark.

Bram builds a seismograph in hull four's workshop and brings it in by boat. Taurre allows it to stand on the watch's own fixed point on the ring's stable wall, on the watch's terms: the watch reads it too, and it comes off when he says. The watch's people pretend not to look at the turning drum. Ferra buys dried fish, kelp and salt with iron, delivery to the hulls included, and Bram watches the trader's colour do what the pilot's did. Ferra has the first load weighed on the quay and writes down the figure. The trader takes it as an insult until he sees her weighing the empty crates on the way back too.

On the glassmakers' quay Armand buys the first pieces for the collection he will bring home: a held-glass offcut, a glass float, and a child's glass bead toy. He writes up each one: what, where, from whom, the price in Kaldo iron, and whose diary. The glassmaker asks what a dry man wants with a toy. She takes him for about thirty, and when he says he is past ninety she does not believe him. He tells her it is so that a child who has never seen the sea will know another child played with it. She gives him a second one.

Chapter 14: The Long Table

The chapter opens at home on 10 May and runs to about 20 May, overlapping the events at the ring. Saban is where Saban always is: the customs house on the quay, his own office, the line room, and one morning at the dam because he walks up to see it. Everything else reaches him through reports, the home net's traffic and his men's notes.

The Velmoor factors land, and they are exactly who the papers named, except one clerk. The named clerk fell ill at Velmoor, and his house sent his nephew with a genuine letter asking leave for a substitute. The letter came on the same ship rather than in advance, so under the rules the nephew cannot land, and Saban's people refuse him. Harrold admits him by written exception. Refusing would hold the whole first cargo on the quay and give Velmoor a reason to walk away, and the contract's own clause allows the regent to admit a substitute for cause. His conditions: the nephew stays inside the customs house, under escort every hour he is ashore, and sails with the ship. Harrold calls the lock intact because the escort is the lock. Saban minutes his objection beneath the first one. It is a calculated exception, not a gap, and both men are right about something.

Corbin is good at the stewards' reports, better than Harrold, and is learning the word book at night. He asks Harrold once why Harrold watches him when he thinks Corbin is not looking. Harrold says it is what fathers do, and Corbin lets it go. Harrold keeps his promise: Emeric means to tell Corbin about the leash himself, with Harrold present, on his return. Until then it stays untold.

On the first call day, Saban is in the line room for the harbour set and stays because it is worth watching. Tess gets through first and out of turn, having marked her call technical and urgent. It is a glass problem from the Forge books that she has already solved, and she wants Emeric to hear how. The operator puts her through with a sigh he will grow used to. Before she rings off, Emeric asks her for something that can lift Zale's ship clear of the water the next time something comes up under it. She asks for the hull's weight, its plating and where its ribs are. Saban hears only her side, "twelve, then, and they'll want charging," and does not ask. Harrold's report to Emeric is flat and official, the voice of a man who hates being regent: the holdings, the quay, the first Velmoor cargo, and that Corbin settled a water quarrel between two of Milford's villages without anyone having to ride out. Harrold does not praise, which makes it sound true. Saban hears only Harrold's half and the pause before he answers a question he does not repeat.

Saban walks to the dam the morning the first stage closes its gates and the reservoir begins to fill. Tess walks the crest on her own feet. The liquid mana store goes into the plant house at the dam's foot. By Ambrose's report from the line room, the first fibre line reaches the Answer Towers, and Amara tests it and calls it good.

Then Orla Garran at the Well logs something odd: a faint carrier signal from the east that keys on the relays' heartbeat interval, every interval, for three nights, and then stops. It is not a message. It is a clock being set. It reaches Saban as her weekly report with the log sheet copied. It is not a breach, since the heartbeats are broadcast and anyone can hear them, and listening proves nothing. Saban writes it in his book, tells Harrold it changes nothing until it does, and tells Orla to keep logging and Ambrose to change nothing, so the listener will not learn it was heard.

The fleet's news arrives by relay: the binding, Ushar, the call on the steps, the court. Harrold reads that his son Zale was called water at his own wedding and says something unprintable about the merfolk court, which Amara makes him repeat. Rowena adds one dry line about Velmoor merchants over the field set. Home is holding, and somebody else is listening to the same heartbeats Kaldo is.

Chapter 15: Clicks

From 14 to 17 May, Bram's seismograph drum records clicks: sharp, irregular, and coming closer together over two days. Emeric reads them the way an Earth record would read them, as rock breaking near the vent, on the open channel on the far side of the ring. He wants the vent district's channels closed and its people moved.

Taurre hears the clicks and knows the sound. It is held glass losing its keying under heat. Not the vent, but the structure behind the six-year-old seal on the Rim's side of the ring, where nobody was looking. He has heard it twice in his life, on smaller work. They disagree in front of the watch. Emeric says plainly that he is reading from what he knows of rock and does not know their glass. Taurre's call stands, because it is his water. Had they followed Emeric, they would have cleared the wrong side of the ring, and Emeric knows it before anyone says so.

Taurre is right about the place and wrong about the time. He closes the channels near the structure, moves people out of the nearby hard rooms, and sends a crew to shore the structure, judging from the click rate that he has days. He has hours. The same afternoon, by the queen's leave, the Challenger is down on the stable inner wall with Ressar aboard, because the Keepers' etchers asked to see her glass under load. Ned is at the helm and Noll at the stones, and she is loud under way, as she always is.

Then the release comes. Gas pours from the structure, a passage is lost, and a hard dome on the Rim's side begins failing slowly in foul water. The crew gets out, all but one. A repair diver named Haurin is caught at the dome's far door, alive in the bell beyond it.

The blame lands at once. The watch's listening nodes have the collapse, and minutes before it they have the Challenger's running gear on the inner wall. It is honest evidence, and it points one way. By dusk the Rim is saying the land ship shook the glass loose. The watch distrusts any readings from the accused, Bram's drum included, and says so. Ressar was aboard her and says where she was, but it is one Keeper's word, and the Keepers are divided.

Nobody goes in before the water is measured, and Emeric does not push past that. He asks what Taurre needs measured. The gas from volcanic vents is mostly carbon dioxide, with hydrogen sulphide and sulphur dioxide in smaller amounts, so the water near the release is low in oxygen and thick with dissolved gas. Gills cannot work in it. Before anyone suits up, Emeric says aloud that Kaldo's gill packs, which also draw their oxygen from the water, will fail in it for the same reason. At the Keepers' hall, Haurin's partner Oussa is waiting. Emeric sees her and goes back to work, which is the right thing to do and feels like nothing.

Chapter 16: Flood Slow

On 17 and 18 May the rescue begins with measurement. The failing hard dome and the bell beyond it lie about sixty feet down, where the sea presses at nearly three times surface pressure. The bell holds air at that pressure. The dome holds air at surface pressure and is failing. The real danger is not the bell but the dome: if it implodes, the shock travels through water and rock and kills anyone near the bell's water door. So Taurre's first order is to flood the failing dome slowly, over hours, through its own vents, until it holds water at sea pressure and can no longer fail. Nobody goes close until its load is falling.

Sample bottles come up on a line from the Challenger at the passage mouth and are read on deck. Lime water clouds for carbon dioxide and lead paper blackens for hydrogen sulphide. There is no oxygen number, since a flame test would be unreliable and unsafe with a gas that burns, so Bram and Taurre take the conservative call: the water is unbreathable until it tests clean. Taurre opens one route, along the passage side away from the failing dome, for one diver and one standby at a time. He sets a reserve line on the Challenger's air bank, enough for one failed attempt and a safe exit for everyone, and makes it clear that when the bank reaches that line everyone withdraws, whatever the dome is doing.

Haurin's bell makes its air from the sea through gill plates, and the sea around it is foul, so his emergency store is nearly gone. He needs fresh air fed in and the carbon dioxide he breathes out taken away. Bram remembers Neris's dish in the kiln cellar and goes to her yard. She agrees on one condition: the watch logs the charge as hers, by name, not as the guild's. Her rival took her soap under the guild's mark, and this one will carry her name. Taurre logs it. She makes slaked shell lime with a little of her lye through it, which is soda lime, the same absorbent Earth's rebreathers use, loaded into porous clay beads from her glaze kiln. It removes carbon dioxide and makes no oxygen; the oxygen comes from the Challenger's hose. At the outlet end she adds a handful of beads treated with a reef lichen she uses in glazes, which changes colour as gas starts getting through. It is a warning, not a gauge, and they change the charge on the clock regardless. The whole thing sits in a sealed, baffled clay canister with felt dust traps and a hand bellows, and two spare charges sealed in wax. Nobody has made this mixture before, and they say so. Bram copies the method into Tess's notebook with Neris's name at the top.

Finn goes in on a hose from the Challenger's flask bank, the standby behind him, a bail-out flask on his back, while the Challenger's electric floods light the passage and merfolk on the watch boat stare at the light as though at something ugly they cannot stop watching. Haurin wants out. If he opens the water door and swims, his gills take the foul water. If anyone opens the failing dome to reach him, it goes. Finn has to keep a frightened man inside a bell, through a sheet of clear water, at arm's length, with forty words of his language. He uses the hand signs from the primer and gives Haurin back the hand across the eyes: I am afraid. It works because it is true. Finn passes through the end of a third hose, metered by a flow limiter Bram sets, and the canister with its spares. On Finn's sign Haurin shuts his gill plates' valve, and he works the bellows himself, which keeps his hands busy and his mind off the water door. The outlet beads begin to change colour as Neris said they would. By the second charge Haurin is watching the beads instead of the door. Finn withdraws to a shallow hold under the watch boat while the dome fills. Noll calls the air bank every quarter hour, and it gets closer to the reserve line than anyone says out loud.

When the dome is full and safe, Finn goes back, brings Haurin out on his second mouthpiece across the foul stretch, and Haurin's gills take over as soon as the water is clean. The station runs Haurin's ascent by its own rule. Finn, who has no bonded stone, does his stops on the hose, surfaces with Imani waiting, throws up on the Challenger's deck, and is embarrassed about it for two chapters.

At the merfolk's diving station the healers know pressure injury better than Imani and say so. She knows things about bad air that they do not, and they write them down. Oussa holds Haurin's hand and keeps her own well away from his throat, and Imani notices that everyone here does this without thinking. A deep-house widow named Orrane, who works at the station as some court women do, watches Imani more than the patient. They speak only about the work.

Taurre states his finding once: the clicks were the glass, and the failure began at the structure. He does not say whose. The rescue proves nothing about guilt. The nodes still show the land ship down there that afternoon.

Chapter 17: Fixed Points

From 19 to 22 May, Taurre lets Ferra read the watch's stones with him, Ilenne translating and the word book open. Each reading of the fixed points near the structure looks like normal seasonal movement on its own. Ferra lays the structure's points beside those of the stable wall, week by week, the way she would lay two ledgers side by side, and reads the difference. The drift began in mid-April by her count against the convoy's log, before the fleet's first hull reached the thick water's edge, when the Challenger was about a thousand miles away, slung under the K Endeavor. Taurre sees it the moment it is laid out and says in his own colour that nobody had ever laid them side by side. A second watch official checks the comparison, since Taurre's part in it could invite a bias charge.

Taurre's finding says exactly what the stones show. The failure began before the convoy came. The land ship did not start it. Whether her running gear hastened the end of something already failing, the stones cannot say. He writes that last line himself, because it is true and because he would rather the house find it in his finding than prove it was left out. The Keepers accept that she did not start it. The Rim's house does not. Its answers are that the watch's stones belong to the watch, which stood witness at the door for the land man, and that by the watch's own words the land ship may have finished what the seal began. The second argument works, because nobody can disprove it.

After an event in its water, the watch may call any record touching a structure there. Taurre calls the seal's record, and Ferra reads it in the watch house as his guest. The Rim's house built the seal, its own inspectors signed it every year, the Keepers countersigned, and the bench passed it. The yearly inspections read the seal's face, where nothing leaked, which was the point of a seal. Nobody suppressed anything. Everyone assumed.

Taurre publishes the finding under the watch's seal. Ferra wants Kaldo to add its own charge in the dry hall: that the house blamed the Challenger for a failure its own seal caused and its own inspectors signed off. Emeric says no. The finding is the watch's and speaks for itself, and Kaldo standing up to interpret it would turn a safety record into a land king's accusation against a great house. Ferra's case is strong. Restraint leaves the house's version standing in every hall the watch's seal does not reach, and the house will not restrain itself. Emeric's judgement holds too. They do not settle it.

That night the three of them lie in bed in the hard room with the pumps ticking. Emeric tries to pull Ferra away from the records. She says work first. He says he would like that in writing. Imani says it is in writing, on Ferra's list, item eleven. Then Imani refuses to let the Rim question go: why did he fight so hard against accusing a house that is plainly guilty?

He admits that when he first saw convicts in irons on the road, years ago, his first thought was to ask who owned them, and Ferra finds the wording disturbing and says so. He gives them one short, personal account. On his world, men owned men. People were carried across an ocean and sold, and their children were born owned, because the law made a child's status follow the mother's. Bondage existed there in many forms, but this one was for life, by birth, and by race. Then, briefly, Ferra's hair: there is an island on his world where red hair is commoner than almost anywhere, conquered by its neighbour, its land taken, thousands sent to labour overseas, and a famine while food still left its ports. And the honest part: that was brutal, and it was not the same as being made property by birth, and people on his world used the one to make the other sound smaller.

They challenge his old theory of why it never happened here, that one mage does a gang's work and a crushed people might birth a mage who tears you down. Ferra does not accept it. Most people here have no magic, the towers kept the glyphs, and mines and fields still needed hands. Imani asks, "Is a chain not a chain because nobody can see it?" She names the rite, the glyph laws, the road gangs. He concedes. His guess was a guess.

Then he says it once: a man kills you, takes your house, and his grandchildren say they had nothing to do with it while sleeping in your bed. "That's us. That's Kaldo." That is why he will not stand up in their court and accuse their great house, guilty or not. "So we pay rent," Ferra says. "Rent means someone agreed to let us stay." He asks whose permission they are missing. "Start with the people whose water we put that stone in," Imani says. Ferra reaches for her list, and he catches her wrist. "Not item eleven." "No," she says. "Item one."

They find their way back to warmth, and to one last quiet thought. The Challenger at the gate, knights who can breathe the merfolk's water, his brother's lineage named on the steps: nobody here has said war, and all of it is the threat of one. He says he hates that it works. He lies awake after. The women have their own thoughts, and Ferra's are on the list.

Chapter 18: Under Is Teeth

Between 24 and 26 May, Zale swims with Ilenne inside the ring in his Mark II suit, on the gate side where the watch allows it. It is the best hour he has had since the wall. Then something big comes up from below. Emeric, on the gate steps, feels it and cannot read past it: the animal's own density swamps his sense, so he cannot tell whether anyone is with it. Ilenne turns it by speaking to it, which in home water she can do better than any merfolk could in thin water. The charm does not come into it, because the beast is not fast, only big. Zale comes up with two cracked ribs and a torn suit for Abloh to mend.

Ilenne explains what she could tell. Animals show a sea-talker what they are doing in pictures and wants. This one was not hunting. She has met a deep thing that was, the hungry one on the crossing, which took anything and would not listen. This one listened. It had been given a shape, a long thing with a land smell that does not belong in the water, and it went for that shape and nothing else. That much she perceived. What she infers, and marks as inference, is that animals here do not hunt a shape they have never met, so someone who speaks to sea life gave it that shape, and the people she knows who can do that are her own. She does not say who or which house, and neither does anyone else. That dusk, ribs strapped, Zale goes up the stages for the post anyway, because his crew is waiting.

At the station that evening, while Zale's ribs are seen to, Orrane finds Imani alone and tells her the custom's other edge. Kill the husband and the wife is free. Done openly, she would hate you, so it is done quietly. Most never do it. Some who believe a person should be theirs do. Orrane tells it the way a woman tells a doctor how a thing is done in her house, and says nothing about her own husband. Imani writes it on a card in her clinical hand, like a medical history from a witness: what she was told, by whom, when, and what she could not check. It goes in her coat. That night in the hard room she tells Zale, Ferra and Emeric. Zale names it widow-making. It is a joke for one second, and then it is not.

Ilenne knew. Zale goes down to the bell and asks her. She learned it as a child at court, the way every court child knows the thing nobody says, from the way widows were looked at and from what the women did not discuss in front of the girls. She believed the house would play the public road first, because it always had, and that she had time to tell him once they were inside and she could watch his back herself. The beast came first. She did not say it on the voyage for the same reason she would not say the house's name on the sea: saying it out there made it real, and out there was the only place it was not. The question she has to face is not why she was ashamed but why she did not at least warn him to watch the water. She has no good answer, does not pretend to, and says she was wrong. Zale answers that she let him walk in blind, that he would have walked in anyway, and that the second thing does not fix the first.

Thalune, at her post outside the water door, hears almost nothing and sees everything: the heir's colour broke over her husband, and she did not hold it. By the next sitting every hall has it. The house now has a witness that the bond is real and that the two of them are hurting, which is exactly what makes the quiet road worth taking. They do not make up. His public answer on the steps was a thing he did; this was done to him by the person he did it for, and her admission does not close it. They sleep in the same bell, him on the floor again, and this time it is not funny.

Privately, Emeric tells Ferra and Imani what comes next if this is the custom: more accidents, each deniable, with Zale as the target because Randillia is the deterrent. Nobody can say who gave the animal its shape, and he does not pretend to know. He does not say what he would like to do to whoever did it. Imani sees it anyway.

Chapter 19: Dead Ground

From 27 to 29 May, the Rim's house names the stone. In the dry hall, before the court, Ushar says the queen let a dead zone into her water and told the court nothing. The stones say dead ground sank the First City; that is history. Ushar says this stone is that weapon, that the land brought it here, and that the queen let it in; that is his charge. He lays it beside the old promise and the vent, one after another. In the dry hall none of it binds his name. Among the people of the old families of the shallows stands the guard who went slate at the door, Dessaun, who heard Emeric tell the queen on the steps. He does not look away from her. For his family, a dead zone in their water is the war again. He waited to see whether the queen would tell the court, and when she did not, he took it to the one house that would use it.

The queen answers. She was told at the steps, first, before her own daughter. She kept the ship beyond the markers on her own order. She did not tell the court because the court has no closed sitting and anything said there travels, and she is watching it travel now. It is true, and it does not save her with the old families. Some of them go slate and hold it.

The court asks the land king, and he answers on the dry. He does not offer to sink the stone, hand it over or destroy it. None of those can be undone, none is his to decide in someone else's sea, and handing a dead-zone stone to a frightened court would be worse than keeping it. He does not argue the old promise; he says he has not read it and will not pretend he has. Then he gives one narrow, exact dry "I will": the stone will not come inside the markers while his ships are in Aumere's water, without her word, and he answers for it himself. Until now the K Amara's line was only the queen's port order, so a breach would have been her failure. Now it is also his public word, and a breach would be his before it is hers. That gives the queen something to stand behind. Ushar says a dry word from a gold-eyed king is worth what dry words are worth. Emeric does not answer, and the hall notices.

The queen then gives her own port order, separate and tighter, through the watch. The K Amara goes out of view of the ring's watch posts, to the watch's station at the Grey Mark, a grey reef-stone pillar on the fetch line about thirty miles from the ring, where she will lie at anchor on the sea. Ward's rule that no hull goes alone sends escort three with her. The cost, counted honestly: two of the fleet's three armed hulls, about four hundred of its people, about half, and about ninety of its knights, about three quarters, Arthur's team among them. At the ring stay escort one with Ruan's guns and its knights, four flight knights on each of the three air hulls, and the K Endeavor's hospital deck: about thirty knights in all. Ward will command the knights by set from the K Amara's deck. Escort one's knight-captain, Sir Hadden Voll, will command them on the spot. Emeric commands the fleet. Nobody pretends it is the same.

Ward takes the order over the field set. He says once that this is the second time a risk only Emeric thought he could manage has been decided somewhere Ward is not. Emeric says he knows. Then, privately, they set the rule. Outside the outer markers, Ward may move either hull anywhere to save life without asking, reporting to the watch as he moves. Inward past the markers is a breach of the queen's order and of Emeric's word, whatever the cause, unless the queen's word comes first. If it comes to that, Emeric tells him: "If you have to, come. It's mine." Ferra sends word to the Rim's fish traders that the K Amara's loads will now go out to the Grey Mark under the watch's escort, weighed as before.

After the hall empties, the queen speaks through Ilenne. The full court sits at the floor, Below, near the Breath, and it will only hear a land king properly in the water. She invites him down, an invitation and not an order, with her hand turned up. He says yes, on the dry. Ferra looks at the field set on her belt and does not say what she is thinking. Emeric knows where his last contact will be: the head of the bell stages.

Maeve goes out with the boat carrying the order to the K Amara, packs her kit while the ship readies to lift, and comes back with the boat to the K Discovery without asking anyone. Mira is at the K Amara's rail as the boat pulls away. It is the first time the two of them have been on different hulls since Kaldo. Emeric notices, and for once does not glare at Bren.

At dusk the K Amara goes out past the markers with escort three, her scorched side turned to the ring, and the Rim's watch-fires come on one by one along the breakwater as she goes.

Where the Story Stands After Chapter 19

Kaldo has learned the shape of Aumere's power and accepted every limit it was handed. The court found the binding valid but deferred Ushar's motion to set it aside, because answering it would mean saying aloud a custom nobody says. Zale answered the insult in the merfolk's own form and won, but Ushar's parting line, that he would not speak in the water to a man who cannot breathe it, stays with him because it is true. The old promise between the peoples, now called a breach, has been charged in open court and cannot be answered, since its text is sealed somewhere far away, and the winged ones have already carried the news off in two directions.

The quiet road has opened. A beast given Zale's shape came up beneath him, Orrane has told Imani the custom's other edge, and Zale now knows that Ilenne knew and did not warn him. He is still angry, they have not made up, and the court's watcher has reported that the bond is real. The vent disaster cost Emeric a public mistake: Taurre was right about the place and wrong about the time, and Haurin was saved through Taurre's procedure, the Challenger's air and Neris's lime beads. The watch's finding clears the Challenger of starting the failure but cannot say whether she hastened it, and the Rim's house leans on that gap. Ferra and Emeric still disagree about whether Kaldo should accuse the house, and Ferra's list now has an item one.

The stone has been named in court through Dessaun's leak. Emeric has given his first dry "I will," and the queen's port order has sent the K Amara, escort three and most of the fleet's fighting strength thirty miles out to the Grey Mark. Ward holds a private rule: if he must come in to save life, the breach is Emeric's. Emeric has accepted the queen's invitation to go Below, beyond any radio's reach. At home, the dam is filling, the Velmoor factors are on the quay with one clerk's nephew admitted by exception, Corbin still has not been told about the leash, and something in the east has been listening to Kaldo's relay heartbeats and setting a clock by them.

This batch shows how much Kaldo is willing to give up to be a lawful guest, and how much that costs: Emeric's word, the fleet's strength, and his reach are now all bound by someone else's rules, just as the dangers around Zale begin to close in.
