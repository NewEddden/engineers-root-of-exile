Chapter 2 - Hollow Gifts

Any time a prince or princess left the capital, the kingdom held a banquet in their honor. Well wishes, gifts, a full room of people who'd rather be elsewhere. A going-away party with better silverware. They held his in the same room where the exile had been decided. His father had called him in privately beforehand to lay out where he'd be sent. A barony on the outskirts of the capital, called Kaldo, named for the knight who founded it. Kal had earned enough merit in service that the crown gave him a choice: stay or go, and he'd gone and built something of his own instead. The banquet commenced. The local nobility filed through with their wishes and their gifts.

Being the third prince meant nothing of real weight was ever going to come across that table. Emeric sat too many steps removed from anything worth buying, and most of the capital's nobles were the quiet-plotting sort who reserved their expensive gifts for people who could return favors later.

His father went first. Fifty gold Rand in a pouch, pressed into his hands with more formality than warmth, the kind of gift a king gives when he can't say the thing he means. Alongside it came a modest heap of mana crystals, uncut and unremarkable, the sort of thing an affluent tutor hands a promising student. Emeric read the shape of it without needing help. Harrold had never quite given up on the boy who used to be enthralled by crystals, who read every book on them he could get his hands on just to see how they worked, and who might resurface given enough idle hours in a quiet barony. They'd also pawn well. Blacksmiths loved the things.

Corbin came next and made a show of it, the way the crown prince made a show of most things. "Something to bring back the old passion." He held out a set of Hollow Crystals, smiling the sort of smile that read as nostalgia to anyone not paying attention. He turned one over in his palm before handing it across. "Unchanging. Just like you. Keep staying true to you, little brother." The word sat there, pleasant enough on the surface.

Emeric took it apart anyway, out of old habit, the way he'd have parsed a clause that compiled clean and ran wrong. Unchanging isn't a compliment for a gift. It's a diagnosis. They were called Hollow because nobody in recorded history had ever gotten one to do anything at all, dead weight carved to look purposeful, worthless enough that giving them away by the handful cost Corbin nothing. So the word wasn't nostalgia. It was a verdict wearing nostalgia's coat. Just like you. Third son, legitimate by blood and by absolutely nothing else, and never going to be more than that no matter what came next.

Corbin hadn't handed him a hobby. He'd handed him a sentence, wrapped warm enough that the room would hear the wrong one.

"Your mother gave you one of these once." Softer now, almost gentle. "Thought it might mean something, you having some others."

Something in Emeric's chest went quiet and heavy. No fear in it, nothing sharp, just the specific ache of a memory he'd spent years keeping in a drawer. He couldn't say why the rune itself pulled on that ache the way it did, only that it did, some old grief surfacing without paperwork attached to explain itself. Corbin was already moving on to the next pleasantry. Whatever his smile was doing by then, it wasn't sincerity.

General Voss brought a soldier's gift for a soldier's road. A heavy oilskin cloak, collar reinforced, stitched the way gear gets stitched for months in the field rather than one season at court. No speech came with it. A short nod, and: "You'll need this more than a crystal where you're going, Your Highness." First gift all evening that assumed he'd actually be cold.

Armand found him a few gifts later, unassuming as ever, holding out a slim leather-bound notebook with both hands as if it might bruise. "My own notes." His old tutor wasn't quite meeting his eyes. "On crystal theory. I never did finish teaching you properly. Maybe you'll get further with it than I did." It wasn't an apology. It sat close enough to one that Emeric didn't know where to put it, so he thanked the man and let the line move before either of them had to stand in the space neither of them had filled.

Then a duke he barely knew. Old blood, from a house said to be as old as the main Randillia line itself. His gift was a set of carved wooden soldiers, the kind you give a boy playing war on his bedroom floor. The kind you give a boy half Emeric's age. Duke Aldane offered them plainly, with a smirk so small most of the room would have missed it. He'd come out of courtesy, the way most of the capital had come out of courtesy, and he hadn't bothered dressing the insult as anything else. He didn't think Emeric was worth the effort of a decent lie.

The rest of the room moved through the same motions. Minor nobles, foreign envoys, generic luxuries handed over because attendance demanded it. Nobody in that hall expected him to matter again.

The line finally let him go, and he crossed the hall toward the doors through the last of the crowd, and somewhere in the middle of it a man passed close behind him, near enough that the small hairs on his neck stood without being told to, and by the time he turned there was nothing there but backs and shoulders and the same faces that had spent the evening measuring him and finding him wanting. Something had brushed the edge of a sense he did not yet have a name for, a wrongness with no shape to it, there and gone before he could put a wall around it. He put it down to the wine, and the nerves, and the particular airless dread of a room full of people who had all quietly agreed he was finished. It was not a reading. It sat beside nothing, belonged to no column, and a single stray number with nothing to enter it against is a number a careful man sweeps off the page. He swept it off and went to find his carriage.

The carriage waiting in the courtyard was plain, built for distance rather than display, and Emeric's eye caught on the undercarriage before he reached the door. Four black stones, flecked with small points of light like chips of night sky, sat fitted into the axle housings. They'd been carved in a dense repeating lattice, and no one cuts a pattern that consistent for decoration. Consistency like that means the pattern is the point. He crouched for a closer look. One of the stable hands misread the interest as concern and rocked the carriage to demonstrate, and the whole frame settled and came back in a slow cushioned wobble no cart axle had any business producing on its own. Emeric had seen that motion before, on Earth.

A stone carved to resist and release force at a fixed threshold instead of passing it straight up through the frame. No moving parts. No linkage. Nothing to lubricate or replace. Somebody had made a rock behave like a spring, and done it without a single spring anywhere in the build. It shouldn't have surprised him as much as it did. He'd spent years in carriages exactly like this one, crossing the capital at a leisurely pace, thinking about nothing. Today it bothered him. He wondered when he'd stopped caring how any of it worked.

He climbed in without saying anything, unwilling to hold the day up further, and spent the first mile at the window watching the road and the castle and the people, feeling the carriage go soft and slow over ruts instead of jolting through them, and deciding he'd better remember the feeling the next time he caught himself assuming he already understood what this world could do.

The castle looked grown out of the hill rather than built on it, tier stacked on tier all the way to the top, each ring of wall sitting higher and tighter than the one below. From the window, the whole city read as a single fortified spiral. Outer wall first, hugging the river's curve, then a band of packed rooftops, then a second wall, then more rooftops, narrower now, until the pattern ran out of room and the castle took over, spires catching light above everything else.

The river did most of the work at the bottom, looping nearly the whole way around the base of the hill before doubling back on itself, so that from high enough the city would sit inside a coil of its own moat. Bridges crossed at a handful of fixed points and no more, each narrow enough for a squad to hold and almost certainly meant to be held that way. He counted the rings without deciding to, the same reflex that used to count load paths, and the count came out clean. Nothing about the layout was accidental. Someone had designed it like a filter. Only so many ways in, every one of them watched. It dawned on him somewhere past the second wall that the capital didn't smell.

The him from this world had never known it to. The him from Earth had read plenty about what a medieval city was supposed to do to a nose. Back when he was more studious, he'd worked through the books Armand kept giving him, and every one of them said the same thing about this place: the capital was one of the best places in the world to live, and the reason was mages. Not battle mages. Utility. Farming, waste, water, a dozen other systems every kingdom needed and only this one had staffed properly. The mage tower was the loudest example of it.

It rose past the rooftops well before the carriage reached the bridges, and he found himself watching it through the glass without meaning to. Too far off to make out anything. No gallery ring, no mages, just the shape of it against the sky and the fact, sitting in his head with no picture attached, that his tutor had taken him up there once as a boy. He didn't remember the day. No stairs, no sound of Armand's voice, no sense of what the air was like that high up. What he had was the outcome, flat and factual, the way a fact survives when everything around it has rotted off.

He'd stood at the crown of that tower and watched a full council session, twenty mages strong, hold a floating gallery up by hand. That was the part nobody in the capital found strange. A full ring of stone hanging clear of the tower's spine with nothing beneath it but air and rune-light, and the thing keeping it there was men. Ten of them at any hour, each gripping a crystal the size of a melon, eyes half closed, jaws set with the concentration of someone holding a weight he can't put down. Behind each of the ten stood another, watching the way a spotter watches a lift, ready for the instant the first man's grip slipped. Every so often one would step back with his shoulders dropping and another would move into the gap mid-stride, the handoff practiced so smooth it barely read as a handoff at all.

He'd asked an acolyte why nobody had simply built the thing to hold itself up. The answer had stuck word for word. The gallery held the Circle's council, and it floated because the Circle answered to no staircase. The men holding it weren't laborers. They were the order's best initiates, competing for the honor of doing it.

Somewhere in that same conversation, a boy who hadn't yet lost interest in how things worked decided the whole arrangement was the coolest thing he'd ever seen. That verdict hadn't traveled with him. Only the record of having reached it. Looking at the same tower now, with nothing behind his eyes but an engineer's read on the problem, Emeric couldn't find the boy's answer anywhere in himself. Ten men. Before them ten others. Before those, ten more, generations of them passing an unsolved engineering problem hand to hand because somebody a long time ago had decided the cost was the point rather than the flaw.

It wasn't impressive. It was the most spectacular waste of skilled labor he'd seen since he got here, propped up by a story good enough that nobody currently carrying that gallery had ever been handed a reason to ask why they still had to. He caught himself sorting again as the tower slid past the window. That reaction was the boy's; this one is mine, the two of them in separate rooms comparing notes. Then the capital gate was in front of him, and he couldn't say when it had gotten there.

"Goodbye, sweet Randillia City," he said, and heard the sadness in it.

The roads inside the walls were smooth, and that was before you accounted for whatever those axle stones were doing. Just past the gates they went to cobble. Not terrible. Not what he'd gotten used to either. After that they stopped pretending to be roads at all, and the suspension started earning its keep. On Earth he'd have been calling a mechanic the day he arrived.

Kaldo was the better part of a week out by carriage, slower than a horse would need, but princes didn't ride the whole way through country like this if there was another option. Six guards rode with him. Ward at the lead. Rylan, Declan, Osric, Petram, and a lean, quiet man named Garrin who had said maybe ten words since the capital. Two days out, three at most, they'd hit the bad stretch, the forest road past Wayford, and the horses would need real rest before it. Not a water break. Rest.

Petram raised it first, reins loose, voice pitched in that easy, reasonable register guards used when they weren't trying to be noticed. "Horses won't make the forest road fresh, Your Highness. Better we camp here and push through in daylight tomorrow." It was sound advice. Nobody argues with sound advice.

By the time the stories started, the fire had burned down to a low orange bed. Osric went first, some half-true business about a bandit camp outside Grayrock that grew in the telling, three men becoming five, five becoming a small army by the end of it. Declan called him on the arithmetic without much heat, and the two of them argued the number back down while Petram laughed into his cup.

"You're all soft," Ward said, not looking up from the blade he was working. "Try a real posting sometime. You'd stop counting bandits and start counting hours until you're relieved."

It wasn't much of a story, and he clearly meant it to end there. Nobody pushed. Something in the flatness of it told them not to.

In the lull the fire threw its small ring of light and the dark stood close around it the way dark does past the last town on a road, and Emeric, half-listening, felt it again, the same brush at the edge of that unnamed sense, a wrongness out past the horses where the treeline started. He looked and found the ordinary nothing a man always finds looking into the dark. A fox, he told himself, or a marten, or one of the patient night things that drift to the edge of a fire's reach to weigh what the light is worth and then think better of it. He was ringed by six armed men with a seventh working a blade by the coals. Whatever stood out there in the country could want the horses or want nothing at all; either way it was a guard's problem and not his. He filed it where he had filed the thing in the hall, which was to say nowhere, under no heading, a second stray number with no column to keep it in and no reason yet to think the two were the same figure written twice.

Rylan filled the quiet instead, gentler about it.

"Kaldo used to be good, before all that." He poked at the fire with a stick, talking more to himself than the circle. "My wife's family's been there three generations. When I was a boy the harbor still had real trade coming through, not just fishing boats patching their own nets. My mother used to walk me down to watch the ships unload." He shook his head. "War took the men. Plague took the rest. My family stayed through both. I don't know if that was courage or just nobody having anywhere else to go."

"Your wife still there?" Petram asked, easy, like it was nothing.

"Nessa, yeah. And our boy Finn. Little Wren too." His voice warmed on the names. "Haven't seen them in four months. Figured I'd get a real homecoming out of this posting, at least."

Garrin didn't say anything. He listened, the way he'd been listening the whole trip. Petram had dug tea out of the carriage stores and steeped it over the fire, and Emeric didn't think twice about the cup that came around. Bitter, herbal, the kind of thing men drank on the road all the time. There was nothing to think about. Not until the world went soft at the edges and every guard but one slumped where he sat.

"Fuck. We've been poisoned." Emeric got the words out through the drowsiness that was already taking his jaw.

Ward didn't go down. He swayed, fought it, planted himself between the fire and the carriage like a wall refusing the concept of falling. Whatever dose had been measured to drop a normal man barely made a dent in a frame built like his. Emeric didn't go down either. His vision blurred and his limbs turned to lead, and then something in him that had always run a little hotter and cleaner than it should have burned the fog off before it finished the job. He came back to himself in time to see Petram's knife already drawn and already moving toward Ward's back.

After that it was quick. Ward turned faster than a drugged man had any right to and took the blade across his forearm instead of his spine, then put Petram into the dirt hard enough that he wasn't going to be running anywhere. Emeric was surprised the man was still breathing. Half-fogged and bleeding, Ward had kept enough sense to remember that a dead traitor tells you nothing. Garrin was already gone. Boots into the treeline before anyone thought to stop him. Ward's bracer sat dead against his skin. The Kinetic Rune in it needed a clean line of focus to pull the crystal's charge through, and there was no clean anything in his head right then. He could probably have run the man down on muscle and instinct alone. Chasing a healthy man through dark woods without the boost, on legs he couldn't fully trust, was also a good way to end the night face down in a creek. He let him go. Somebody would have to find Garrin later, if Garrin was going to be found.

Emeric sat in the dirt afterward with his hands shaking, watching Ward bind his own wound with his teeth and one working hand, and something in his chest broke open that had been sealed for fifteen years.

"I froze." His voice came out smaller than he meant. "Just like then."

Ward didn't ask what he meant.

"You were seven," he said.

Emeric was already elsewhere. A carriage that smelled like his mother's perfume. Her arm coming across him, pushing him behind her body half a second before the world ended. He hadn't moved then either. He hadn't moved, and she'd died, and somewhere in the years after, a small silent part of him had decided that not moving was safer than moving wrong. From now on, he thought, dragging the back of a filthy hand across his face, there's no Emeric and Marcus. There's just me. I don't get to hide behind either name anymore. Not when standing there quietly nearly got my men killed.
