Book 2, Chapter 23: Taster

The baskets came down to the head of the stages at a quarter to eight on the Thursday morning, and Imani was waiting for them on the last stair with her register open on her knee and her scale on the step beside her, because that was where she'd decided to be.

Fire was the Rim's. That was the thing to understand about the food, Ilenne had told her in the first week, and Imani had written it at the top of a page. Below, nobody cooked, because nobody could; a bell couldn't take a flame and the sea couldn't hold one. Everything eaten at the floor that had been near a fire came down from a Rim kitchen. And the land husband, who couldn't live on raw fish and kelp and didn't pretend to, was fed by the house that held the Rim's furnaces, every day, a basket down the stages at eight and another at four. It was a courtesy. It was also a door, Ferra had said, and the house knew it, and so did everyone else.

So the custom came with it. The sender's taster went down with the basket and ate from every dish in front of the receiver's people before anyone else touched it. A mouthful from each. That was the old rule, and the Rim kept it, and the house kept it most carefully of all.

And Imani had her own rule, which was older than the Rim's as far as she was concerned, because it was hers. Everything Kaldo's people ate Below went past her station on the last stair before it went into the pool. Zale's, every day since the binding. Now Emeric's and Armand's as well. Weighed, and looked at, and carded: what it was, how much, and which of the heavy fish was in it, because the thick sea's fish carried more of what the water carried than anything at home, and a man who ate too much of the wrong one too fast at depth lay down and didn't get up for a day. She'd learned that before they ever reached the ring, and written it on the back of the dose card.

The taster came down the wet stair behind the two porters with the baskets, carefully, in bare feet.

Her name was Sarrou. Imani had learned that on the Tuesday. She was a small neat woman on her legs, older than Ilenne and younger than the queen, in the house's dark green with her hair bound up under a cloth, and she did this every day, twice, with her face composed and her colour held at a pleasant pale amber, the colour a servant wore in front of guests. She set the basket on the step and took the cloth off it.

Six covered dishes, earthenware, each with its lid tied down and a mark cut in the clay: one for the land king, one for the old land man, two for the husband, two for the Keeper and the pilot at the land men's door, who ate what the land men did. Bread. A crock of the salt fish in oil. A pot of something thick and brown with grains in it that smelled of smoke and ginger. Fruit from somewhere, dark and wrinkled.

Sarrou untied each lid in turn and ate from it.

She did it properly. A mouthful from each dish with a horn spoon she wiped on a cloth between, chewed and swallowed in front of Imani and the porters, and a sip from each of the two water skins. Then she put the lids back on and tied them, and sat back on her heels, and waited for the land healer to finish being slow.

Imani was being slow that morning. She knew it. She'd been slow every morning, and this morning she was slower, because on Tuesday a drain stone had come out of Zale's back strap and she hadn't slept properly since. She took each dish out of the basket and set it on her scale and wrote the weight. She opened each one and looked, and smelled, and with the tip of a clean knife turned over what was in it to see what was underneath. The king's had the smoked grain and a piece of the white fish, which was light. The old man's the same. The Keeper's and the pilot's the same, with more fish. She carded them. She put them back.

Then the husband's two. The first was the same as the others. The second had fish in it she didn't know: three pieces of something dark-fleshed and oily, cut thick, under a lid of the smoked grain. It wasn't on her card. It wasn't on the back of the dose card where she'd written the heavy ones as Ilenne named them, the ones Ilenne had said no to, never, and the lighter ones she'd allowed a little of to the knights. Imani stopped over it with her pencil and frowned. She wasn't going to card a fish she couldn't name and send it down to a man with cracked ribs who'd be bracing all day.

"These two wait," she said, and put her hand on the husband's dishes, and then on the step beside her: here. The porters understood that. "The rest can go."

The first porter took the basket down with the four dishes in it, the king's and the old man's and the two at their door. Sarrou went with him into the pool, on his hand, as she always did. Her colour was pale amber and perfectly level as the water closed over her. The second porter sat down on the step with Zale's two dishes beside him to wait for the land healer, with the patience of a man who was paid by the day.

Imani got out her book and her drawings and began to go through them for the dark fish. It wasn't there. She went through them again. She'd ask Ilenne at noon. She'd write the card when she knew. She sat on the step with the dish open on her knee and the second porter beside her, and took her time, because she'd decided on Tuesday that she was going to take her time over everything that went down that pool for the rest of her life.

At five past nine the first porter came up out of the pool on his own, very fast, and his colour was something Imani had never seen on anyone.



She didn't understand what he said. She didn't need to. She put her hand flat on the husband's two dishes and looked at the second porter until he nodded, and then she had her bag on her shoulder before he'd finished saying it, and the gill pack off the hook on the wall where she'd hung it in case, and she was in the pool with him with her helm going on as she went under, and he took her wrist.

The second bell.

Sarrou was on the bell's rock shelf with the water to her waist and her back against the glass, where she always waited for the porter to catch his breath, and she was trying to breathe.

She was doing it badly. Imani saw it from the water before she'd come up into the air: the small neat woman's chest going in short hard jerks, too fast, and her mouth open, and the cloth gone from her hair, and her colour gone from amber to a dull mottled grey-brown, like a bruise a week old. The basket was on the shelf beside her, half unpacked, with the four dishes going on down to the floor and nobody to take them.

"Stop it," said Imani, through her helm, and then pulled the helm off because nobody could hear her through it. "The basket. Nothing goes down. Nothing."

The porter didn't understand her. He understood her hand, flat, on the basket: stop. He took his hands off it.

Imani came up onto the shelf beside Sarrou on her knees.

She looked first. She always looked first. And she let her sight open, as narrowly as she could make it, the way she'd taught herself on the knights: in at the throat and the chest, and nowhere else.

It wasn't the depth. Sarrou had done this stair twice a day for weeks. It was something in her. Imani could see it, the way you'd see damp spreading in a wall: a slowness in the woman's blood, going outward from the gut, and everywhere it went the small fast work of the body went thick and stupid. The muscles round the lungs were doing their jobs late. The lungs were getting less with every breath. Not stopping. Slowing. It would get to the heart in its own time.

She'd eaten from every dish.

Imani thought of the six dishes on her scale, one after another, and her own knife going into each of them, and her pencil. An hour ago. The woman had eaten from all six an hour ago on the last stair, in front of her, and been well, and gone down.

"Which one," she said aloud, not to anybody. She knew. She'd known since the porter's face. "Which one was it."

She put her hands on the woman's chest and gave her what she could. Healing had a rate; it always had, and it always would, however much Emeric liked to talk about what it might do one day. She could push against the slowness. She couldn't make the poison not be in her. She held the muscles round the lungs and made them work a beat sooner than they wanted to, and felt them answer, and lose it, and answer again, and knew she couldn't do that for an hour. Not by herself. Not on legs.

Not on legs.

She'd learned it at the station, a fortnight ago, from Orrane, with her own hands on a furnace hand who'd breathed smoke: a merfolk in the air breathed with her lungs, the way anyone did, and when the lungs laboured you didn't fight them. You put her head in the sea. The gills were there all along, the four slits under each ear that Imani had looked at on Ilenne's neck the first week, and in the water they opened and did the work themselves, with no muscle in it worth the name, just the water going through. Orrane had done it for the furnace hand in a tank in the floor of the station's back room and he'd been up by evening.

"In," said Imani. "Into the water. Help me."

The porter at the rim had put the basket down on the shelf. He came and took Sarrou's other arm. They got her off the shelf into the sea between them, and Imani went in with her, with her helm back on and the pack drawing; and in the water, with her head under, Sarrou stopped fighting it.

Imani put her hand flat on the side of Sarrou's neck and looked.

The gills had opened. She could see them, fine and dark red, four under each ear, opening and closing slowly, and the water going through them, and on the other side of them the blood taking what the water gave it without the lungs having to do anything at all. The slowness was still there. It was still spreading. But the breathing didn't need the muscles now, and the muscles had stopped fighting a war they were losing.

Imani kept her hand there, and gave her the rest of what she had into the heart, which was the next thing it would reach.

She kept her alive the rest of the morning like that. In the bell's water, with the porter holding Sarrou's head against his shoulder so that Imani could watch her eyes through the glass, and her own hands; until the station's healers came down the stages at eleven, Orrane first, and took over without a word, and Imani sat on the shelf with the water to her waist and her hands shaking, which they never did.

She looked at the basket on the shelf.

Nobody below had eaten. The four dishes had stopped here with Sarrou, and the king's and the old man's would go back up to be thrown in the sea. And Zale's were on the last stair, up at the head, where she'd left them with the second porter, with no card under their ties, because she'd sat over the dark fish and not known it.

That was all. She'd been slow, and she'd been slow on purpose. If she'd been quick, Zale's two dishes would have gone down with the rest at eight, and he'd have eaten the dark fish at the bottom of the sea an hour ago, and there'd have been nobody in that bell who knew what she was looking at.



Sarrou worked it out on the pallet at the station at one o'clock, lying on her side under a blanket of woven weed with her colour coming back to her a little at a time, and Imani watched her do it.

She didn't say anything. She lay and looked at the wall. But Imani had been a doctor long enough to watch a patient understand something about herself, and she thought she watched it now: the woman's eyes going over and over something, and stopping, and going back. The morning, Imani thought. The dishes, and the order. Whose dish. Who'd packed it in the house's kitchen and tied its lid and cut the husband's mark into the clay, and who'd sent her down to eat from it first, knowing she would.

Her colour moved once, at the throat, very dark, and she held it.

Imani wrote on a card. S. The taster. Ate from all six at 7.50. Down at the second bell about 8.50. Head in the sea; gills; kept alive. Z's two dishes held at the head, uncarded. The house sent her. She looked at the last four words and left them.



Zale went up to the market at three, because his crew were out of salt.

That was all it was. He said so. He came up the stages with the morning still on him, grey, with his arm held in, and Imani on the last stair told him about the dish and the card and Sarrou, all of it, and he listened with his face doing nothing, and at the end of it he said, "Thank you," and then he said, "They're out of salt. And rope. Hesk says the good rope's at the market stairs. I'll go and get it."

"Somebody tried to poison you this morning," said Imani.

"Somebody tried to drown me on Tuesday," said Zale. "I'd still like some rope."

Two of Voll's knights went with him, in their blues, with their rifles on the body, as they had every time he'd set foot on the Rim since the slate. A big quiet one off escort one whose name she'd never caught, and a younger one. They fell in behind him on the landing without being asked.

And Ilenne came.

She'd come up the stages behind him. She stood on the landing in the afternoon light with the sea still running off her, and she had a bident in her hand: a guard's bident, long-hafted, with a dull black head, that she'd taken from her mother's door guard on Tuesday night when she came home from the salvage quay and had carried since. She hadn't asked anyone. Imani had seen her with it on Wednesday and decided it wasn't her business.

"I'm coming," said Ilenne.

Zale looked at her.

"You don't have to," he said.

"I know." Her colour was very carefully neutral. Not the pale polite green. Nothing. "Not without me. On the Rim. Not this week." She looked at the knights, and away. "That's all."

It wasn't all. Imani saw that it wasn't. It was the nearest Ilenne could get to sorry, standing on a landing with a borrowed bident and her colour held flat, and Zale saw it too; he looked at her for a moment longer, and his face did something it hadn't done for a week, and then he nodded and turned and went down the street toward the market, and she walked beside him, a foot off.

Imani went too. Ilenne was her patient. And she'd stopped trusting anybody's food.



The market stairs went down from the street to the water beside the salvage quay, a broad flight of black steps, worn hollow in the middle, with the sea at the bottom and stalls all the way down on both sides: rope, salt, fish, lamp oil, cord, shell, the dried dark fruit, a man with hooks laid out on a cloth. It was crowded at three. It was always crowded. People on their legs going up and down with baskets, and children, and at the bottom the boats coming in to the lowest step with their catches, and the noise of it all coming up off the stone like heat.

Zale found his rope. The good rope was halfway down on the left, at a stall with coils of the pale cord stacked as high as a man, and the stallholder knew him, or knew of him; she watched him come down with the two land knights at his back and the queen's daughter at his side with a bident, and decided, Imani saw, to sell him rope anyway.

He was bargaining. Badly. Imani was three steps above him with her bag on her shoulder, watching the crowd, because she'd started watching crowds. The knights were one step above him, either side. Ilenne was at his left hand.

The man came from below.

He came up the stairs out of the crowd at the water's edge, one of a dozen people coming up, a Rim man on his legs in a working coat, ordinary, with a basket on his arm; and two steps below Zale he dropped the basket and there was a knife in his hand, and he came up the last two steps at Zale's ribs with it, fast, low, and nobody had time to do anything at all.

The charm caught it.

Imani saw it happen and didn't understand it until afterwards. Zale was wearing the guard charm on its cord under his shirt, the one Ilenne's house had given him, for the knife you don't see; and the knife came at his ribs faster than anything ought to come at anyone, and the air in front of his side went hard. She saw it. A flat white flare, small as a hand, where the blade met it. The knife skidded off it sideways, as off glass, and went through the cloth of Zale's sleeve instead of into him and out the other side, and the man's hand went past Zale's body with the knife in it and his weight carried him into Zale's chest, and they went back together against the rope.

The big knight fired.

He fired from one step up, at a yard, a ball round, into the man's back as he pulled the knife free of the sleeve. And the man's own charm took it.

A crack, loud as a slammed door. A flash, white, off the man's back where the round met the air an inch from his coat. And the man still standing, staggering, turning, with the knife in his hand and his face gone blank with shock.

He was wearing one too. Under his coat. A ward, on a cord, the same as Zale's.

The big knight fired again. Crack, flash. The younger knight fired. Crack, flash, and this time the flash was dimmer, a guttering, and the man screamed, though nothing had touched him, and the charm on him was empty; Imani saw his hand go to his chest where it hung, and come away. Three rounds. That was what it had held. And the man knew it, and turned and went down the stairs in two long bounds through the crowd, which opened in front of him screaming, and off the bottom step into the sea.

The water closed over him.

Ilenne went in after him.

Imani saw her go. She went off the step into the sea with the bident in her hand and she was a tail before she was under, and then something happened to the water that Imani had never seen happen to water.

It boiled.

Not hot. A long white tear in the surface, straight as a ruled line, going out from the foot of the stairs into the harbour, with a sound like a kettle the size of a house and steam coming off it in a sheet, and at the head of it, in a sheath of white water, something going so fast that Imani's eye couldn't hold it. Out. Straight. A hundred yards, two hundred, in the time it took her to stand up on the step; and she saw the white line bend, once, sharply, and stop.

The noise stopped.

The steam drifted off the harbour in the afternoon light. Out at the end of the white line the water was quite still.

Then Ilenne's head came up, a long way out. And beside her, something else.



She brought him back to the stairs herself, towing him, on her tail, slowly, with the bident through him.

It had gone in under the arm and out through the chest. Imani was at the water's edge on the lowest step with her bag open before Ilenne had reached it, and she took him off the bident and onto the step with the help of the stallholder from the rope stall, who'd come down without being asked, and laid him out on the wet stone, and put her hands on him and looked.

She didn't have to look long. She knew it before she'd opened her sight, from the colour of his lips and the way the blood was coming, and she opened it anyway because that was what she did, and she gave him what she could, and felt it go into him and not stay. The bident had gone through everything that mattered. Healing had a rate. This was faster than any rate there was.

Ilenne was in the water at the step's edge beside her. Imani glanced up at her once, and saw the skin of Ilenne's shoulders and arms where they came out of the sea: covered in something. A film. Clear and faintly shining, as if she'd been oiled all over, and thick enough that the water ran off it in beads and didn't wet her, and under it her colour was moving very fast, dark and light and dark.

The slick, Imani thought. She named it to herself the way she'd name a symptom on a card, without deciding to. It was a skin on the skin, laid down for speed; she'd heard Ilenne speak of the body and of the asking on the water, and she hadn't heard of this, and here it was in front of her, plain as a rash. The slick. She'd write it down later.

The man's lips moved. Imani bent close. Nothing came out. His throat had no colour on it at all.

He died under her hands at twenty past three, on the lowest step of the market stairs, with his blood going into the sea.



She was still kneeling over him when the crowd came down.

It came from above, down the stairs, all at once: the market's people, who'd scattered up the steps when the knife came out and the guns went off, coming back down them now, toward the bottom, toward the land men. Not running. Walking, fast, close together, dozens of them, a wall of bodies on the stair from one side to the other. Shouting. Imani didn't know the words. She knew the sound. They weren't coming to kill anyone. They were coming for the dead man on the step, a Rim man lying in his own blood at the bottom of their market with two land knights standing over him and their guns still smoking, and they didn't know or care who'd put the bident in him.

The knights changed magazines.

Imani heard them do it. She'd heard Mira drill it on the K Amara's apron till everyone could do it with their eyes shut: the hand going down to the left hip, feeling the base plates, two notches, out, in. Baton. The big knight said something short to the younger one, and the younger one's hand went down and felt and came up with a magazine whose base had three notches. Stun.

"Legs and belly," he said, loud. "Never above the belt."

They fired into the front of the crowd at the knees.

The baton rounds went off with a flat cough, nothing like the crack of the ball, and the people in the front row on the stair went down in a heap, holding their legs, howling; and the stun darts went in with a snap and a man doubled over with his whole body jerking. The crowd stopped. The front of it fell back onto the ones behind. Imani counted without meaning to: four coughs, two snaps.

And then a boy screamed. A high, thin, terrible scream, a child's.

He was in the crowd's front row on the left side of the stair, where it was pressed hardest against the stalls, and he was down on the steps curled round himself, and he was small. Much smaller than the people round him. He'd been pushed forward by the press from behind until he was at the very front, close, much too close, and one of the baton rounds had taken him in the side at about fifteen yards.

Inside twenty. Ward's rule was never inside twenty.

Imani was up the stairs before anybody could stop her, with her bag. She went through the knights and through the front of the crowd as if neither were there, and nobody touched her, and she knelt down on the step beside the boy and put her hands on his side.

Two ribs. Broken, not shattered. She could feel the ends grate under her palm. A bruise already coming up under them, black, the size of the round's rubber cap. His lung was all right. She looked, to be sure, and it was. He was twelve, or near it, thin, with a cut lip from the step and his eyes screwed shut and both hands clenched in his shirt.

"I've got you," said Imani, in Kaldo, which he couldn't understand. "I've got you. Lie still. It's ribs. It's only ribs. You'll live." She put her hands flat on the break and gave him what she could, and held the ends of the bone still while it started, and the boy's breathing changed, and slowed, and he opened his eyes and looked at her.

A woman had come out of the crowd and was kneeling on his other side, holding his head, crying. His mother, Imani supposed. She didn't look at Imani. She looked at the boy.

Nimo. That was his name; Imani heard the woman say it, over and over, and wrote it on her card later. Nimo, of the rope stalls, twelve.

Behind her the crowd had gone quiet. Not gone. Quiet. They stood on the stair in a mass from wall to wall, with their hurt in the front of them on the steps, and looked at the land healer on her knees with her hands on a Rim boy's ribs, and at the land knights with their guns, and at the dead man on the bottom step, and at the queen's daughter standing in the sea at the foot of the stairs, out of the water to the waist, with her colour gone dark from the throat down and a bloody bident in her hand.

Imani didn't know what any of them were thinking. She didn't look up to find out.



Ferra came at five, from the pilots' hall, and paid.

Imani was there; she'd gone up to the house above the rope stalls to look at the boy again before dark. Ferra paid Nimo's mother at the door, in iron, out of the expedition chest, with the watch officer standing by. She counted it, and said the amount aloud, and the woman took it without looking at it, and Ferra wrote the name in the book: Nimo, with his age and the ribs and Imani's word that the lung was clear. The four with bruised legs and the man who'd taken the stun had gone home. Ferra asked the watch officer to write their names on his slate, and he did, and she copied them under Nimo's.

The watch had the dead man's name by six, cut on a slate at the pilots' hall: Orsaule. A knife for hire, the officer said, from nowhere anybody on the stairs knew. Nobody had seen him in the market before.

Imani had the rounds for her, from the big knight, because Ferra would ask. Three ball. Four baton. Two stun. Nine. Ferra wrote that too.

By dusk the story was all over the Rim.

Imani heard it on the landing, and at the station, and from Linnet, who'd had it from Hesk, who'd had it from the woman at the rope stall, who'd been there: the land's guns had killed a man in the market. That was the story. Land knights with land guns had fired in the market stairs in the middle of the afternoon, and a Rim man was dead at the bottom of them, and a Rim boy had his ribs broken, and four more were limping, and a man had been shocked half to death with something out of a land gun that crawled into him like a wasp.

That the man had come at the queen's daughter's husband with a knife travelled some of the way. That the queen's daughter's own bident had killed him, in the water, in front of half the market, travelled less far than that. Imani could feel it not travelling. By the time the watch-fires came up along the breakwater it had been left behind somewhere on the causeways, and what was going round the Rim's kitchens and kiln yards was the land, and its guns, and the boy.

Ferra stood on the landing with the book shut under her arm and listened to Linnet tell it.

"It's what they wanted," said Ferra.

"Who?"

"Whoever sent a man with a ward on into a market to stick a knife into someone who'd a ward on." Ferra looked out at the harbour, where the white line of the boil had been. "He wasn't meant to kill Zale. He was meant to make land knights fire in a crowd. And they did, because they had to, and now it's ours." She turned the book over under her arm. "Twice in a day. The dish and the knife. And the knife got what it came for."



The station was quiet at ten.

Imani went back to it after the meal she didn't eat, because Sarrou was there, and Nimo was there, both of them on pallets in the long back room with the lamps turned low, and she'd told herself she'd look at them both once more before she slept.

Nimo was asleep, on his back, with his mother asleep sitting up beside him with her head against the wall. Imani looked at the ribs without waking him. They'd started. It would be weeks.

Sarrou was awake.

She was lying on her side as before, under the weed blanket, with her colour come back almost to amber, and her eyes were open, and she watched Imani come down the room.

Imani sat down on the floor beside her pallet.

They didn't have much between them. Sarrou had a little Kaldo, the house's servants' Kaldo, Imani supposed, a few dozen words for food and thanks and the time of day, learned for the land husband's baskets; and Imani had the station's words, the ones for breathe and hurt and here and sleep, and her hands. It was enough for what Sarrou wanted to say. She'd been waiting to say it, Imani thought.

"I serve," said Sarrou. She said it slowly. "The house. I serve."

"I know."

"I know," said Sarrou, "what it did." She touched her own mouth, and then her chest. "This. Me." Her colour moved, very dark, and was held. "I know."

Imani waited.

"I don't know," said Sarrou, "if I say." She looked at the ceiling. "To the watch. To the court." She thought for a while, hunting for a word, and didn't find it, and used her hands instead: one hand flat, palm up, and the other coming down on it and lifting, coming down and lifting. Weighing. "I don't know yet."

"You don't have to know tonight," said Imani.

Sarrou looked at her.

"I'm only saying," said Imani. "You nearly died this morning. You don't have to decide anything tonight. Sleep." She put her hand flat on the blanket over Sarrou's shoulder, the station's word, and the woman's eyes closed.

Imani sat there a while longer.

Then she took a card out of her coat, and her pencil, and wrote on it in her small square hand, resting it on her knee in the low lamplight, so that it would be on a card and not only in her head.

S. awake 10 pm. Knows what the house did. Hasn't decided whether to say. Don't push.
