Book 2, Chapter 1: The Wall Water

They put the third relay over the side at ten to two on the Monday morning, five miles up over the wall water, on the convoy's charging climb, because Ferra's sheet said every third night for the racks and the racks agreed with her.

It had taken them five days and a half to get that far. The K Tam had done it in two, in March, with her sister for company and nobody to wait for. Eight ships went at the pace of the slowest of them and came down on the sea at every dusk, so that eight hundred and thirty people could sleep at the mark Imani had set in their cabins, and by the Friday Emeric had given up working out when they'd arrive and started asking Sorrel, who told him the same thing each time. When we're there.

The second relay had gone over on the Saturday night, halfway between the first and the wall water, from the K Discovery alone on a short climb with the convoy asleep on the sea below her. Ferra had done the sum on the Thursday, with Amara's green lamp still fresh: two drums could hear each other across four hundred miles and not much more, and the wall water was a good deal more than four hundred miles from the first. So the K Discovery had gone up by herself for an hour, for the drum and not to charge, which she wouldn't be allowed to do again once they were past the wall, and come down with thirteen in the hold.

They'd come to the wall water at the Sunday's sunset and stood off, and then gone up in the dark, all eight, with their necks open. He could hear the racks filling from the forward deck. It wasn't a sound exactly. The whisper of the louvres had something under it now, a long even pull, like a tide going out through shingle a great way off.

The relay went out on its arm from the waist hatch, a grey drum the size of a rain barrel, and hung on its own stones while two of Sorrel's hands unshipped the arm from it and brought the arm in. Then there was a yard of night between it and the hull. Then ten. Sorrel eased the K Discovery off it on quarter veins, the way you'd back out of a room where somebody was sleeping, and it stayed where it had been put, with the stars over it and the cloud a long way under, getting smaller.

He had his sense laid out along it, and waited. This was the part he always waited for. The thing beat to itself for a while, small and regular, as it had beaten in the hold since the day they'd woken all fifteen. Then, at a moment of its own choosing, it beat to something else. Somewhere east of them in the dark the chain heard it and said so, faint, and he felt the answer go back.

"That's it talking," said Sorrel, from the wheel. She hadn't turned round.

"That's it talking."

"Twelve," she said. She liked to have the number said. She'd told him on the second day out that a scout who didn't know what was in her own hold wasn't one.

"Twelve."

He stayed at the rail longer than he needed to. A drum that talked could be heard, by its friends and by anybody else who'd thought to listen. Saban had said so in September, more than once. Emeric thought it now, and went below.

At four the racks were full. The eight of them came down out of the Band together, slowly, with the cold coming off the hulls in sheets, and were on the swell again before it was light.



Ilenne had given Zale the bearing herself. It wasn't the one they'd flown in March. That had been the nearest water that would carry her voice. This was the road, she said, and when Zale asked her what the difference was she'd told him, patiently, that one was where you shouted from and the other was where you went in.

So they lifted at first light and ran south-west for three hours down the edge of the wall water, over the deep heavy blue Emeric remembered, with the feel of it rising in him steadily. He knew this part. He'd swum in it. Zale's ship had stood off in it twice. Ilenne, on Zale's foredeck a quarter of a mile to starboard, had been brightening since the sun came up, and had her hood pulled forward over it.

At a little after nine Zale's voice came out of the set by the wheel.

"She says there. Dead ahead. She says you'll know."

He was going to ask how, and then he saw it.

There was a line on the sea. It ran away north and south as far as he could follow it, a long crooked seam with weed along it and a thread of foam, the sort of line a river makes where it comes out into a bay. On the near side of it the water was blue. On the far side it was another blue altogether, darker and slower, with a swell that didn't match. It was perhaps three hundred yards ahead. The K Discovery went over it at a walk.

It felt good.

That was the first thing he noticed about it, and he didn't trust it for a second. The sea had been loud in March. This was the same room with every door thrown open. It came up through the hull and the deck and his boots, and in at his eyes and with his breath, a pressure on the sense he never shut, from every side at once and from below most of all. And it didn't hurt. It was like coming in out of a hard frost to a kitchen with the range lit. Everything in him that the stone had rebuilt leaned toward it, and he stood at the rail and let it, and thought: nothing that's free feels like this.

The stones in his coat had gone warm against his ribs. He put a hand in the left pocket. One of them was warm enough to notice.

Behind him one of the hands said "Oh," not loudly, and sat down on the hatch cover.

"Sorrel."

"I know." Her voice was level. He looked round. She had both hands on the wheel and her eyes on the binnacle, and she kept them there for the length of three long swells before she let her shoulders down. "Compass is fine," she said. "It's fine. It hasn't moved." She breathed out through her nose. "The veins want less than I'm giving them. A good deal less. She's trying to climb."

"Give her less."

"I'm giving her less. You wanted to know what she asked for."

The air had changed with the water. It was heavier in the chest, and there was a sweetness in it, faint, that he couldn't place and stopped trying to. Sound had changed too. The K Amara was the best part of a mile astern, coming up to the line, and he could hear a man on her forward apron laugh, and a hatch go down, as plainly as across a yard. And the light. He looked at the back of his own hand on the rail. It was the same hand. The brown of it was a shade deeper, and the brass under it a shade more like brass, and out over the bow the sea was the colour things went in Kaldo for an hour after rain and never kept.

"She says that's the mark," said Zale. "Off your port bow. Do you see it? I don't see it. No. I've got it."

It stood up out of the sea a mile ahead, on nothing. A pillar, grey-white, as tall as the light on Kaldo's sea wall, with the swell breaking round its foot on a shoal that didn't otherwise show. The top of it was set all round with glass, in colours, a red and a deep green and a yellow like honey, and the morning was in the glass.

"It's for pilots," said Zale. There was a pause, and a murmur under it. "It's where ships wait. You come to it, and you wait, and somebody comes out and fetches you in." Another pause, longer. When he spoke again he'd dropped his voice. "She says nobody's waited at it in a long time. Longer than the songs go back. She says she didn't know if it'd still be standing."

Emeric looked at the glass as they came up on it. There wasn't a streak on it. No weed, no salt, no bird had been let sit there. In all the years of nobody coming, somebody had gone on climbing that pillar with a cloth.

"Bring her down beside it," he said. "We'll wait."



He went across to the K Amara at ten, by air, because the convoy was coming down round the mark a hull at a time and it was quicker than a boat. That was different too. He'd flown bare over the bay at home often enough, and it had never once felt as though the air wanted him to. He landed on the starboard apron harder than he'd meant, with a great deal more left in him than he'd allowed for, and Ward, who was standing at the after end of it with his hands behind his back, watched him do it and passed no remark.

The K Amara's knights were on the aprons in their blues, in ranks, and something was the matter with all of them. You wouldn't have seen it from another ship. A man rolled his shoulders. Another opened and shut his hands. Down the front rank they stood too straight, and when Emeric turned his sense on them he understood it: every one of them was lit. The knight's way, the held heat they kept themselves warm with on a winter deck, was coming up in them unasked.

"How long?" he said.

"Since the line. Forty minutes." Ward didn't take his eyes off them. "I gave it till now to see if it'd level. It hasn't."

"Then..."

"Then now." Ward raised his voice, not much. He'd never needed much. "Hold it and burn it. As drilled. Hold, and open. You've done it at the foot since the autumn and you'll do it here until I say stop, and I'll not be saying stop today. Any man goes heavy, any man yawns, he says so, and he goes below and lies down at the mark. If he won't say it, the man beside him says it for him. Begin."

It went down the ranks. He felt them open, one after another, heat going out of men into nothing at all, steadily, on purpose, and the too-straight backs eased a rank at a time.

Third from the end of the front rank, Mira's breath caught halfway in. She stopped. She looked down at her own chest with real suspicion, as if it had spoken out of turn, and then she laughed, once, short, and got on with it.

"That's a first," said Bren, beside her, out of the side of his mouth.

"Eyes front," said Arthur, on her other side, who hadn't moved at all.

Ferra came down the ladder from the bridge deck with the book under her arm and a slip in her hand. She'd been at the chart table since the line; he'd felt her there, faintly, the whole way across, the same warmth in her that was in him, and her distrust of it sitting right beside his own.

"Noll," she said. "By the K Endeavor's set. He says, and I'm to say it exactly, that he hasn't touched them." She gave him the slip. "The test rack. All six dials. They've been creeping since we crossed. At sea level, with nothing open."

He read it. Six small figures, taken ten minutes apart, each a hair above the last.

"That's the air filling them," he said. "By itself."

"That's what he says."

"That's..." He stopped, because he'd been about to tell her what it was worth, and he could see by her face that she'd already priced it.

"If it holds, and if the big racks take it the way six test stones do, which nobody knows, it's charge we didn't climb for." She took the slip back. "I'm not putting it in the column."

"No."

"I'm putting it in the margin. In pencil."

"And I'm not counting on it at all," said Ward, without turning his head, "so you can put that in the margin with it."

She did. He watched her write W.: not counted on under the six figures in a hand a size smaller than her own, which was how she wrote down things she agreed with and wished she didn't.

Up on the bridge deck Armand was asleep in the chair in the corner with both hands on the head of his cane, and opened one eye long enough to say he was thinking, not sleeping, and shut it again.

By eleven the K Amara was on the water with her sea anchors out, two hundred yards from the mark, and the swell was lifting her and letting her go in long slow heaves that took longer than a swell ought to. It was a heavier sea. You could see it in how it moved. A deckhand hauling a bucket up the side for the tender swore, and got it over the rail anyhow, and held his hands out from his sides, and Emeric saw why: they were coming up red from the wrist down, in a rash of pinpricks. He put his own hand in the bucket. It didn't sting him at all.

"Gloves," he said. "For anybody who hasn't a stone. Pass it to the other hulls. And tell Imani before she hears it from somebody's hands."

"She's told us," said the boatswain. "Twice. He wasn't listening."

The boots were Bren's fault again.

Emeric had meant to try a pair himself, off the tender, quietly. He'd said in March that past the wall the chip would bite twice as hard, and that they'd want retuning, and that somebody would get thrown the first morning if they weren't. He had said it wet, and out of breath, to Mira. He remembered her answer. So did everybody else, which was why, when Ward stood the first rank down at half past eleven to eat, there were forty knights at the rail over the tender and Bren already in it with the boots laced and the collar round his neck.

"I've done eighty yards in these," said Bren.

"You've done eighty yards in the lee of an island," said Emeric. "This isn't that water. Put your foot down flat and take it up straight, and don't lean, and if it holds you, stand still and tell me what it..."

Bren stepped off the gunwale.

His left boot went onto the sea and stayed there. It didn't sink an inch. The chip took the water under his heel and pinned it the way it would have pinned a paving stone, and the rest of Bren, which had been expecting to go in to the knee and come up running, went on over the top of his own foot at the speed he'd stepped out, and came down full length on his face. It didn't sound like a man falling into the sea. It sounded like a man falling onto a wet mattress. His boot let go a breath later and he went under, and the collar brought him up, and he lay on his back in the swell with his arms out, looking at the sky.

Nobody on the rail made a sound for about as long as it took to be sure he was breathing. Then all forty of them did.

"Somebody was Bren," said Mira.

"I heard that," said Bren, to the sky.

Under him, in water as clear as gin, a fish had come up to look. It was the shape of a bream and the length of the tender's oar, and it hung there for a time with one flat eye the colour of pewter turned up at him, and then it went down again without any hurry, having seen what it came to see.

"Hull four," said Emeric. "All of them. Every pair." He'd had his sense on the chip when it took the water, and it had bitten twice as hard as it did in the bay, which was the figure he'd given in March and the one he'd have liked to be wrong about. "Tell Bram the pin wants halving and the push wants bringing forward, and tell him I said so before he tells me. Nobody steps off in them till they're back."

"And when they're back," said Ward, "the first man over goes on a line."

The books were out by then, the oilcloth ones with a hull and a number stamped on the front, on every deck in the convoy. Arthur sat on a bollard with his on his knee, drawing the mark and getting the glass wrong, because the light out here was fuller than any he'd learned his colours in. Mira had the spear off the rack and was showing Maeve a turn at the rail, and Maeve was bad at it and didn't mind, and rapped the rail with the butt every time.

It was the hands without a stone who showed the rest of it. By noon the watch on deck were moving as if the planks had been tilted against them, and yawning, one after another, down the whole length of the rail, and not one of them could have said why.



He rowed himself over to Zale's ship at dusk. He could have flown it. He found he'd had enough for one day of the air being glad to see him.

The unchanged had gone below to the mark an hour before, hull by hull, yawning; he'd watched the lamps come on behind the cabin ports all down the line. The knights were still on the aprons, a watch at a time, bleeding it off into the evening. The sea anchors were out. Eight ships rode in a loose string to the north of the pillar with their riding lights lit, and the pillar's glass held the last of the sun after the sea had lost it.

Zale was at the lee rail with a pot of something and Hesk was on an upturned bucket beside him, splicing, and screwing up his eyes at the work. Ilenne stood a little way along from them with her cloak round her and her hood up. She'd kept it up since morning. Under it, at the edge of her jaw, where she couldn't quite keep it in, she was the colour of the inside of a shell with a lamp behind it.

"You're holding it," said Emeric.

"I am holding." She said it shortly. "Not home. Not yet. I shine now, I shine all the way there. It is not," and she looked for the word, and found one of Ferra's, "dignified."

"She's been like a lighthouse with a sack over it since breakfast," said Zale. "I've told her nobody minds."

"I mind."

"I know you do. I said nobody else."

Emeric leaned on the rail between them. He could feel nothing under the swell. His sense went out a boat's length into that crowded water and came back full of the water and nothing else, and somewhere under the keel there was a mile of it.

"Tomorrow," he said. "You call it at first light."

"At first light. The sea talks then." She looked west, where the last of the day was. "It comes. It is not happy. It waits for us since my mother counted, and it does not like to wait, and it does not like this water even. For it this is thin." She held finger and thumb a hair apart. "Soup with nothing in."

"And then it takes us in."

"It goes in front. So the deep ones know whose we are."

Hesk had stopped splicing. He had sailed with Zale on the coasters, and he'd been aboard the one time this ship had gone past the wall before, a little way, and had stood at the top of that same ladder with a line round his captain's chest while the sea got up under them. He said, to the rope, "There's a thing they say on the islands. On the beach. When a boat goes west."

"I know what they say," said Zale.

"Past the last island the water gets thick." Hesk pulled a strand through. "And then it gets hungry."

Ilenne turned her head. She had the saying; Zale had given it to her in the winter, and she'd laughed, and then asked him to say it again slowly.

"Yes," she said. "That is true. Both."

"And the old one's for the hungry," said Emeric.

"The old one swims in front and speaks to the deep things. It says, these are the queen's, go away. And they go." She held her cloak closed at the throat. "Most."

The swell came up the side, and went down.

"Most," said Zale.

"Most things, they hear a queen's house, they give room. I am telling you true. You said always tell it before." She said that to Emeric, with her chin up. "So. Most."

Hesk went back to his splice. Zale drank what was in the pot. Nobody asked what the rest did.

He didn't row back.



She went down the ladder at twenty past five on the Tuesday with the east just going grey.

He'd slept on the locker in Zale's cabin, under a coat that smelled of tar, because he wanted to be on this deck at first light and not a quarter of a mile off it with a glass. He'd told Sorrel so on the set. Sorrel had said the K Discovery could find her own way to a slick, thank you, and that she'd have the veins warm.

Hesk put the ladder over the lee side, weighted at the foot. Ilenne gave Zale the cloak. She went down hand under hand, and her legs went in the first yard of sea, and she hooked an arm through the last rung and put her face in the water.

It wasn't like March. In March she'd been under for half an hour. This was short. Her throat worked, twice, and a colour went down her back that Emeric had no name for and Imani would have, dark, with a shine on it, and then she took her face out and hung on the rung with her tail moving and waited.

He felt it before anything showed.

He'd had the sea in his sense for a day now as a single thing, a noise so even it had stopped being a noise. Off to the west the noise changed. Something was coming through it that was so much more than the water round it that the water seemed to lean away, and he couldn't read it. His sense went out toward it and stopped dead. It had no edges. It had a direction, and a size, and the size was wrong.

"West," he said. "Low."

Zale was already looking. "I've nothing. I've swell." A breath. "No. That."

A piece of the sea half a mile off had gone smooth. It was longer than any ship they had. The swells came up to it and went flat and crossed it without a mark and stood up again on the far side, and it was coming toward the ladder at about the pace of a man walking fast. Hesk, who had seen one of these before, went back a step from the rail and then forward again, and held the ladder's head in both fists.

A hundred yards out it stopped. Then it came up.

Not all of it; he never saw all of it. A length of back broke the surface, grey as the pillar and pitted like it, with the water sheeting off, and a second length broke a full hull astern of the first, and between the two there was only smooth sea with something under it. The swell it had pushed ahead of it reached them a moment later and Zale's ship went up on it and over and down, and everything loose on the deck slid a foot and came back. He put the K Amara beside it in his head, bow to stern, and then put her there again. Then the smell came. Low tide. Old harbour wall. A cellar the sea had got into.

Ilenne had let go of the ladder.

She was in the water between the ship and the thing, small, upright, with her tail working under her, and she was going through colours faster than he'd ever seen her, at the throat and down both arms. It didn't answer in colour. If it answered at all he couldn't tell how. But she stopped twice as though she'd been interrupted, and once she drew herself up in the water and said something to it with her whole body that he didn't need a card to read: she was being her mother's daughter at it.

"She's winning," said Hesk, low.

"She's arguing," said Zale. "It's not the same." He hadn't taken his eyes off her. His hand came off the rail an inch, and went back.

When she climbed out her legs were back before she reached the rail, and she was so bright Emeric had to look at the side of her face instead of the front. She didn't take the cloak. She stood dripping on the planks and talked to Zale fast and low in her own tongue, and Zale listened with his head down, and twice he said a word back and she corrected it.

"Right," said Zale. He turned round. "I'm to give you this in order. One. It knows whose we are. It was told in March and it's been off that shoal since, and it wants that understood. Two." She said something. "Two, the ships smell of the dry."

"We stink," said Emeric.

"Dry is a smell," said Ilenne, in Kaldo, before Zale could. "Tar. Iron. Smoke. Your paint, under." She pointed down, at the hull. "It does not like. It does not go under you. Any of you. Never."

"So if a man goes over the side," said Zale, "he's ours to fish for. That one's from me. Three. It goes in front. A mile, or what it thinks a mile is. We go where it goes and no faster."

The set by the binnacle clicked. "And if it goes somewhere we'd rather it didn't?" That was Ward, on the K Amara. He'd been listening since Emeric said west.

Zale looked at Ilenne. Ilenne looked at the set.

"It is my mother's," she said, loud enough for it. "She tells it: bring my daughter and her guests to the door. It brings. It does not hurt you." She stopped. "It does not ask which way you like."

"That's a great deal of ship to hang on what a fish was told in March," said Ward.

"It is," said Emeric. "Say it again at the table tonight and I'll say you're right there too." He took the handset. "Sorrel. When it moves, you follow it. A mile. Not nine-tenths."

"Following a fish," said Sorrel. "At a mile." She said it without any expression at all.

He was back on the K Discovery's forward deck before six, by air, the short way. At six the back went under and the smooth place drew out long and went away west by south, and Sorrel lifted and went after it at a few hundred feet, and behind her the seven others came up off the water one by one and fell in.

It was slow. He'd thought a thing that size would go like a ship; it went like a big animal with the whole day in front of it, twelve knots, fourteen, and the K Discovery had to be held back on a fraction of her veins to keep the mile, wallowing. By eight Sorrel had stopped looking at her own heading and was steering on the slick.

At ten, over open water with nothing in it, it bent hard to the south and rolled as it bent, so that for a breath it turned its pale underside to the K Discovery and its back to a stretch of sea off to the north-west, and it held the turn for four miles before it came round onto its line again. Sorrel had the glass on the place the whole way past. He had his sense on it, with everything he had. It was water. Zale asked Ilenne, and Ilenne asked the old one, and it didn't answer, and she didn't seem to have expected it to.



Linnet Shaw's nose had started at four on the Monday afternoon, and Imani heard about it at ten to midnight, which she thought about afterwards as the entire difficulty in one sentence.

It was Hesk who'd found her. He'd gone to the radio cabin for the hour, because Zale's ship kept no clock he trusted but the one over the set, and found the door shut and the key silent and Linnet at the table with her head tipped right back and a rag held to her face, and on the open log, across the eight o'clock line, three round drops that had dried brown. He'd had the sense to row for the K Endeavor himself and not shout it across the water.

Imani tipped Linnet's head forward first. They always tipped it back. She pinched the soft of the nose and held it and counted, and with her other hand turned the lamp up, and opened her sight the width of a hair and looked: the small vessels at the front, swollen, weeping, nothing behind them, nothing in the chest. First stage. She'd seen hundreds of them at the foot of the Band and she could have drawn it.

"When?" she said.

"Just now," said Linnet, through the rag.

"That's dry on the log. Try again."

A silence. Linnet had been Armand's student before most of this fleet had been built, and she had held a mile of sky off Milford in the locust year, and none of it had kept her nose from bleeding at the foot of the Band. She'd sat it every Tuesday for seven months and bled late, exactly as her card said she would. She was also the only person on this ship who could send Ferra's cipher fast and clean.

"Four," she said. "It was only a bit. It stopped. Then it didn't."

"And the eight o'clock?"

Linnet's eyes went to the log, and then to the slip pinned beside the key, in Ferra's square hand, which the king had brought across in his pocket at dusk. Mon 20 Apr. Past the wall 09.10. All eight. All well. Mark sighted and stood by. 12. Half of it was copied out on the pad and struck through in pencil, where she'd started to send and stopped.

"I couldn't keep it off the key," she said. "It kept going on the key."

So the report that said all well had sat four hours on a table because the one person who could send it was bleeding on it. Imani let that go by. It would keep, and it would be funnier in a month.

"Can you send it now? Sitting up. I hold your nose and you send."

"Yes."

"Then send it. Put the hour on it. The real one."

It took six minutes. Imani stood behind the chair in a cabin the size of a cupboard with Linnet's nose between her finger and thumb while Linnet's right hand rattled out the king's business, and Zale stood in the doorway in his shirt with the lamp and said nothing, which for Zale was an effort you could hear.

The answer came back before she'd let go. Linnet took it down left-handed and didn't need the book for it. She turned the pad round.

A.: Four hours. Why?

Imani read it twice. She knew whose question that was. Saban would have asked what, and Ambrose wouldn't have asked at all. She thought of a woman in dark plate at the end of a long desk, who heard every drum on the chain, and who had sat from eight until midnight with all of them beating and no word on any of them.

Two doors down, on the locker in Zale's cabin, the king was asleep under Zale's coat. She'd seen him on her way in, with one hand flat on his own chest, the way he slept after a day of being careful. She could wake him and give him this. It would cost him the night, and his mother would hear from him what she was about to hear from Imani, ten minutes later. She let him sleep.

"Give me the pencil," she said.

She wrote it herself and had Linnet send it as written. I.: Operator's nose, first stage. She hid it. My fault for not asking twice. All eight well. He is asleep. Ask me next time, not her.

Then she took Linnet's card out of her own breast pocket, where she'd put it when Hesk came up the side, and laid it on the log, on the dry part, and made a dot on it for the Monday.

"You're off the night watch for a week," she said.

That did what the blood hadn't. Linnet came half out of the chair. "That's the watch that talks to home. That's the only one that's anything. The day's all hull to hull, it's Sorrel saying she's turned, a child could..."

"It's at night that you're meant to be at the mark, lying down, and this cabin isn't at the mark. I should have seen that before we sailed and I didn't. That's two of us who got it wrong." Imani capped the pencil. "A week. Days only. You'll hate it."

"Then who sends?" said Zale, from the door. He said it lightly. He was watching her, though, and not lightly at all. "I've four hands and a mermaid, Imani. I'm not arguing with the card. I'm asking who keys the night set on my ship, because that's the one that talks to home."

"The K Tam's operator can take your traffic."

"Nell Haddow's operator sends like a man knocking in a fence post. Ferra'll have it back to him corrected before he's signed off." He rubbed his jaw. "I'll do it."

"You can't key."

"I can key a bit. Enough for her to be rude about." He looked at Linnet, who was looking at him in open horror. "It's a week. You'll teach me in the afternoons, and you'll be rude about it, and I'll get better out of spite."

"Routine, then," said Imani. "Anything that can't wait for you to find the letters goes to the K Tam, fence post or not. I'll put it on Ferra's slip."

"Fine."

It still wasn't the answer she'd have picked. It was his ship, though, and with the K Tam behind it she had nothing left to put against it but a preference, and she left it where it was.

She was back on her own deck by one, and she didn't sleep. She sat at the end of the hospital deck's long table with the cigar box and wrote the rule out eight times, once for each hull, in block capitals a rigger could read by a bad lamp, and Bettany, who'd woken when the tender bumped, and who said the air was one glass of wine and no more, sat down across from her without being asked and ruled the lines.

A NOSE IS REPORTED. TO THE SURGEON OR TO HER MATE, AT ONCE, BY WHOEVER SEES IT. REPORT IT AND YOU LIE DOWN AT THE MARK UNTIL YOUR CARD SAYS UP. HIDE IT AND YOU ARE OFF WATCH TWO DAYS FROM WHEN IT IS FOUND, AND YOU LIE DOWN AT THE MARK ANYWAY. IMANI, SURGEON.

"They'll say it's a punishment," said Bettany.

"It's two days' sleep. It's the kindest thing on the sheet." Imani blotted the last one. "It's a punishment for being clever. I don't mind that. I've had seven months of them being brave at me."

The rule went round by boat before it was light, and was read out on every hull at the change of the watch, an hour before the old one came. By the Tuesday noon she'd had two noses reported by set that she'd never otherwise have heard of, a rigger on hull four and a galley hand on escort three, and by the sound of the mates who reported them, neither man had come forward gladly.

The rest came in by trade. The same air, and eight hundred bodies taking it eight hundred ways, sorted by what they did with their hands. The helmsmen went first, because a helmsman stands still and breathes for four hours, and by the second watch there wasn't one in the convoy who wasn't steering a heartbeat behind the ship and correcting too hard; the K Endeavor's own master sent to say his man was sailing her like a hay cart, and could he be looked at. The riggers, who climbed, were fine till they stopped. The deckhands who'd had a bucket in their hands by the pillar came up red to the wrist, as she'd told them they would, and she had every pair logged and gloved and kept an eye on them. Ruan's answer came over from escort one by flag, not by set, which told her what he thought of being asked: Gun crews have heads like the morning after a wedding. Can lay a gun. Have laid six. Stop asking.

And the K Endeavor's cook had been to see her.

He wasn't ill. He'd come up to the hospital deck at seven with two loaves black on the bottom to the depth of a finger, out of forty, from stones he swore were turned down as far as they went, and Noll had stood in his galley with a dial and sworn it with him. He wanted to know who he saw about the air.

She'd told him she would raise it. She had. She'd put it at the foot of the noon slip to the K Amara, under the two noses: K End. cook asks who is responsible for the air. I said I would ask. Ferra's answer had come back inside the quarter hour, by set, in plain. Tell him Ilenne's mother.

They came down at dusk because the old one did. She was at the rail of the hospital deck for it, with Bettany and the box, taking the evening off the cards as the day hands went below. Nobody had passed an order. A mile ahead the smooth place on the sea shortened and stopped, and the K Discovery stopped a mile short of it, and the seven hulls behind her closed up and settled onto the swell one after another, and the sea anchors went out, and up and down the line the lamps came on behind the cabin ports at the mark.

Zale's boat came across in the last of the light with Ilenne in the stern.

That was Fletcher's doing. He'd had the sea tank drained since noon. He'd pumped out every gallon of the Kaldo water it had sailed with, the bay water he had settled and strained and fussed over as if it were varnish, and filled it again from over the side, from this sea, hers; and then he'd stood beside it for an hour with his cap off, pretending to look at a seam. He had built that tank with his own hands.

Ilenne came down the companion with her hood back at last, there being nobody on the tank deck but a carpenter and two women with a box of cards, and she was so bright in the low light that Bettany, behind Imani, breathed in. She looked at the tank. She looked at the open port beside it, and the whole of her own sea lying outside it, a yard away, a thousand miles wide.

Imani saw her see it. She saw the remark arrive, the one about the boots, about the water being there, for swim; she saw it get as far as Ilenne's mouth.

Ilenne looked at Fletcher's cap, in his hands.

"It is the right water," she said. "You knew to change it."

"Seemed only sense," said Fletcher, to the seam.

"Yes." She put her hand flat on the tank's side, the way you'd put it on a horse. "Thank you. I get in now. You go away, I have no legs in a minute, it is not for looking."

He went, and he went up the companion very nearly at a run, and Imani took Ilenne's card out and put on it, beside the Tuesday, the line she had for the bright pearl, and under that line a second mark, which she made up there and then and didn't yet have a word for.

When she came up on deck again it was almost dark. The eight of them lay in their string with their lights lit. The knights' last watch was on the K Amara's aprons, burning it off. And ahead, where there was still a little grey on the water, the smooth place was going. She watched it. It drew in from both ends, slowly, and the swell came back across it a yard at a time, until there was nothing a mile off but sea.

"Where's it gone?" said Bettany.

"Down."

"For the night? What for?"

"I don't know."

"Did anybody ask it to stay?"

Imani looked at the eight lit ships, sitting on a sea none of them could see into.

"Who would it have listened to?" she said.
