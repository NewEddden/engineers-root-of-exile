Chapter 40 - The Standard

Rylan came back on the eighth day, and the precision of it told Emeric more than a speech would have. A man who wanted to be forgiven comes back early, hat in hand, to be seen wanting it. A man who meant to be finished comes back late, or not at all. Rylan came back on the first hour of the first day he was permitted and not a moment before, because he had been told a week and he had taken exactly the week, spent it where he was told to spend it, and returned on the terms he had been given rather than the ones that would have looked best. It was the smallest possible thing and it was the whole man in it. He had been given an order and he had kept it to the hour, even the order to rest, even the order to be a father, and he stood in the yard on the eighth morning waiting to be told the next one.

He was not the man who had left. The one who left had been careful and quiet and conscientious, a man who did his sums and kept his head down and had a soft place in him the whole world could see if it looked, and the men who took his son had looked. The one who came back had been broken all the way down and put back together around a single hard thing, and Emeric, who read men badly, read this one without any trouble at all, because grief that has decided what it is going to do with itself is not subtle. Finn was home and would be home, and would wake in the night for a while yet, and a father who had learned exactly how the world reaches for a man through his children had come back with the fixed and quiet intention of never being reachable that way again. Not by being loved less. By being ready.

"You said come back a different man," Rylan said. "I don't know how to be a different man. I know how to work. Tell me the work."

So Emeric told him, and in the telling laid out the second half of the fix, the half that was not made of runes and stones and mutual delving but of bodies and sweat and the long unglamorous grind of turning men who happened to be strong into men who were made strong on purpose, to a standard, in a way that could be taught to the next ones and the next.

The four elites were some of the best this world had made, and Emeric had spent a month quietly learning that some of the best this world had made was a ceiling, and the ceiling was not going to be enough. Ward and the other three were good the way a gifted smith is good before anyone shows him a thermometer, by feel, by talent, by long private repetition of things they had never once had a name for, and it made them excellent and it made them unrepeatable, and unrepeatable was the same disease as the single hand on the wall, one more thing that lived in specific irreplaceable people and died with them. A barony could not be held by four men who happened to be born able and could not pass the ability on any faster than a life takes to live. It had to be held by a standard, a thing written down and drilled and made, so that the fifth man and the fiftieth could be brought up to it deliberately instead of waited for.

He knew things about the making of soldiers that this world did not, and none of them were magic. That was the part that mattered, the part that kept it honest by his own rule, that everything he meant to import was downstream, ordinary, buildable, the accumulated grind of another world's long expensive experience with the plain problem of keeping men alive and useful in violence. Not a spell. Not a crystal. The sense of your own body in space that keeps a bound man breathing in black water and a fighting man upright when he cannot see his own feet. Strength built to a plan instead of a habit. Endurance past the point where talent quits. Tactics that were taught and not merely possessed, coordination drilled until men moved as a thing and not as a crowd, survival in cold and hunger and dark, and the water, always the water, because Kaldo was a port and a lake and a river and the one route out that was not the ford had been water and would be water again, and a man who could not keep himself alive in it bound was a man the water could always be used against. He had survived the channel on a drill he had watched other men learn on a screen in another life. He was going to make it a thing his men learned in this one, in the lake, in daylight, on purpose, so that the next time the river was turned into a weapon it found people who had already been taught to float.

"It won't feel like enough for a long time," he told Rylan, because it wouldn't, and because the man deserved the true shape of it. "None of it pays off tomorrow. You build a body to a standard over months, not days. You build a company over years. I'm starting a thing today that I won't be able to use for a season and won't have finished for a decade, and I'm starting it anyway, because the mistake I made on that wall was thinking I had time to do the slow work later, and there is no later, there's only the next boat, and the only question is whether it finds us the way the last one did or finds us ready."

He did not leave himself out of it. That was the other change, and the one only he could see. He had spent a childhood suppressing what he was and a second life offloading everything he could not suppress onto tools, and both of those had been the same move, the same refusal to grind a thing into his own body where it could not be taken from him. On the wall he had reached for a cast he did not have and come up with nothing because the one discipline he had never been willing to pay for was the one that lived in a trained body and answered an empty hand. So he would pay for it now, late, badly, starting from behind, the handless casting that a real mage held whole in his mind, the spell and the shape and the direction and every parameter of it carried at once without a stone to lean on or a hand to point, a thing built only by casting and casting until it lived below thought. It would take years he should have spent already. He started it that morning, in the cold, doing a beginner's clumsy work at a thing a suppressed boy could have had a decade of by now, and he found he did not mind being a beginner, because being a beginner on purpose was the opposite of being the only one, and he had learned which of those kept a man alive.

The other guards watched Rylan take the first drills and did not say what they thought, and Emeric let them not say it, because he had no words that would answer the thing they were holding off their faces and he knew it. A lord who did not hang the man who opened his gate was a lord who had told them the door might still be for sale, and no speech would unsay that. Only the work would. They would watch the man who betrayed him become the hardest-trained soldier in the barony, watch the gate that was opened once get rebuilt into a thing no knife to any child could open again, watch the standard rise under all of them until the question of whether a lord would look away for a man he liked stopped being the question, because the wall itself no longer depended on any one man's liking or any one man's spine. The answer to their silence was not going to be given in the hall. It was going to be built in the yard, over years, and they would come to it or they would not, and either way it would be true.

He took a blade session of his own that same week, from Ward, in a bad-lit corner of the yard where no one had cause to watch, and he was clumsy at it in a different way than he was clumsy at the casting, clumsy with a body that half knew forms his mind had never learned, so that his hands kept starting motions he had to catch and unmake, and Ward said nothing about the ruined wrists and only moved him through it slow, once and then again, the way a thing is taught to a man who will need it and not to a lord who wants flattering. Knights were what the barony had lost, one gifted hand at one gate. Soldiers were what it would keep. He did not make a speech of it. He said it once, to Ward, between forms, that they were soldiers now and not knights, and Ward heard the whole of what was under it, that no wall worth the name leaned on a single spine, and the two of them went back to the work.

And under all of it, cold and level and going nowhere, the account stayed open. He did not speak Marrow's name to Rylan and Rylan did not speak it to him, because it did not need speaking, because it was in every drill and every cold morning and every hour of the slow work, the shared and unhurried knowledge that the man who did this had walked away thinking the night a wash and would be wrong about that the way he had been wrong about a spent father and a cargo prince. They were not going to chase him angry into the dark and be picked off one at a time by a professional whose whole trade was patience. They were going to become, patiently, the thing that patience could not outlast, and one day, not soon, not carelessly, the account would come due, and it would be paid by men who had been made ready for it on the morning after they were nearly destroyed, in the yard, to a standard, on purpose.

"Tell me the work," Rylan had said.

Emeric told him, and then he rolled up his own sleeves over his ruined wrists and took the cold clumsy first hour of the rest of his life beside him, and the two of them began, in a barony that had nearly lost the one hand that held it, to build the many hands that would.
