Book 2, Chapter 11: Say It in the Water

The Keeper of the Stones came up to the hard rooms at seven on the Saturday morning, two days after the door, because the queen had asked him to teach the land man the forms, and he made it plain inside a minute that he thought the land man should have learned them before he got married.

Zale had been up the stages since six. He'd come up to the hard rooms for breakfast because Imani had told him to, and because the floor of the bell was glass and had not got any softer on the second night, and he was sitting at the table in the Keepers' hall with a cup of something hot and green that tasted of the sea's bottom when the bell door opened and an old man came through it with a stone under his arm.

The Keepers' hall was a hard room too. It was the next one along from the guest rooms, through a short tunnel in the rock with a door at each end, and it was smaller, and older, and the ribs of its dome were set closer and the panes between them were so old the glass had gone the colour of weak tea. There were shelves cut into the rock walls all the way round, and on the shelves, flat, one beside the next, stones. Grey stones, mostly, the size of a tray or a slate, each with lines cut into its face. Hundreds of them. The hall was full of their quiet. Emeric had walked in yesterday and stopped in the doorway and stood there a good while, and Zale had seen him put his hand out toward the nearest shelf and not touch it.

The old man put his stone down on the table in front of Zale, face up, and sat down opposite him, on his legs, and looked at him.

He was tall and very thin, with grey skin gone loose at the jaw and long white hair tied back with a cord, and a plain grey coat like the door Keeper's, and his colour was so still it might have been painted on. His hands were long. One of them stayed on the stone. His eyes were pale and clear and without pity, and they went over Zale once, from his wet hair to the cord at his throat, and stopped on the cord, and came back up.

He said something.

His voice surprised Zale. It was low, much lower than the build of him promised, and rough, and flat, a voice with all the tune taken out of it, as if somebody had put it through a mangle. It didn't go up and down. It just went.

"He says you are the land man," said Ilenne, beside Zale. She'd come up the stages with him and sat down without being asked, which was how he knew she'd known who was coming. Her colour was held as still as the old man's. Her eyes were on the stone on the table, not on him. "He says he hears you bow well. He says the bow is the easy part. He asks have you read the forms."

"Tell him I'm a sailor," said Zale. "I read charts."

Ilenne told him. The old man went on looking at Zale, and then he said something short, and Ilenne's mouth moved at the corner, very slightly.

"He says then he reads them to you. Like a child."

"Tell him that's very kind."

She didn't tell him that.

His name was Ressar, and he was the Keeper of the Stones, and he'd taught Ilenne.

She told Zale that while the old man was turning the stone on the table to the light and finding his place on it with one long finger. She said it quietly, in Kaldo, fast, not looking at him. When she was a child, every child of the deep houses sat in front of Ressar and the teaching stones and learned them: the forms, the courtesies, the songs. She'd sat in front of him for years. She'd never once looked at his face. She'd watched the stones.

"Why not?"

"Because I was frightened of him." She thought about that. "Because everyone was."

"Are you now?"

Ilenne didn't answer. Then she said, even more quietly, "He was a girl."

Zale looked at her.

"Before. A daughter. Of a small house, very old, not great." Her hands were together on the table in front of her. "The last son of the house dies young. The house needs a man for its seat. So she takes the change." She glanced at him, quickly, and away. "Some of us can. Once. When a house needs. Not for wanting. It takes a season, in deep water, and it takes a great deal, and after you are weak a long time. And it takes something of who you were. Always a little." She looked at the old man's hands on the stone. "He sang. When she was a girl. The old ones say nobody sang the stones like her. Now he can't. The children learn them from him, but spoken. You hear his voice."

"I heard it."

"Forty years he's a man. The court calls him he." She said it as plainly as she said anything. "Nobody calls him soft."

Zale sat with that. He'd grown up in his father's house and then on ships, and he'd known a fair number of people who'd turned into somebody else halfway through their lives for one reason or another, and none of them had done it like that. He looked across the table at the thin grey man with the flat voice, finding his place on a stone with his finger, who'd been a girl who sang, and had given it up for a seat at a table because there was nobody else, and he thought of his mother.

"Right," he said.

Ressar had found his place. He looked up, and said a thing, and put his finger on the line.

It took him most of an hour, and Zale understood about half of it at the time and the rest on the steps.

The sideways hand at the door wasn't a word. That was the first thing, and Ressar made Ilenne say it twice. A sign said nothing in the water; you could make any sign you liked at anyone and it bound nobody. That was why it was so bad, and why it was allowed: it was the thing a man did who wanted to say the worst thing there was and not have to stand behind it. So the custom gave the man it was made at a way to make him stand behind it.

"You go back," said Ilenne, putting it over as Ressar's finger went down the stone. "To the place. The door. The same witnesses: the captain, and Taurre. In the season. In the water. And you call him. You call him to say it. Not with the hand. With his mouth, in the water, before them."

There were two ways it could go. The first was if Ushar came and wouldn't say it. Ressar's finger lay on that part of the stone and stayed there while Ilenne gave it. If he was called and came and didn't speak, the witnesses said so, on the record: called, and did not speak. And from that moment the sign went back to him. It was void against the man he'd made it at. He couldn't make it at him again before witnesses, ever. And the shame of a thing said with the hand and not with the mouth sat on him, not on the one he'd said it to.

"And the second way?"

Ilenne's colour didn't move, but she took a breath.

"If he says it. In the water. Before them. Then it's his word, in the water. For ever. And a word like that he has to make good. Before the court, in the season, he has to show your word is broken." She looked at Zale. "Your word in the water. The binding."

"Which it isn't."

"Which it isn't. So he can't show it. And if he can't show it..." She let Ressar say it, and then she said it after him. "Then he carries the name. Water. Himself. For ever."

Ressar said something more, and Ilenne hesitated.

"He says it's a very old saying. On the stones. Say it wet, show it wet." She put her hand flat on the table, and then turned it, as you'd turn a cup over. "He says there's one on the stones who did it. Long ago. Said it wet, in the water, about a binding a week old. He carried the name to his grave. His sons carried it." She glanced at the shelves round the walls. "Ressar says it's on the third shelf. He says if you could read, he'd show you."

"Tell him I'll take his word for it."

So nobody said it, Zale thought. Not about a binding made last week. Nobody sane. Whatever you thought of the man, you couldn't show his word broken a week after he'd given it, because he hadn't had time to break it. The hand at the door had been safe because it was only a hand. If Zale called him, Ushar couldn't take the second way. He could only take the first.

He looked at Ressar. Ressar was looking at him, with the same pale eyes, and something that might have been the beginnings of interest.

"Ask him," said Zale, "if I can make him come without saying I will."

Ilenne turned and looked at him then, properly, for the first time that morning.

"Because I'm not saying it again," said Zale. "In the water. Ever. I've said it once this week and once's plenty. Ask him."

She asked. Ressar was quiet. Then he said one short thing, and for the first time his colour moved: a thread of something, very faint, at the throat, there and gone.

"He says you ask," said Ilenne. "A question isn't a promise. He says..." She stopped, and her mouth went at the corner again. "He says maybe you read a little after all."



He asked her afterwards, in the tunnel between the halls, with the door shut at one end and the other.

"It's your door," he said. "It's your mother's name on it. If I go out there and stand in the water and call a man of the Rim's house up the stair to answer for a thing he did with his hand on your wedding day, that's in front of your mother's people, at your mother's gate. If you'd rather I let it lie..."

"You can't let it lie. It's your name."

"I know it's my name. I'm asking you."

She looked at him in the dim tunnel with the cold lamp over them. And before she'd said anything at all, before she'd opened her mouth, her colour said it. He saw it come. Not the blue at her throat that he didn't know how to read. The other one, the warm one, the one she'd taught the whole fleet on the aft deck for yes, for for, that went up over her face and her shoulders all at once like sun coming through cloud. Then she'd let it go and she was holding it still again and her face was quite serious.

"Yes," she said. "Call him."

"You'll come down?"

"No." She shook her head. "This one is yours. I stand on the dry. At the top. With your brother." She put her hand on his arm, quickly, and took it off again. "You do it in my tongue. Badly. Like the fisherman's uncle."

"I'm going to have to learn some new words, then."

"You learn them by eleven," said Ilenne. "The tide turns at eleven."

He learned them by eleven. He learned them in the guest rooms with his back to the wall, from Ilenne and then from Ressar, who stayed, and who corrected his sounds with a flat patient contempt that Zale found he didn't mind at all, because it was the way the mate on his first ship had taught him knots. Emeric sat at the far end of the table with a cup and the dry coat over the back of his chair and didn't say a word the whole time. Armand sat beside him with a book open and wasn't reading it. Taurre sent a runner up from the watch at nine, a girl on her legs with a slate, to say that word had gone to the Rim's house as the custom required: the land man would call at the turn of the tide. The runner looked at Zale as if he were a two-headed fish and went away again.

At a quarter to eleven he went down to the gate.

They were all there. He'd known they would be, and he'd been ready for it, and it was still worse than he'd thought. The landing at the top of the steps, where Taurre had stood on Thursday morning, had people on it: the watch, three of them, in their dark coats with their slates; a pilot or two; a handful of merfolk on their legs in good coats whom Zale didn't know and who stood very upright, from the Rim or from Below, he couldn't tell. Inside the gate, on the water, his own ship, with every one of his crew at the rail, and Hesk at the front of them, and Linnet beside Hesk with her arms folded. And in the water itself, under the arch, on the steps where the twelve had stood, heads. More heads than there'd been on Thursday. Merfolk in the sea, in a loose crowd all round the foot of the stair, keeping their places with their tails, watching.

Maeve had come down. She stood at the side of the landing by the rock with her hands behind her back, as she'd stood on the threshold on Thursday, and when she saw him come out of the stair she lifted her chin at him.

Ilenne went to the top step and stood there on the dry in a plain dark dress with her colour held like stone. Emeric went with her. He stood at her shoulder in his shirtsleeves on the top step of the queen's door, with the sea twenty steps below him and his hands behind his back, and Zale looked at him once and saw how he'd put them there: one hand round the other wrist, the way you'd hold a dog.

Then Zale went down the steps into the water.

He went in to the waist. The sea was cold under the arch, colder than out at the ship, and it lifted him and set him down with the swell coming through. The captain of the twelve was there already, standing on the step below him on one side in the deep water, as he'd stood on Thursday, with his colour level and his bident upright. Taurre came down from the landing into the sea in his long coat as if it were nothing, and took the step on the other side. Ressar came last. He came down the stair slowly on his old legs and went into the water to the chest, a step up from the witnesses, with no stone under his arm now, and stood there with his long white hair floating out behind him on the swell.

The tide turned. Zale felt it. He'd felt tides turn all his life, at moorings and in harbour mouths and on the beach at South Sentinel, and he felt this one: a slackening, a pause in the push of the water through the gate against his chest, as if the whole sea had stopped to listen.

He called.

He said it in her tongue, the words Ilenne and Ressar had beaten into him all morning, as loud as he could say them without shouting, which was loud enough: he'd shouted across decks in gales. He said the name, Ushar, and the name of his house, the Rim's, the long name that Ilenne had made him say forty times; and then he said the shape of the call, which was a question. He didn't say I will. He'd have bitten his tongue out first. He said, as near as he could put it into his own head: You made a sign at me at this door, before these. Will you say it?

His voice went across the water under the arch and came back off the black stone. He heard it come back. It was the voice of a fisherman's uncle.

Nobody laughed. He'd half expected somebody to.

The heads in the water kept their places. The people on the landing kept theirs. Up on the top step, Ilenne didn't move at all. He couldn't see Emeric's hands from where he stood.

He waited.



Ushar came level.

He didn't come up from under. Zale had wondered whether he would, and had been ready for it, and had decided in the night on the glass floor that if he did he'd stand still and not turn round, whatever it cost. He needn't have. Ushar came in from the side, through the open water inside the gate, swimming on the surface with his head up, slowly, where everyone could see him come, and the crowd in the water parted for him without being asked. He came to the steps. He came up the stair under the water until his feet found a step, and then he stood up out of the sea on the same step as Zale, ten feet off along it, at the same depth exactly, waist-deep, and faced him.

Level. Not above, not below. Ilenne had taught them that one first.

He was a big man. Zale had known that from Thursday, but he hadn't stood beside him. He was half a head taller than Zale and broader, and he had his hair bound back and the collar of red stone round his throat with the sea running off it. His colour was perfectly still. He looked at Zale for a long breath without anything in his face at all.

He bowed. The guest's bow, level, exactly as Zale had done it on Thursday, to the same depth. Somebody on the landing let out a breath.

Zale bowed back. He didn't think about it; his body had learned it on the aft deck under Ilenne's eye and did it for him.

Then Ushar spoke.

He didn't say the word. Zale knew within three words that he wasn't going to; you could hear it in the shape of the thing, a man starting to refuse before he gets to no. He spoke slowly and clearly, not loud, in his own tongue, and he spoke not to Zale but past him, to the witnesses and to Ressar and to the steps, and Zale stood in the water and understood none of it but the rhythm, and waited for Ilenne.

When Ushar had finished, Ilenne said it from the top of the steps in Kaldo, clearly, so that it would carry. She said it flat. She said it as she'd said Ressar's forms, without any colour in it at all.

"He says: he doesn't speak in the water to a man who can't breathe it."

It went across the steps like a gust across a harbour. Zale felt it go. He felt it go through the heads in the water and the people on the landing, a movement, not loud, and he saw colours go in it, up and down the crowd round the foot of the stair, here and there and here. Not slate. He knew slate. Something else, warmer, quick, there and away, and on more of them than he liked.

They thought it was fair.

Not all of them. Some of them. Enough. He could see that much without being able to read a single colour on any of them, the way you can see on a quay which way a crowd's leaning before anybody's said a word. A land man stood waist-deep in their sea at their door, on the steps their own children learned to walk on, and called a son of the Rim to say a thing in the water, in a tongue the land man spoke like a fisherman's uncle; and he couldn't go under. He couldn't swim the stages without a woman holding his hand. He couldn't breathe the water he was calling a man to speak in. Ushar hadn't said the word. He'd said something worse, and true, and allowed.

And he'd still refused. That was the other half of it. Zale held on to that half. He'd come when he was called and he hadn't said it.

The captain of the twelve spoke. Short, formal, to Ressar. Taurre spoke after him, the same few words, in the same order, in his level clerk's voice. Called, and did not speak. Ilenne didn't need to put that into Kaldo; Zale had heard Ressar say the shape of it forty times that morning.

Then Ressar spoke, from his step above them in the water, in his flat voice with no tune left in it.

It was not long. It didn't need to be. Zale knew what it was. The sign was returned. It was void against the man it had been made at. The man who'd made it could not make it at him again before witnesses. And whatever shame there was in a thing said with the hand and not with the mouth, it belonged now where it had come from.

Ressar finished. He looked at Ushar, not at Zale, and the look went on for a moment after the words had stopped.

Taurre took his slate out from under his arm, there in the sea, and wrote. Zale watched him write it. Three lines, and a mark at the end.

The colours in the crowd went again, all down the foot of the stair, a different way. Zale saw the shame go across. He couldn't have said how. It was a thing a crowd did with its attention: it had been on him, and it moved, the whole weight of it, ten feet along the step, onto the big man in the red collar standing in the sea beside him, and settled there.

Ushar didn't move under it. His colour stayed perfectly still. He'd known, Zale thought. He'd known when he came that this was what it would cost him, and he'd come anyway, and he'd paid it with one sentence that cost Zale more, and he was going to stand there and let them all watch him pay.

Then he bowed again, to the witnesses, to Ressar, and last to Zale, level, exactly right; and he went back into the water as he'd come, on the surface, slowly, with his head up, and the crowd parted again to let him through.

Zale stood in the sea with the tide beginning to push against his chest again.

He'd won. He knew he'd won. Ilenne had told him what winning looked like, and this was it, written on Taurre's slate in three lines and a mark. He'd have liked it to feel more like it.

He looked up the steps. Ilenne was standing where she'd stood, on the dry, with her colour held so hard he could see it cost her. Emeric was beside her. He'd taken his hands from behind his back. They were at his sides now, open, and quite still, and his face had nothing in it at all; but Zale had sailed with his brother long enough to know what it had cost his brother to stand at the top of a stair with his hands behind him and watch, and do nothing, and let it be Zale's. He could see it on him from twenty steps down.

Zale climbed out of the water.



On the landing, after, while the crowd went back to wherever crowds went in a city half under the sea, Armand went to Ressar.

Zale saw it because he was sitting on the third step from the top, the dry one, with his legs out in front of him and his trousers draining down the stair, getting his breath. Ressar had come up out of the water on his old legs and was standing at the edge of the landing wringing out his hair with his long hands, and Armand went across to him with his hands in his coat pockets and his head a little on one side, the way he went across a lecture room to a student who'd asked a good question.

Armand said something. Zale didn't catch it. Ilenne, who'd come down to sit by Zale, did, and put it over for him under her breath. He'd asked how the teaching stones were read. Not what they said. How. With the finger, or the eye, or something else. Whether a person who couldn't hear their songs could still read them.

Ressar stopped wringing his hair and looked at him.

He said a short thing. Ilenne didn't need to translate it. Zale knew it from the morning.

"He asks have you done the reading."

Armand thought about it. Then he said, seriously, that he had. All of it. Every page of Ferra's word book, and every note Ilenne had given her in the word room in July. He'd done all the reading there was. In the wrong language.

Ilenne put that over too. She did it carefully.

Ressar considered him, a thin old man in a wet grey coat looking at a man with a young face and an old man's way of standing. His colour did nothing. Then he turned round and went down the stair past Zale and Ilenne into the water, step by step, without a word, and went under.

Armand stood on the landing with his hands in his pockets and watched the place where he'd gone.

"Well," he said, to nobody in particular. "That's a beginning."



The notice came in the middle of the afternoon.

Zale was sitting on the steps again by then, because there was nowhere else on the Rim he was allowed to sit that he liked better. He had his legs in the water to the knee and his boots beside him on the dry. Ilenne was sitting on the step above him, on the dry, with her feet tucked under her dress, and not in the water at all. She'd said that if she put her feet in she'd go, and she didn't want to go, she wanted to sit.

She was teaching him the colour signs. Not the colours: the hand signs for them, the ones the fleet had learned on the aft deck so that people who couldn't make the colours could still say them. He knew most of the big ones. She was teaching him the small ones now, the ones you used between two people.

"This," she said, and made it: the fingers of one hand together, drawn up from the wrist of the other, quickly, and opened. "When a thing is done well. Not a big thing. Well done. You say it to a pilot when he brings you in. You say it to a child."

"Like that?"

"No."

"Like that?"

Ilenne's colour went. All of it, at once, a flicker from the throat down, light and dark, and then she'd put her hand over her mouth and he was fairly sure she was laughing.

"What?" said Zale. "What did I say?"

"Not that." She took her hand away. Her face was perfectly straight. "Never that. Not to a pilot. Not to anybody's mother."

"What's it mean?"

She told him. She told him in her own tongue, which didn't help, and then she found the Kaldo for it, which did.

"Oh," said Zale.

Behind them, from the landing, Maeve said, "Do it again."

They both turned round. Maeve was sitting on the edge of the landing with her boots hanging over the top step, where she'd plainly been sitting for some time.

"The wrong one," said Maeve. "Do it again. Slower. I want it."

"You do not want it," said Ilenne.

"I want it more than anything I've wanted all week." Maeve held up her hands. "Go on. Show me. I'll not use it on anybody who doesn't deserve it."

Zale showed her. Ilenne made a small despairing noise and put her face in her hands. Maeve did it back to him, slowly, perfectly, first time, and grinned, and did it again faster.

"Now the right one," said Ilenne, through her hands.

"Later," said Maeve.

That was when the runner came. The same girl as the morning, on her legs, with her slate, from the watch. She came down the steps past Maeve and stopped on the step above Ilenne, and said a few words to her, and showed her the slate. Ilenne read it. Then she took her feet out from under her dress and sat up straight, and her colour was held again, as hard as it had been at eleven.

"Notice," she said. "In the dry hall. Just now. The Rim's house."

"Saying?"

She read it to him off the slate, putting it into Kaldo as she went, slowly, the way Ferra read a contract.

"The Rim's house holds that the binding at the door, of the queen's daughter and the land man, was never made. And says why. Three things." She held up one finger. "A land man can't be bound. He's not of the water." Two. "The witnesses were the queen's. Not free." Three. "The door is outside the city. A binding outside the city doesn't count." She lowered her hand. "The court hears it. In the dry hall. When the queen says."

Zale took his legs out of the water.

So that was what the morning had bought. He'd sent the hand back to the man who'd made it, before witnesses, in their own custom, and the man had paid for it in front of everyone and gone back into the sea; and three hours later his house had stood up in a dry room under the Rim and said that none of it counted, because Zale wasn't anybody. He couldn't breathe their water. He wasn't of it.

He thought about Ressar's face when Ushar said it, and Ressar's look after.

"Ferra'll want this," he said.

"Ferra has it." Ilenne gave the slate back to the runner. "The runner goes to her first. Ferra asked."

Of course she had.



He went down to the bell with Ilenne at five, to get out of his wet trousers and lie on the glass for an hour; and at dusk he went up the stages for the post, as he'd said he would.

He came up out of the pool at the head of the stages on the end of Ilenne's hand, and climbed the wet stair from the last step, where Imani's set had found its voice, past the water door, and the forty steps, and came out on the landing into a red evening with the lamps coming up along the Rim, and went down to his boat and rowed himself across to his own ship inside the gate.

They were all at the rail. Every one of them. Hesk, and the crew, and Linnet with her sheets. They watched him come alongside and they watched him come up the ladder, and nobody said anything, and then Hesk put out a hand and took him by the shoulder and shook him once, hard, as you'd shake a man pulled back out of the sea.

"Well done," said Hesk.

Zale looked at him. Then he made the sign. The wrong one. Slowly.

Hesk didn't know what it meant, and nobody else on the deck did either, and that was the best part.

Linnet sat down on the deckhouse step and read the day. The K Discovery's position. The sick list. A line from Ward that was really to Emeric. And a line from Ferra, written in the guest rooms and sent out to the hulls on the four o'clock traffic, which Linnet read in exactly the same voice she used for the weather.

"Notice laid in the dry hall, 2 p.m., by the Rim's house. The binding never made. Three grounds. Court to hear at the queen's word. We'll want the stones."

The crew looked at Zale.

"Never made," said Hesk. "I was there."

"So was I," said Zale. "It's a funny thing. I'd have sworn to it." He sat down on the hatch cover in his wet trousers. "Read the weather, Linnet. I want to hear something I understand."
