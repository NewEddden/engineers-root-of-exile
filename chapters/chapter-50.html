Chapter 50 - The Same Water

He found Ferra where he expected to, bent over her ledgers in the small room she'd taken for herself, and he delved before he spoke so the words would sit in the quiet between them and nowhere else. It was a habit now, the two of them dropping into the shared dark for anything that shouldn't carry, and neither of them remarked on it anymore.

"Bram's men," he said. "The ones who'll be on the new build. Is there a point on any of them where someone could reach in and turn them?"

She was quiet a moment, and he felt the shape of her surprise before she gave it words. "You knew I'd have them."

"I knew you don't stop at what you're told." He'd asked her once to vet the guards. He had not asked her to work up the men in the foundry, and he had never doubted for an instant that she had, because Ferra's mind ran the way water ran downhill, into every low place whether or not anyone had dug her a channel to it. Wherever her father's work touched, and wherever his own work touched, she'd have gone quietly and written it all down.

They laughed about it, low, in the dark. He asked her how many lives she'd opened up like that, and she told him, without any particular pride, that another couple of months and she could likely have the whole of the barony set down and noted, every household, every debt, every quarrel worth knowing.

"It's like you're playing a game I used to know," he said, before he'd thought it through. "Where you look down on a whole little town from above and every person in it is a piece you can read the whole of. And they don't know you're up there. They just live." He heard how it landed and wished he'd kept it behind his teeth.

She didn't know the game, of course. She couldn't. To tell her what it was he'd have to tell her what it ran on, and that led back through a door he kept shut, so he did the thing he did when his mouth outran his sense, he gave her something real to look at instead of the thing he couldn't explain. He built her a map in the delve, a view of Kaldo from straight overhead, roofs and lanes and the river's bend, the whole town laid flat the way a bird would never bother to see it and a man never could. It was not accurate. He'd never stood above the town, only walked it, in the plague weeks and the farm weeks, and his mind filled the gaps with what walking had taught it, so the map was true where his feet had been and a guess everywhere else. He read her notes as he built, and set each name where it belonged, so that the flat little town filled with the people she'd written down.

She was rapt. He watched her lean into it, this thing that let her see all at once what she'd only ever held one page at a time, and he felt the pleasure of it and, right behind the pleasure, the cold. He made a joke of that too, told her he was worried he was raising a stalker, and thanked whatever ran the world that the map wasn't a living thing, only a record, only the past laid out after the fact and not the present watched in the moment.

He didn't say the rest of it, which was that a version of this that did watch the present, that put Ferra's reach and someone like Ward's memory in the same room and let them cover a place without ever missing a face, would be the most useful instrument he'd ever built and the one he'd least want to explain to the man he'd been. In the right hands it kept a barony safe. In the wrong hands it was every heavy thing he'd fled a whole world to be quit of, wearing a friendly face. He filed it where he filed the tools he wasn't sure he should ever pick up, and told himself the filing was the same as deciding, and knew, even as he did it, that it wasn't.

He came back to the question he'd actually come to ask. Any weak point. Anyone who could be bought or leaned on.

"No." She said it flat, and she said it fast, and both told him she'd already asked herself long before he had. "They've lived here their whole lives, every one of them. Kaldo's all they've ever known. There's nothing under any of them for a stranger to get a hand on."

Bram, when he heard it later, seconded it in his own way, which was gruff and entirely certain. The men were near enough his own sons. He'd watched them grow, taught them their trade, stood over them for years. If one of them turned on Kaldo, Bram would eat the foundry brick by brick. It was not the kind of vouching Emeric could put in a ledger, and it was worth more than anything he could.

So the men were clean, and he could trust them, and that settled the last thing standing between him and the build.

"One more," he told Bram. "I want a coal order drawn up and run through Saban's people. A large one. Grayrock coal, down the river."

Bram's brow went down. "Coal. We've charcoal enough for the furnaces this season, and coal's dirty for iron besides. What do you want with coal?"

"It's a surprise." Emeric let himself enjoy that, a little, which he did not often allow. "Draw the order. The next thing we build changes everything about how this works, and I'd rather show you than tell you."

Bram gave him the look of a man who'd learned that the prince's surprises cost him sleep and were usually worth it anyway, and went to draw the order.

He gathered the men the next morning, and he did not teach them a single thing about measurement, because there was nothing left to teach. The foot was the foot; it had been the foot since the collars, since the week he'd burned every rule in the works that disagreed with the master and struck true copies for every bench. What he taught them was the drawing.

He put a blueprint in front of them the like of which they'd never worked from, denser than anything the furnace line had wanted, and he made it deliberately useless to a man who couldn't read it properly. Every dimension called to the new tolerances, tighter than casting had ever asked, tight enough that the old eye-and-feel would fail here where it had served everywhere else. He handed out the tools that went with it, rules and calipers and a set of gauges he'd had the first true copies of struck the week before, and he made them prove they could read a figure off the page and find it on the metal and know when they'd missed it by a hair. He forced the literacy the only way he trusted, by making the work impossible without it, so that no man could nod along and quietly fake understanding the way men do when a lord is explaining.

They took to it faster than he'd have believed. He set them a hard exit problem at the end, expecting to lose half of them, and lost none. Every man passed, and passed clean.

It nagged at him afterward, the way it always did. He'd have called a room this quick to learn exceptional, in the world he'd come from. Here it was just Tuesday. He'd long since stopped assuming these people were simple and started wondering whether the mana that soaked everything living in a place like this, thin here compared to the deep places but never nothing, put a finger on the scale for every mind it touched, lifted them all a little, the way a rising river lifts every boat in it without any of them noticing they sit higher than their fathers did. He had no way to prove it and no time to try. He filed it under things the world did that he didn't understand yet, a shelf that never got shorter no matter how much he learned.

He took Bram alone into the back room after, and delved, and showed him the whole of it built and running in the shared dark.

Bram walked around it once. Then he stopped, and Emeric watched the understanding cross his face in stages, the way it had with the furnace, a man reading a machine with thirty years of standing in front of hot metal and finding the one thing in it that mattered.

"It's the water," Bram said slowly. "But it's not, it's not turning anything. The wheel's not in this at all." He looked closer at the vessel, the sealed belly of it, the fire laid under. "You're not using the river's push. You're boiling it. You want the water as steam."

"Yep, that same water," Emeric said. "It gave us motion off the wheel, and the wheel was never enough, you knew that the day we lit the first furnace and had to choose between the blast and the hoist. One wheel can't feed what this works is becoming. We framed for a second wheel and never built it. This is what comes instead of the second wheel. Same river, same water, a different thing done with it. Heat instead of push. And heat we can make as much of as we can feed."

"The coal," Bram said, and it wasn't a question now. "That's the feed. Not for the iron. For this."

"For this. Grayrock's ours now, and Grayrock has coal, and coal comes down a river the way everything comes down a river, for the price of loading it and nothing more. I've a notice going up to Grayrock to send enough down that we won't want for a lump of it the whole build. After that the works makes its own weather. It doesn't wait on a dry month or a slow current. It waits on nothing but fire."

He let Bram have the wonder of it for a moment, because the man had earned it and because it was, honestly, a fine thing to look at. Then he made himself say the part he'd been circling, because a wonder you didn't name the price of was a wonder that killed someone.

"It's also the first thing we've built that can kill this whole crew at once," he said. "Everything before, the worst it could do was ruin a heat or cost a man a hand, bad, but one man, one moment. This holds fire and water in a sealed iron belly and asks them not to argue, and the day they argue, it doesn't take a hand. It takes the building and everyone standing in it. I have seen what one of these does when a man decides he knows better than the pressure. There's no magic in that failure and no magic that stops it. It's just iron and heat and whether the men respect a thing that gives them no reason to, right up until the one time it does."

Bram was quiet, reading the vessel again with different eyes.

"So the machine's the easy half," Emeric went on. "I could build the machine in my sleep; it's my own field, there's nothing in it I don't know. The hard half is the crew. Drills they'll think are foolish. A man on the pressure who's allowed to stop the whole works and answer to no one, not even me, if the needle climbs where it shouldn't. Rules that cost us time every single day the machine doesn't fail, which will be nearly every day, which is exactly why they'll want to stop keeping them." He heard his own certainty and, under it, the older certainty he'd learned to distrust, that knowing the system was the same as knowing the room. It never had been. He read machines truer than he read men, and the machine was not the thing that would get someone killed here. "That's the part I'll get wrong if I get any of it wrong. Watch me on it."

"Aye," Bram said. "I will."

The coal came down two weeks later on a pair of broad flat floats, riding the current the easy way, and every man who watched it come understood without being told why the prince had wanted it off the river and not off the road.

It cost the river nothing to carry it down. That was the whole trick of the thing, the same trick Emeric had turned over half the night not long past when he'd been staring at roads and rails: water carried a load downhill for free and would not carry it back up for any price. The floats had come down loaded and quick. They would go back up empty and slow, poled and hauled against a current that gave nothing away, and the men who worked them would earn every hour of the return. Nobody pretended otherwise. Down was a gift. Up was work, and the coal only ever needed to come down.

They unloaded it onto the bank below the foundry, and Ferra had already covered the delivery against the invoice she'd run through Saban, so there was nothing owed at the water's edge. Emeric had them add a little past the fee anyway, and put it in the boatmen's hands himself, not so much that it looked like a lord buying loyalty, enough that it looked like a man who meant to want these floats again and knew the difference between paying for a load and paying for a road that would still carry him next season. The boatmen took it and marked him, the way rivermen mark anyone worth remembering, and that was worth more than the coins.

Then they started to build, and for once the building was the part he wasn't worried about.
