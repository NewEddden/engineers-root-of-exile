Chapter 19 - House Ducatis

Ferra kept the count on the hall wall now, a grid of charcoal squares running to the turn of the month, and each morning she crossed one out before she did anything else. Emeric understood the first time he saw it that the calendar was not for him. It was for her, a way to put her hands on a thing that otherwise had none.

Nineteen squares to payroll.

The bills had started coming in the way she'd said they would, ahead of the ore and ahead of the harvest, indifferent to both. The charcoal burners wanted paying for what they'd already cut and stacked. Saban's carters wanted earnest money in hand before a single wagon rolled north, because carters did not haul Duvrenne ore up a mountain road on a lord's good intentions. And the ore stockpile beside the foundry, a month deep when they'd lit the first furnace, was three weeks now, and thinning faster than three weeks because a running blast furnace does not rest.

And behind all of it, the hole the fire had left. The granary that should have been a season's cushion was a black square of ground and a smell that would not leave the west quarter, and everything it had held, the seed for spring, the reserve against the lean months, the margin that had made the coming harvest only need to be adequate, was ash. Emeric had built a machine that could turn a hillside into plows. He had not built one that could turn nineteen days into thirty, or grain back out of smoke, and the war had taken from him the one buffer that would have let him fail slowly instead of all at once.

So he did the thing he always did first. He reached for his own playbook before he reached for anyone else's.

"Sell it forward," he told Saban. "The foundry is going to pour plow-heads and tools all season. Farms inside two days' ride will all want cast iron once they see what it does. So we sell it now, at a fair price, for coin now, and we deliver as the furnaces run."

Saban turned the idea over the way he turned over everything, looking for the seam. "Presell the harvest of a foundry instead of the harvest of a field." He nodded slowly. "It can be done. Men will pay ahead for a good price and a sure thing." Then, because he was honest, and honesty meant not letting a lord hear only the half he wanted: "How sure a thing can I tell them it is?"

That was the trouble, and Emeric knew it before Saban finished asking. He could not promise volume. The mine was thinning, the imported ore wasn't moving yet, and even with iron to burn, the foundry could not run flat out, because the hoist and the blast still shared one wheel and one man calling when the crane was allowed to move. He could sell what he was certain to make. He could not sell what he'd built three furnaces to make, because he could not yet make it.

So Saban went out and sold the certain part, and came back with less than they needed and a stack of promises Emeric now owed in iron. Some lords bought. Some freeholders bought. And Holt, who farmed the largest fields outside Kaldo proper and whose custom would have been worth more than the rest together, did not.

"He didn't haggle," Saban reported. "I'd have known what to do with a man who haggled. He heard the price, and it was a good price, and he said he'd buy his plow-heads the old way, forged, from a smith he could name, and if my lord's sunless iron held up a full season in someone else's field, he'd think again next year." Saban spread his hands. "It wasn't the money. The money was never the thing. He doesn't trust iron that was poured instead of beaten, and he doesn't trust being the first to find out he was wrong."

Emeric had priced the deal to be irresistible, and it had not occurred to him that the largest man in the valley would find it easy to resist, because the largest man in the valley was not afraid of the price. He was afraid of the same thing the town crowd had been afraid of when they counted instead of cheered, and Emeric had misread that once already.

Some cash in. Not enough. And now he owed delivery besides.

"There's another way," Saban said, and he said it carefully, the way a man lays down a card he'd rather have kept. "You could borrow it. Not from a moneylender in a market. From a house that lends to baronies. House Ducatis."

Emeric knew the shape of the answer before he'd examined it. "No."

"My lord."

"The whole of what we've built here is that it answers to no one. Every coin in Kaldo has been Kaldo's, or mine, and that is the point of it, Saban. That is the strategy. The moment I put my hand to a lender's paper, some house in the capital owns a piece of a place I bled to keep clean." He heard his own voice climbing and stopped it. "There is a reason I have never once done this."

Saban did not argue. He waited, which was worse.

And into the waiting came Bram's voice from the night by the cold iron. You read that furnace to the degree, Emeric. You didn't read one face in that crowd. And under it, newer, the row of the unarmored dead, and the black square where the granary had stood. Half the town had staked everything it owned on him, and then a good part of it had died on his wall, and the rest was going to go into the winter on a harvest that had to come up out of the ground and cover a hole a fire had burned in it. If the money ran out before that harvest came in, if the furnaces went cold and payroll went unmet, it would not be Emeric's independence that paid for it. It would be theirs, again, and he had just watched what it cost when he let his principles be paid for by other people.

"Where," he said, "does one find House Ducatis?"

One did not find House Ducatis. House Ducatis found you, and only if it judged you worth the ride.

Saban explained it on the way back to the hall, and Ferra filled in the rest from a merchant's almanac she kept for reasons of her own. A chartered lending house, older than most of the noble fortunes it lent against, seated in Randillia City and outliving the men who ran the city by design. It had lent to baronies and crowns and outlasted several of both. It did not care whose son you were. It cared, exactly and only, whether it would be paid, and it had a long memory for the difference.

The factor arrived nine days later, a woman the house sent under the name Vantic, and she did not come to attend a prince. She came to appraise an asset. She refused the seat of honor at the hall and asked, before she had taken off her road cloak, to be shown the foundry, the fields, the mine, and the books, in that order, in daylight, on her own feet.

She walked all of it. She stood in the foundry through a full tap and watched the iron run and did not once look impressed, though she asked what the fire stone cost, and how it was replaced, and what happened when it drained, and wrote the answers down. She walked the field lanes and asked what the yield would be, and when Emeric told her, she asked how he knew, and when he said he'd modeled it, she wrote that down too, in a way that did not look like agreement. She went down into the mine far enough to see the thinning seam for herself. She walked the wall, and the graded approach below it, and the black square where the granary had been, and she asked what had happened there, and listened to the answer without any change to her face, and wrote something down that Emeric could not see and did not like the length of. She spent the longest time with Ferra and the ledgers.

That was where the season's one clean piece of luck paid out. Ferra's books were immaculate, every coin in and out of Kaldo since the day Emeric arrived accounted to the copper, and Vantic went through them looking for the lie that was in every set of books she'd ever read, and did not find one. She closed the last ledger and said the only warm thing she said the whole visit, which was, "These are honest. That is rarer than the furnace."

Then she priced him.

Emeric came to the table the way he came to every system, certain he understood it. He laid out the case for Kaldo, and it was a good case, and every part of it was true. The foundry was real and running. The land was fertile and worked. The methods were teachable and already taught. Kaldo had just beaten a coalition three times its strength, which surely counted for something. Kaldo was, over any horizon that mattered, a sound bet, and he made that argument cleanly, and he made the further one underneath it, the weight of royal blood, a prince's attention, the goodwill of a house that helped a son of the King when he needed it.

Vantic agreed with almost all of it. That was the part he had not braced for.

"Your barony is a good bet," she said. "I would not have made the journey if it were not. You have mistaken me, my lord. I am not here to be persuaded that Kaldo will pay in five years. I believe you. I am here to price the chance that Kaldo does not survive the next one." She set a finger on the ledger. "An unproven harvest the whole thing is balanced on, and now with no reserve behind it, because the reserve is a smell in your west quarter. A mine failing under you. A neighbor or three who liked you so little they marched, and lost, and are the sort to try something quieter next. And a borrower who is a third-born son, exiled, with no writ, no seal, no guarantee from any crown that I could take to a magistrate if he defaults. Is there such a guarantee?"

There was not. Harrold's help was real, and it was deniable, which meant that on paper, where houses like Ducatis lived, Emeric stood entirely alone. He could not produce a single line the King would admit to having written.

"Then I lend to the man in front of me," Vantic said, "not to his father's house. And the man in front of me is a good bet who might be dead broke or dead outright by spring." She priced it accordingly, and nothing he said moved a single term, because he kept arguing that the deal was good and she kept not disagreeing, and it took him longer than it should have to understand that being right was not the currency she traded in. She did not price the wall. She never once mentioned the wall, or the four Enhanced men, or the thing that had punched a war-leader off his horse. Those were his strength, and strength was not what she was buying. She priced his weaknesses, one at a time, and the fire had handed her a new one.

The terms were exact, and they were brutal. Interest steep enough that servicing it would eat the first years of surplus. A Ducati's man resident in Kaldo for the life of the loan, with standing right to the books and the works. And the covenant that cut deepest: no new capital works of any size until the debt was brought current. It told a builder he was forbidden to build. Whatever Kaldo did to climb out of this hole, it would have to do without raising a single new thing of stone, which meant it would have to be done in trade, and training, and metallurgy, in the things that made money without looking like a fortress growing, and Emeric understood as he read it that the house had, without meaning to, written the exact shape of the next year of his life. And the security: first claim on the coming harvest and every coin it earned, with the foundry itself standing behind it. If the fields came short of the debt, the thing he had just poured his barony's last into answered for the rest.

He had read the deal correctly and the house not at all. He had believed a good enough bargain would soften them, and they were a house that already knew it was a good bargain and had built their whole terms on the one thing he could not argue away.

He signed.

He read every line twice, and Ferra read them after him, and there was nothing in them to negotiate that had not already been refused. So he took the pen and put his name to a paper that gave a house in the capital a standing hold on the barony he had bled and buried men to keep unheld, and the relief that came with it was real, because payroll would be met and the furnaces would eat and the town's bet would not collapse this month. It was also hollow, because he knew precisely what he had traded for it, and it was the one thing the whole design had been built to keep.

The money came in behind the signature. The colliers were paid. The carters got their earnest, and the first Duvrenne ore turned onto the mountain road inside the fortnight Saban had promised, coming down a road that was not Grayrock's, because Grayrock was not selling to Kaldo anymore and both of them knew why. Payroll met the turn of the month, and Ferra crossed out the last square on the wall and did not draw a new grid, and Emeric understood that too.

But the arithmetic had changed under him. The harvest had never had to do more than survive, before, than feed Kaldo through the winter and put a little by. Now it had to pay a house, and refill a burned granary, and cover a barony that had spent its last on the steel that saved it, and it had to do all three at once. The bet from the cold-iron night had a second creditor now, and this one was watching, resident, with a right to the books, and it had not come to hope alongside him. It had come to be repaid.

He had bought the wait he could not afford. The price of the wait was the one thing the whole design was built to protect, and what he had bought with it was a partner at his own table who would watch the fields as closely as he did, and who, unlike him, was hoping for nothing in particular except to be paid.
