Book 3, Batch 3: Struck

Chapters 19–28 | Monday 1 November, Year 18 – Monday 28 February, Year 19

A mother beast is loose somewhere north of the Isle's shelf, her young have come ashore on two coasts, and the count Emeric named has begun: the first beast part sold by anyone not Kaldo's went across Kesrath's quay for imperial gold. The Assembly has rewritten his beast line against him and he carries it out as written. The Bulwark has closed its hand, stood waist-deep and walked forty paces under a sealed works, its core still not certified. The Isle Works Review owes the first part of its finding in late January. And the money houses have kept their private armies under new names while thirty-one cases crawl through the Inspectorate.

This batch is about money and the law catching up with the king. The Assembly passes the Issue Act, which makes the crown note the realm's one legal paper from a fixed day, and the houses answer with the law itself: a lawsuit, and nineteen hundred calls for coin timed to land before old debts convert. A cheese farmer cannot pay his hands, and a banker risks her own tower to pay them. In Cinderhale the Hearth burns a collector's rolls without asking the woman whose case they were, and the king finally sits in Corrick's hall without a dais.

In the middle of it the review reads part one of its finding, and it costs the king exactly what he did not want to lose: the fast emergency power, and his own oil for ten years. He reads the recommendation against himself to the Assembly word for word, and at the end of February the Assembly strikes the emergency exception by name. From that night, the next emergency belongs to the Assembly.

Chapter 19: The Law of the Note

On Wednesday 17 November, through Vantic's eyes, the Assembly passes the Issue Act. The House's notes have circulated in Kaldo's states for years beside coin; what the Act adds is that the crown note becomes the one legal paper of the realm. It is good for every tax and public fee from Monday 10 January, Year 19, the week a year after the fleet came home. For a hundred and twenty days old contracts written in coin stay in coin: a creditor can demand coin, and a debtor cannot force paper on him. On day 120, Monday 8 May, old debts convert at par and can be paid in notes, provided the note's redemption guarantee holds, meaning notes redeem for coin at any county office on its published days. So one farmer can pay the crown in paper and his seed-merchant in coin in the same week. The House prints under its charter, and the Issue Board takes over the printing at the charter's renewal, as Vantic designed it, writing herself out of the seat. Before the vote a Fairhythe member argues that "the one legal paper" could let a future Board favour some counties or suspend redemption by order, and the bill passes with three additions drafted overnight: the redemption days and the coin held against the notes published each quarter, an independent yearly audit of that cover, and an appeal to Keller's court from any county's refusal to redeem. The March's and Fairhythe's members vote against it, since the note takes Pryor's exchange and Sayer's weigh-house, and so does the mill lord, who votes against everything. It passes by name. Tullio sits in the gallery for the vote, takes no notes and does not speak. Ferra says that is worse.

That month Vantic also hears from the Brask miller's sister, at her own counter, the question the miller asked Tullio in October: whether a rescue that puts his wheel in a stranger's book is a rescue at all. And she still has the two imperial houses' names Tullio gave her, and his unasked warning that one paper under one board is a bad harvest away from ruin. The night after the vote the first proof sheet lies on the House's table: the Board's own rag paper with coloured fibres, a watermark against the lamp, a metal thread woven through, an engraved border you can feel with a thumbnail, Bram's lathe curves, a line of microprint, a serial with a check digit, a mark that glows under the black lamp, and a dust thread that twitches into the same figure every time on the counter's dust plate. The design is the House's engravers', Bram's lathe men's and Ferra's, and Emeric's one addition is the check digit. Vantic asks what a forger would try first. Ferra says the thing they did not think of.

In November, under the covered berth, Tess's remap splits the Bulwark's grip into fingers, and Wren curls one finger and only that finger moves. Her half-second has shortened, not gone, and Imani's hour-for-an-hour rule holds. Tess asks who can certify the arm. Only the sworn panel can, and it has not sat, so a clean demonstration is not a certificate, and "not certified" stays on the card.

Through Merryn Pellow's eyes, the first frost comes on the marsh and nothing moves. With the Isle's shard fields fenced, every store on the March and the Isle in iron, and the cold, the young find little, stop growing and sit; some move inland along the peat creeks, some go back to the sea, and some starve. None reaches the second stage. The pups' tracks stop at iron doors. On Saturday 20 November Oskel's men kill one at the cutters' camp on Duvrenne's half, and its parts are sold on Kesrath's quay on the 27th, sale three in Ferra's column. On Monday 22 November the Saltreach chandlers' guild, through its elected sealer Wat Greave, who has sealed ship's stores for twenty years and sells tar by the cask, files at the Inspector's door the first application to seal a beast's heart in a house the crown does not own.

Chapter 20: Whatever's on the Forms

On Sunday 5 December, at her mill house in Cinderhale, through Mabel's eyes, her brother Fulk Garside comes for his birthday with a gift he could not have afforded two years ago: a cast-iron weighing scale for her flour counter, Dunholt-made, with a brass pan, the kind she has priced three times and never bought. It was paid for with the autumn night-gang bonus Wystan's works paid in November, which is a legitimate reason. When the mill whistle goes he glances at the door, and he answers too quickly when she asks what Wystan's works is making: whatever's on the forms. She does not ask again. He re-hangs the sluice-gate hinge on the mill's leat, which has needed doing since spring, without asking, and she lets him, because he is good with water. She takes the gift. After he has gone she weighs a sack of the mill's flour on it twice, then against her own weights, and it is true. Then she sits up with her slate, working out how much coin she will need to keep back when the note comes, because a scale is a thing you buy when you know what next year looks like, and a foreman's wages are a thing you have when someone is paying you not to ask. She does not write that down.

On Thursday 9 December Merryn Pellow shoots one of the two lost pups in the salt marsh behind Saltreach, a hundred yards from where it was last seen in September.

On Friday 3 December Alina brings Kesrath's letter to Kaldo: leave to read the tideshell's core, not to sell it. Duvrenne's fishers lost a cutters' camp to the first one and want to know whether more are coming, but Kesrath's council will not sell the first mature core anyone has held to the crown that has just been told it cannot keep its own oil, and says so in a polite sentence. The same letter names the fear underneath: a Kaldo that holds every mature core will decide which crowns get machines. On Tuesday 14 December, in the Claim's iron shed, through Tess's eyes, the case is opened under its seal's number in front of Oskel and Alina, and she reads it through the sealed reader stone. The mark says born, under the Isle, the word she still cannot read, and a fourth form she has not seen: the body sign joined to a sign the core uses elsewhere for water moving against stone. In plain words, it was not made to be a tideshell; it became one because of where it grew. From one core that is as much as she can say, so she tests it against the two September pups' marks, which have born and under the Isle and no shore sign, as fits young that have not grown the part that reads the shore. It is consistent, not proof, and she writes it as an inference until a second mature core is read. The core knows swimming. Copies go to Kesrath and Kaldo as the custody requires, and Oskel makes her read the copy list aloud before the seal goes back on; when Kaldo's name comes before Duvrenne's, he asks Alina which order the leave was written in, she says Duvrenne's, and he has the clerk redo the list. The part bearing on the hollow goes to the review under seal as paper twenty-one. The core goes back in its case under a new seal, Duvrenne's. That evening Tess tells Emeric it is not Ward's kind of heart: it is the sea kind, the one for Zale's machine. Emeric tells Ward himself. Ward says then he will wait for one of his kind. Nobody says they hope one comes.

Chapter 21: The Long Table

On Saturday 25 December, in the castle's long hall, through Imani's eyes, Kaldo holds its first Long Table at home since the voyage. The six are there; Amara, in a body that does not eat, makes a show of sitting with a plate anyway; Harrold; Corbin; Tess and Osric; Wren; Maeve; the Crestless; Zale and Ilenne up from Aumere, Zale back at the seat he had as the first Long Table's guest years ago; Waylon and Alina from the Isle, married four months; and Runa, at her first at home. Her first was on the ice under the aurora, where nobody gave presents, and she does not understand why everyone gives each other things, and then is overwhelmed when she gets some.

At Aumere a gift given at table is a debt to be repaid in kind before the year turns, so Ilenne brings her gift with a written tally of what she expects back. Zale laughs and does not take the tally from her hand. Harrold asks what the count is for, and she says she wants to know what this table expects of her before she sits down. It is a fair question, and nobody answers it at dinner. Emeric gives Tess a set of precision files from the Forge with her name cut in the tang. Ferra gives Emeric an empty ledger with a note: for something that isn't work. Imani gives Ferra a tin of the mint she chews when she is counting and will not admit to. Waylon gives Alina a sand-glass he made himself, badly, on the Isle, which she keeps. Runa, told to give someone something, has put it off all week. Low, she asks Imani whether she will be expected to do this every year. Imani says she will be expected to turn up. Runa gives her a brass compass she mended at the school bench and tested on the tablecloth, and says it works, and that she wanted to give Imani something that works. Imani has to leave the table for a minute. By the window, while the pudding is argued over, Ferra asks whether the ward will spare Imani a week in the spring. Imani says she will ask only if Ferra asks properly. Ferra does. Their hands are on the same sill, and neither moves hers.

Through Emeric's eyes, he tries to steal both of them off to the stair before the pudding. Ferra says the pudding first. Imani says the pudding and the toast. He says that is two things. Ferra says she can count. Alina has brought the words of a Duvrenne cradle song in her own hand for Wren, and stays to hear Wren sing it after the pudding. Nobody at the table mentions the note, the review or the young. Harrold raises a glass to whoever is sitting in that room next month, and the table drinks to it without asking what he means.

Harrold stands to give his toast, and for the length of it he is in the partial form: wings half open over the table, light coming off his scales so the candles look pointless, the glass in a clawed hand. It came at the start of December on the bay shore after eight months of the king's lamp, by which time he was coming back in minutes, and he has told nobody. Then he puts it down and sits. Amara is the only one not surprised. Waylon, who has had his partial form since 29 October and has also told nobody, says nothing, and Alina kicks him under the table.

Chapter 22: Day One

On Monday 3 January the first clinic bands go on the four flecked crewmen, turning the register's monthly readings into a week's worth a day: pulse, blood oxygen, skin temperature and a fall log. Imani's register shows their density steady since the work stopped, with no reversal, and the suspected column unchanged. All four said yes first, and one asks whether the band will tell him when he is ill before he knows. She tells him what it measures. On Friday 14 January a starved pup is found curled in a Saltreach lime kiln's cold draw-hole, its back crystal dull and its gut empty of stone, the first proof that they starve. On Friday 21 January the Inspector licenses the Saltreach chandlers' guild after inspecting it twice. The king does not like a guild that sells ship's stores holding cores, and signs the shut cases over at cost anyway, because the law says at cost and the guild met the standard. Ferra notes it is the first licence the crown did not want to give and gave. She asks him which of his two objections, the tar stored beside a core or the end of the crown's monopoly, he actually means. He says both, which is true.

On Monday 10 January, day one, in the League's hall in Cinderhale, through Maudie's eyes, the terms are on the wall and the League's chest takes notes for real dues at face and nothing else. A man tries to pay a year's dues ahead in notes he bought cheap, and Maudie refuses him and can say why: notes bought at seven parts in eight would hand him one-eighth of the chest's own loss, because the chest redeems at face. He says he earned the notes, so the money is his. She says it is, and the one-eighth is not. A stranger who wants coin out of the chest is refused too. Mabel's slate is on the counter. A carter pays. A baker posts his price, and a family buys a loaf at the posted price with paper.

That day, at the records hall, through Ferra's eyes, the first reports come in by set. At the Gate Pryor's changers take the note at seven parts in eight, posted. Sayer's weigh-house at Fairhythe holds grain settlements until the paper is proved. The harbour duke at Halverne takes it at face from the first hour, because he gains from paper, which shows the money interest is not one bloc. Tullio files suit in Keller's court that conversion takes a creditor's contracted right, and at Ferra's suggestion Kaldo News prints it whole, because the king wants it argued in public, not muttered. His lawyer's example is printed beside it: a widow who lent her brother coin on a note repayable in coin would be paid in notes at par on a Tuesday, when the county office does not redeem, and would wait until Thursday for the coin her brother promised. It is a sympathetic creditor's loss, not a pretext. Day one is also the card bank's last issue day: from now every new account is a band account, cards already out are honoured to the end of Year 20 and then work only as visitor passes, and nobody's money moves. Every Board counter has a dust plate and a printed card, Feel. Look. Plate., and the schools read it aloud that week. That evening Emeric is about to build a speech on Cinderhale's loaf. Ferra says one town; don't build a speech on it.

On Tuesday 11 January, in the crown book's counting house in the capital, through Tullio's eyes, he calls nineteen hundred harvest notes in coin, still payable in coin inside the window, on a thirty-day demand falling due on Thursday 10 February, about seven thousand four hundred Gold Rand in all. Every one is lawful. He signs them in the order the farms lie on the map and tells his clerk, not unkindly, that he is not doing anything the note did not invite.

Chapter 23: Part One

On Friday 28 January, in the review's room at the records hall, through Emeric's eyes, the room holds Keller; the mill lord, the crews' man and the third delegate; Mother Sabbe; the orc families' empty chair; Duvrenne's observer; the families' bench with Dacey at its end; the king and Harrold in the front row as signatories; Ferra in the public gallery, as anyone may sit; Honor Linden copying; and Kaldo News. Keller reads the finding whole, as promised. He gives the facts in order: the clause, the exception under it, the charges, the datum moved forty yards, the seal already gone by the time of the stop order, the waking. The clause made the charges possible but moved no datum; the finding names the crew that moved it and the officer who signed the charges, and says the fourteen died of what those people did under the clause. The cause was the charges fired under the exception. Then part one. First, the review recommends that the emergency exception be struck from the crown's prerogative, so that no crown may order or continue any works or move any force under a claim of emergency without the Assembly's own authorisation. Second, the crown's share of the Isle works is held in trust for ten years for the families of the fourteen and the Islanders, with Mother Sabbe, two members chosen by the families' bench, and Keller's clerk as keeper of the books for trustees, and the awards to each family are paid from the crown's own purse besides, within thirty days. Not yet: the hollow, which waits for part two and the orc families' member.

Keller asks whether the crown has anything to say before the finding is entered. The king says it once. The first part takes away the fast power in a year when four armed houses are testing the Act and a letter has told him to fear more than one room; the second costs Kaldo its own oil for a decade. He does not name what he truly fears, which nobody in the room knows of. He says the review has the facts right and the remedy is slower than he would make it, and that he will keep every line. He also argues, not well, that the review judged the charges by what the fourteen became and not by what the order said when it was given. Keller answers that the review judged the order by its words, and the minute says so. Emeric says a minute is the review's, not the crown's, and sits. The finding is entered.

Through Dacey's eyes, she had expected to feel something and feels tired. Bryn's name is in it, in Keller's voice. She is a trustee because the families asked her. Before signing, the trustees must decide how an award is valued, by the wage in the works' pay book or by a flat sum per family. Dacey votes for the pay book, which pays Bryn's award more than the flat sum and pays a casual hand's widow, who has no book, the flat sum. She says before the vote that she knows Bryn's book better than anyone on the bench, and the bench counts her vote anyway. Mother Sabbe votes for the flat sum and loses by one. Dacey signs because the others asked her to, and walks back to her lodging the long way, past the First Room, without going in. That afternoon the king and Harrold sign the trust deed before Keller's clerk, and the publication order goes to Kaldo News.

That night, in their room, Emeric asks Imani a question he carried out of the review's room and did not say there: what would she do if the next emergency came faster than a committee sits. He does not finish the sentence. Imani says a day late is better than a day wrong, which is what happened to the fourteen. Ferra says nothing and holds his hand, and he lies awake longer than they do.

On Tuesday 1 February, through Corbin's eyes, the king stands at the crown's place on the Assembly's floor and reads the recommendation word for word from the printed finding. He does not argue for it or against it, since the review bound him to put it, not to plead it. A member asks whether the crown wants it struck. He says the crown wants what the Assembly decides, and that his own view is printed in the review's minute book for anyone who wants it. Corbin moves it to committee with a vote fixed for the 28th. From that day the older bores run again, now that the finding is in, and the crown's share of their oil goes to the trust. Ferra's first purchase of the trust's oil for Kaldo's tanks is entered at Duvrenne's quay price, the same she has paid since July, and she tells him it is the first time she has bought his own oil off him, and that she will be doing it for ten years. On Monday 7 February the first award is paid from the crown's purse by the Treasury's ordinary warrant. The king has read a recommendation against his own power word for word and sat down without telling anyone what he thinks of it.

On Sunday 30 January, through Emeric's eyes, he takes Runa into the delving space and says he'll help her remember. He asks her to describe one of the men on her list and lets the man form from her words, then leads her one small suggestion at a time until the man looks different and she agrees that's how he looked. He never touches her real memory; only the shared picture moves. She's furious and runs to a high ledge on the north tower. He lands behind her and apologises for the trick, not the lesson. She asks whether he forgets people, and he tells her there were things about his mother he lost without noticing until he got them back. Her real fear comes out: that Veya's face is drifting too. So they get it out of her head while it's strong. On the Tuesday the school's drawing master comes, and she corrects him until it's right. She walks down from the ledge, then decides it's far and rides down on his back.

Chapter 24: The Rolls

In the second half of January, through Maudie's eyes, Ezra Pelham's agents in Cinderhale demand coin for dues already paid. Maudie's written complaint goes to the mill lords' clerk on Monday 17 January and is acknowledged the same day. Pelham's agents keep demanding for two more weeks, and by the end of the month Maudie has thirty-one named tenants for her case. On Thursday 3 February, in the League's hall, she hears from a runner that the rolls are going into the courthouse hearth that night, and that nobody asked her.

That night, through Kezia's eyes, the Hearth's crew works by the shuttered lantern they have used since the watch house, and Kezia checks every bundle by its spine label before it goes in. Only Pelham's working collection rolls burn. The assessment register and the tenure books are left on the clerk's table with a cloth over them so the smoke does not stain them. Before the fire Kezia says that Maudie, who needs these rolls to win, was not asked. Corrick says Maudie would have objected, and they go ahead anyway. One bundle is labelled as Pelham's collection, but its slips are in a tenant's hand. Kezia pulls it from the pile, and it goes to the League's own box for Maudie to sort. Who chose the pages? We did. Who is we?

On Friday morning, 4 February, in a separate case, Keller's court upholds conversion at par, narrowly and conditionally: redemption is part of making the creditor whole, so a creditor can still get relief if redemption fails. Tullio bows to the bench and leaves without looking at his sister. Vantic reads the part about redemption twice, because it is the part that binds her. Mira's squad killed a pup on the Claim's north beach on 2 February, and the Register reads it in. That morning Maudie, furious, is out in Cinderhale's kitchens with her satchel, recovering slips one tenant at a time. Tench's neighbour is relieved the rolls are gone. Both of them are right.

Chapter 25: Friday

On Monday 7 February, in the long room, Keller advises on general reforms, which are announced, and the circuit's first Cinderhale sitting is set for Friday 25 February. On Wednesday 9 February the crews' man brings a standstill bill: no new coin demand on a harvest note inside the window, from the bill's passing to day 120. It cannot reach a demand already made, since a law undoing a lawful call after it was served would be the taking Tullio's suit was about. It will take nine days, the Coinage committee holding it three, and the king cannot make it faster. On Thursday 10 February nineteen hundred farms on the western plains owe coin, coin goes short, and Fairhythe's holds and the March's discount make it worse. Ferra's map of the county redemption offices shows the problem in one line: they redeem on Thursdays, and the next is a week off.

Through Tom Ashlock's eyes, he delivers his cheese on Thursday on a cartage invoice to the shipper for six hundred and forty Gold Rand in coin, due Monday 14 February, written inside the window, so the shipper owes coin and cannot discharge it in notes without Tom's agreement. On Monday the shipper offers notes, and Tom refuses. His two hands and his wholesaler need coin on Friday.

Through Vantic's eyes, she opens her House's window on Monday 14 February, because the bill will take nine days and the calls are four days past due. It buys verified unpaid claims once each, after her clerk's five checks, at face less a disclosed one-in-a-hundred fee. On day two, Tuesday, Tom's claim is bought for six hundred and thirty-three Gold Rand and sixty in coin; five hundred and fifty go straight across her clerk's counter to Tom's wholesaler and his two hands, Tom gets the rest, and the assignment notice goes to the shipper. A Halverne factor is refused the same morning, because he holds bearer notes and no claim; he calls it a banker's trick, and her clerk shows him the rule on the wall. Her senior clerk, who sits on the House's minority creditors' council, objects that the ring fence is only as good as an auditor who is not hers. Vantic signs every assignment notice in her own name, so the minority creditors' share is hers to answer for, and tells him so. Her glass tower is at risk. On Wednesday Emeric comes to her counting house. He argued against the window in November, fearing one House becoming the Board, and the Brask delegate said the same in the Assembly on Monday. He tells her he was wrong about the week and right about the danger, and offers a ceiling on any single claim. She says her clerk's five checks already set one, that he is late, and that he is right about one thing this month and she would like him not to spend it on her ledger. She knows the danger; that is why the window shuts on the thirtieth day.

On Wednesday 16 February, at her mill, through Mabel's eyes, a merchant settles with her in two parts, a coin portion and a note portion, and gives a legitimate reason for the split. On Friday 18 February, at six, Tom Ashlock pays his two hands in coin. He does not know whose it is.

Chapter 26: Four Chairs

On Monday 14 February, the same Monday the window opens, the March's reform committee's petition is read in the Assembly, signed in the March's reeves' houses, Agatha Vell's name third, asking for guaranteed seats and a referendum. On Wednesday 16 February, in the March's barn hall, through Agatha's eyes, the committee holds an open meeting with no vote and no minute. A pensioner asks who pays the widows when the reeve's book comes up short; Agatha does not know for certain, and he says Kaldo will not. A toll worker says she wants seats she can vote out of office. A man at the back says a Kaldo majority could abolish the charter and nobody in the hall could stop it. Agatha writes all three down and does not tidy them.

On Monday 21 February the coalition offers four elected seats and a rights hearing, without waiting for the state map, because Pryor's pass guards hold the only road to Ostrel and nobody wants to find out what they would do with it. Corbin drafts it, and in the Assembly the crews' man says on the record why the seats are being offered. On Wednesday 23 February, in Pryor's hall at the Gate, Pryor refuses to let the March vote on it. His reasons are not stupid, and he answers the pensioner's question from the meeting: the winter the Cinderhale mill's stores failed, the charter's chest paid the widows out of the Gate's own grain. He will not let them trade three hundred years of protection for four chairs. Agatha says they were never his years to trade. He remembers her husband's name again as she goes, and she wishes he had not.

On Thursday 24 February, in Cinderhale, through Kezia's eyes, Corrick pins the March's offer beside July's minute deferring Maudie's petition and writes nothing under either. People stop and read. Kezia watches a carter read both twice and say, to nobody, so that's what it takes.

Chapter 27: The Hour

On Friday 25 February the circuit sits at Cinderhale's courthouse with the hearth still black. Through Maudie's eyes, she stands at the table where the rolls burned and argues from the register they left, and wins her first order for her thirty-one named tenants: the stay, Pelham bound, and the valid dues kept.

On Saturday 26 February, in the morning, the king comes to the League's hall in Cinderhale with Ferra and no dais. Through Emeric's eyes, Gethin will not come in. Emeric sees the new channels in his rebuilt hand and asks who rebuilt it. Gethin says a doctor in Kaldo who did not care where he came from, charged him two coppers, and said that if he came back with the same burns she would leave the fingers off. Emeric looks at the hand a moment longer and says that doctor is his wife. Ferra says one of them. Emeric says she did not say that. Gethin says she said it with her face. Emeric hears the question as he asks it: it is a medical question, and it is not his to ask. That evening Imani gives him by set the answer she gave Gethin: he was a patient, and that is all Emeric gets. He takes it.

The hall's door stands open by its own rule, and forty people stand on the step outside; a carter at the back cannot see the table and says so. Corrick lays out the documents: the unanswered complaint, the Charter, the far court's schedule, the south-road deferral, and the note naming Wayford instead of Cinderhale's observers. He says the king chose Wayford, from a ship, without anyone here able to answer, and was wrong. The king says yes to each, and that he was wrong about what the decision would cost them. Corrick sets out his two doors, aimed at the crown: rise to be a lord inside the same order, or wait for the next heir raised in the lord's world, and he asks which door he is meant to wait at. Emeric knows the criticism is right, and has not told anyone what he means to do about hereditary rule. Corrick lays the south-road deferral beside the March's offer of four seats: the March has arms, and Cinderhale has signatures and a committee minute. Emeric accepts that his coalition has taught a dangerous lesson. Corrick asks for a seat for Cinderhale by royal order, and the king refuses, because a royal grant to one town while every other barony on the south road waits would be exactly the favour Corrick opposes; the March's offer was a ransom, and he will not pay a second one to a town that never pointed a gun. Corrick asks instead for representation by a general law, and gets the king's admission that the state map has slipped twice. Corrick admits he cannot speak for every tenant on the list or for the members who never came to a meeting. Emeric admits the general law will take a year, and that a year suits the crown better than it suits a tenant. Corrick says he had noticed. Then Emeric speaks about the night the watch house was struck. He admires the unglamorous work in the report: a failed first attempt, people checked again, a small footprint, nobody innocent hurt, and an apology to the employee disturbed at night. He tells Corrick that, aside from the considerable damage he has been doing to the kingdom, he has a great deal of respect for him, and that he does not know who did it, because Keller would want to know how he knew. Corrick nearly gives himself away, saying you do not get a lane right in fewer than nine nights, and Emeric lets it pass. Corrick asks who gives the king the right to forgive it. No one, Emeric says; he is not forgiving it, that is for the people it fell on and for the court. Then Corrick asks what keeps the next king from taking it all back. Not enough. Not yet. Corrick puts one proposal, written down: the circuit reads the reform list aloud at the first sitting of every king, and any reversal needs the Assembly's word. Emeric will not promise the next king will keep it. He says the Assembly's word is the only enforcement he can offer, and that is not enough either. They part with respect and no forgiveness.

Outside on the hall's step, through Ferra's eyes, she tells him to say what he means to do with the crown, and he will not. She tells him that then Corrick has nothing to hold him to. He says he knows. The silence is understood, and it does damage.

Chapter 28: Struck

On Monday 28 February, through the crews' man's eyes, the Assembly debates the emergency exception for one day. Arguing to keep it is a member from the Tarrow whose town's quay is Sayer's: her porters stand on it in new coats every morning, and he would like the crown to be able to move fast if they stop standing. It is an honest fear, and the chamber treats it as one. The crews' man, a delegate on the review, answers that if they keep it for the houses they keep it for the next king, and the one after. The mill lord votes with a majority for once. Corbin says the crown has put it as it promised.

Through Emeric's eyes, the exception is struck by name, the March's and Fairhythe's members with the majority this time for their own reasons. The emergency committee's procedure, Corbin's draft, passes the same sitting: the crown or any member may ask the committee for an authorisation, the committee reports to the floor the next sitting day, and the floor debates and decides. From tonight, the next emergency over a works or a force is the Assembly's. The king sits in the crown's place through the vote and does not move. When it is done he signs the crown's notice of the change in front of the clerk and walks out the members' door, not the crown's. At the door a clerk tells him that the Treasury's request to shore the Tarrow quay before the spring flood, drafted that morning as an emergency, must now wait for the committee. He says he knows; he read the draft that morning. Ferra is waiting at the bottom of the steps. She says, "That's one." He knows what she means, Corrick's question about what keeps the next king from taking it back, and he says yes. They walk home.

Where the Story Stands After Chapter 28

The Isle Works Review's first finding is entered and obeyed. The emergency exception is struck by the Assembly, and from 28 February any emergency over a works or a force needs the Assembly's own authorisation through its committee. The crown's share of the Isle works is held in trust for ten years for the families of the fourteen and the Islanders, the awards are being paid from the crown's purse, and Ferra buys Kaldo's own oil back from the trust at Duvrenne's price. Part two, the hollow, waits for the orc families' member in the spring. Ferra's year under censure ended on 23 February, and her power is whole.

The crown note became the realm's one paper on 10 January, and old debts convert on 8 May if redemption holds. Keller's court has upheld conversion at par, conditionally. Tullio's nineteen hundred lawful calls fell due on 10 February and dried up coin on the western plains. The standstill bill can only stop new calls, and Vantic's window, open until 14 March, is buying verified claims at the risk of her own tower. Tom Ashlock has paid his hands without knowing whose coin it was, and the king has admitted to Vantic that he was wrong about the week and right about the danger. In Cinderhale the Hearth burned Pelham's rolls without asking Maudie, she won her order anyway, and the king sat with Corrick without a dais and offered him nothing to hold him to. The March refused four seats because Pryor would not let it vote, and Corrick has pinned the offer beside the deferral.

The young have had a hard winter: twelve killed, one starved, none past the first stage, the mother unseen, and Kaldo still does not know how many there were. The Saltreach chandlers hold the first private sealing licence. The tideshell's core is read, resealed and still Duvrenne's: it became a tideshell because of where it grew, it knows swimming, and it is not Ward's kind, so Ward waits. The Bulwark now moves one finger at a time and is still not certified. Fulk Garside gave his sister a scale he should not be able to afford and told her his works makes whatever's on the forms. The count stood at day 157 when the exception was struck, with four sales, and the switch still holds.

This batch is about the king being bound by what he built and choosing to stay bound. He disliked every ruling that went against him, said so once, and then kept each line, read the recommendation against his own power word for word, and walked out through the members' door. The cost of doing it the slow way falls on ordinary people in the meantime, and the batch keeps a careful count of who pays it.
