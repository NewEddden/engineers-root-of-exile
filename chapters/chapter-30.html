Book 3, Batch 11: Dates

Chapters 95-104 | Wednesday 1 May, Year 21 - Wednesday 25 December, Year 21

The Mage Corps is working across the kingdom on a budget the Assembly trimmed. Every district is on a quarterly ledger that calls the kingdom's service uneven, and the king has learned to stop promising what nobody can staff. Television reaches about ninety sets. The first highway is being cut from Kaldo to Milford. The maglev has run its first mile. Something came out of the sea at Aumere in March and killed three people, and the merfolk queen asked whether it was Kaldo's. Nobody could prove it wasn't. The far board holds one pin, and Ferra's interval chart one dot.

This is the first batch that covers a whole year. Each chapter keeps one or two dated scenes, a month or more apart, and opens with what moved in between, seen by whoever is telling it. The year belongs to the things Kaldo built and the people who run them: a runway, a liner, a cutting through a ridge nobody could cut, a test track, a ride room. One of those institutions catches the king out. The speed he asked for breaks a bridge, and the inquiry that finds out why names his own promise among the causes.

And his mother, who has ridden a golem for seven years while her own body mended under glass, stands up.

Chapter 95: The Field

On Thursday 2 May, through Sorrel's eyes, the Field has grown. The new concrete laid since March runs the strip out to three kilometres over the old grass at the west end, into the wind over the farm belt and the Mere, and the lamp line along it was tested the night before, every lamp. Hangars stand beside the long shed. At nine the terminal opens: a long, low hall of glass and brick with ticket desks that have sold no tickets yet, a waiting hall nobody is waiting in, and band gates that open for a citizen's band and ring for a visitor's yellow one. The first person through is a carter's boy, who walks in and out four times to hear it.

That morning a guard frame stands alone at the west fence while its rider is at breakfast. From this summer the wall's frames carry their first chip controller, so a frame left at a gate holds still, turns towards anything that moves inside the fence, sounds its horn and calls the tower. Its first challenger is a Penhallow farm lad over the fence at dawn to see the jets. The frame turns, honks and calls the tower, and the lad sits down on the grass to wait for someone to come and be angry with him. Sorrel is angry with him, then shows him the hangar. Then she walks the whole three kilometres before she will let anything land on the new end, as she walked the first two. The jets still burn the refinery's kerosene from the Isle's oil; fuel made from Milford's crops comes next year, and she says so when a clerk asks her about the king's fuel rule.

On Saturday 4 May, through Mira's eyes, the first Outrider flies. Mira sits in the front seat with the back seat ballasted, and Maeve flies chase off the wing in her suit, as the two of them have chased every test since the Bulwark's first walks. The jet takes off long and climbs hard. At altitude Mira tries the chroma skin once, and Maeve tells her over the set that she has gone the colour of the clouds and it's rude. The sky watch has her on radar the whole way. Twenty-eight minutes, empty bays, and nothing fired.

A week later, through Maeve's eyes, the second Outrider is hers, and Mira takes the chase in the first. For the first time the chase is a jet, and the suits stay on their racks in the hangar. What Maeve says to Mira over the set is private and plain, and the tower hears it anyway.

Chapter 96: Fares

On Saturday 15 June, through Ferra's eyes, her ledger holds the gap: the last of the hundred and forty drums of the relay net's first phase pinned at the end of May; the third generation of the citizen's band going first to the Works' scaffold crews, because nobody works a high scaffold without a band, and a band that can say where its wearer is when he squeezes for help belongs on the people who fall; a phone in every town hall of the crown demesne and the four states, and the kingdom's maps beginning to say states, cities and towns instead of baronies; the first six menders qualified, Bettany first; the bill for the first myoelectric arm fitted at the Medical Pass Office, on a docker who lost his forearm at Stannary and can close the new hand on a cup; the Library's walls up to the roofline.

Then the K Sorrel carries her first paying passengers to Randillia City. She flies unescorted, because the whole route lies inside the sky watch's chain, and nobody explains why. Harrold and Rowena are aboard, with Emeric and Imani, Corbin, and about two hundred people who bought the first tickets at the terminal's desks. Sorrel flies right seat beside the captain she trained, still mortified and furious that the hull carries her name. Ferra counts the fares, finds the tin one coin over, and spends the flight working out why: a girl paid for her grandmother, who had a pass. The trip takes about an hour and a quarter. The rail takes about a day.

On the old capital's new field Weekes meets them on the apron, and the capital's papers ask the king when the Milford road will open. Emeric is happy. The flight went perfectly, his father is laughing with Rowena, and he says: "The first Monday in September, for the harvest trucks." The highway's own office had said autumn. Ferra hears him say it in front of the papers and writes it in her book.

On Monday 17 June, through Emeric's eyes, the hundred seeds set aside in April are offered over the sets, in Ilenne's order first: a grove's seeds, soaked, for a people's own ground, by its own council's leave, guarded under its own law, with Kaldo's custody book signed at both ends. Each council answers for itself over the week. The merfolk say yes to twenty, for the caldera's rim where their old shore grove stood. The dwarves say yes to twenty, fast, because every golem mind they ever made was bought from the elves one stone at a time; Jofrid asks whether Kaldo will teach the reading of the stones, and the answer is no, and the seeds go anyway. The orcs' treaty-house votes yes to twenty for the river farms, and Kettrin reads the roll out name by name. The Sabir water court takes ten, for the old canal ground, because it remembers that the groves and the Common's water works burned together. The werewolf packs say not yet: a grove is a thing you stand beside for a lifetime, and the packs move. The Murr say no. The winged ones say no, because they won't put a thing on their stacks that men have to guard with guns, and Kaldo doesn't ask twice. The dragons are told rather than asked, and Sohra says she'll tell her grandmother.

Seventy go south. Thirty stay in Ferra's book for the elves and the next voyage's peoples. Ward's cap on the soak racks is about the Watch posts on Kaldo's own ground, and ground abroad is its own people's to guard, so Ward agrees to a third rack run in July. Emeric gets his third run after all, for somebody else's land, and Ferra notes that he is happier about it than he was about the second fruit.

Chapter 97: Against the Shovels

On Monday 8 July, through Tess's eyes, the planting is done, about thirteen hundred trees now stand, the first grow tower turns its carousel beside the dock ration hall, and Tess's first farm robot picks lettuce off the trays on a program Ada wrote, at a speed Hollis calls insulting. The Corps numbers about a hundred and ninety after the summer's hiring. On Ferra's desk the July ledger shows Tarrowmede's hill parishes blank again, because no Corps crew goes onto a duke's land without the duke, and the duke has answered nothing since the Titles Act.

Tess is at Fallow Bank, the long grade on Lord Lisle's side that the old Wayford line has climbed with a banker behind every grain train since the first rail was laid, the one ridge everyone said was too broad to cut. The Capital Line is cutting it. Four crews of the Corps' earth mages work the east face in shifts; a steam-shovel gang with powder works the west, because the line has to be buildable with no mage free. Tess and two of her people time both faces with stopwatches and a chain for a week. The mages move rock about two and a half times faster. Then the honest part: both faces are waiting on the same thing, the spoil wagons, and for half of every shift the mages stand. Dagny, from Bram's Wayford team, runs a belt conveyor across the next hollow so the spoil goes straight into the embankment the line needs on the far side and never sees a wagon. It's mundane, it runs off a portable dynamo, and it doubles the Corps' useful hours. Nobody asks the king. What doesn't speed up are the drains, the slope's angle and the inspector's signature: a mage can cut a slope steep, but only a season of rain says whether it will stand. Two of the four crews came here from the Milford road's bridge. Tess doesn't know yet why that matters.

When she gets back to Kaldo there is a second pin on the far board. On Friday night a Breacher came up at the Sea Hold's sea gate, and the dwarves shut their door on it and lost the quay. Jofrid gave no dead. Ferra's second dot sits under the first, and under it in Ferra's hand is Jofrid's last line: the Sea Hold's smiths are building. Not what. Tess, who knows what dwarves build when they are frightened, says "wardens" to an empty room.

On Thursday 18 July, through the eyes of Albin Keswick, a revenue clerk of the Imperial Core posted to its legation in the old capital, Kaldo comes in through a train window: glass towers inside an old stone ring, the needle tower's frame still climbing with a crane on top, the blue dome at the centre. He notes the towers and moves on. He has come to see whether the south coast's customs returns are lying. At a market counter he buys lunch and is handed a printed receipt off a till: the date, the price, the tax line, the shop's number. Every sale in this city leaves a paper behind, and the Empire still collects its revenue by guesswork and tax farmers. He keeps it.

In the port's customs hall the books are public, so he reads them. The returns aren't lying, they're understating: freight through Kaldo has doubled in two years. Among the outbound lines is a clinic kit, sold at cost, consigned to Brannick's Ford in Velmoor, and he copies it out in full. On the inbound passenger lists from three crowns are nurses, machinists and their families, dozens a month. His courier rode north the day before with his first dispatch, as the Empire's rules require. Keswick writes his second report in Kaldo's post office, posts it on the mail train out of curiosity, and takes the next morning's train himself. His posted report is on the legation's desk a day and a half before his courier rides in. He writes that sentence into the copy for the Core and underlines it.

Chapter 98: Both Lifts

On Saturday 3 August, through Bram's eyes, South Sentinel's Shell has been lit, the first beyond Kaldo's walls, and his spire crews are home. Stannary's new pumps have let the miners down below the old water, and a crate of what they call pitch, a heavy black ore that leaves the stones near it cloudy and dead, came through his rail yard on its way to Armand. The seventy seeds have gone south on the summer hull in sealed tins under two signatures, and in the same hold went the Register's new pages, more shut cases, and the running blade Bram drew for the peg boy at the orc coast years ago, made at last now that the carbon fibre line exists.

On the twenty-mile test track across the Works Town flats, the car runs on both lifts at about a hundred and fifty miles an hour, and from the control hut Tess pulls the sealed stones' feed. The magnets hold her. The gap reading doesn't move. On Monday she runs it again and cuts the electromagnets. The stones carry the car unevenly, and as she slows she settles onto her skids at walking pace at the far end, exactly as designed and exactly the thing Bram was most afraid of. He has spent forty years watching things fail. He watches the needle, not the car, and breathes out, and pretends he hadn't stopped.

On Saturday 10 August, through Osric's eyes, the first passengers ride, and Tess's family go first: Bram, Ferra, Osric, Emeric and Imani in the second row, and Runa, fourteen, who has asked every day since the mile loop and is allowed because Ferra says so. Twenty miles out and back at a speed Osric doesn't believe the needle about. Runa presses her face to the window, then flattens her ears at a hum she says is too high and nobody else can hear. At the station Osric hears that Hester's herds by the Milford closes came in heavier at the weigh and her dogs won't lie down inside the fence. The Fairhythe chandler from last year's seed case comes out of Turnstone in eleven days, and Osric means to have a man at the gate to see who meets him. Tess drives. Osric watches her drive it, and that's the scene.

Chapter 99: Staging

On Thursday 22 August, through the eyes of Hattie Roan, a crane driver from the Works Town, the Milford road is laid all the way back to Kaldo except the bridge, and the harvest wagons are already queuing on the old ford road below. The digits on her new band tell her the time and how far she has walked, which she thinks is nobody's business. She is lifting concrete for the bridge's deck.

The deck was booked for the third week of September. After the king's date in June, the highway office moved the pour a month forward so the road could open on Monday 2 September. Two of the bridge's four Corps crews were already at Fallow Bank. The temporary staging that holds the wet concrete until it cures went up faster, with fewer hands, and the independent check of its design and bracing, a second engineer's signature required before any temporary works carry load, was postponed, because the checker was at Fallow Bank. The resident engineer wrote "checked" himself and meant to have it checked after. On Tuesday Hattie told the foreman the bracing on the second span looked light. He said it was signed.

At about two in the afternoon the second span's staging gives. A brace kicks out, then a row of props, and a third of the span comes down into the river shallows with the concrete and the men on it. Every man on the staging wears a band, and the fall catch brings the fallers down slowly into the water and onto the bank. It doesn't stop what falls with them: the timber, the props and the wet concrete. Two buried men squeeze their bands for help, and the new bands send their positions with the call, the first time anyone has used it, and only because they asked. Milford's two rescue golems come down the road on the fire company's truck, decide where to dig, what to brace and when to stop, and stop once where the crew would have kept going, and they're right. Hattie swings her hook to lift a beam off a carpenter because nobody has told her not to. Fourteen are hurt, three badly. Nobody is killed.

Through Bettany's eyes, nine weeks a mender, the carpenter's thigh is crushed and bleeding fast from a torn vessel deep inside it. In July she assisted at the first time a titanium post was set in a man's thigh bone and the bone grown onto it in days. Now she is alone. She closes the vessel in the hollow view, fast, because it is bleeding fast, and the bleeding stops, and she goes to the next man. She closed it before the wound was clean. By morning the carpenter has a fever. Imani reopens it, cleans it, closes the vessel properly and leaves the wound open to drain for two days, the ordinary way. He keeps his leg. The broken back is Imani's, the second spinal repair after Reuben Orley's, with months of work ahead. The crushed hand is knit by another of the six. Imani says nothing to Bettany until it's done, tells her in three sentences what she did wrong, and puts her on the next case. That week a new line goes into the menders' book: clean before you close.

The same Friday the safety inspectorate stops the temporary works on every Corps job in the kingdom until each has been checked, and the Assembly's committee names Judge Keller commissioner of a public inquiry.

Chapter 100: Turnout

On Wednesday 11 September, through Kezia's eyes, the stop order is being lifted job by job, Fallow Bank was broken through at the end of August, and Monday 2 September has come and gone with the harvest still crossing at the old ford. On the train she reads that the chandler walked out of Turnstone in August with nobody waiting at the gate, and that a fowler on the Narrow March saw something on the salt marsh at dusk: bigger than any pup the hunters have killed, with vanes and the start of wings. It went into the reeds and was lost, and Merryn Pellow's crews are hunting the marsh.

Kezia, who sat the safety inspectorate's open examination at Kestle last October, is one of three inspectors sent to walk the bridge for the inquiry. On the temporary-works sheet she finds the line where the checker's signature should be, with "checked" written in it by a man who wasn't the checker, dated the day the pour was moved. She doesn't hurry to say so. She photographs it, writes it up and goes home on the evening train, not pleased with herself, because the man was trying to keep a king's date, and she understands that. At the inquiry Hattie tells Keller she said the bracing looked light and was told it was signed. Keller asks why she didn't refuse to lift. "Because it was signed." He writes that down.

In July the court decided the League's backlog suit: the crown inspectorate must publish its backlog every month by age and class of complaint, with no complainant named. On Saturday 14 September, through Corrick's eyes, the League's petition to recall Cinderhale's reeve, the old mill lord's cousin, goes to the vote in harvest week. The law needs two in five of the roll to vote in a recall, and the threshold was published long ago. About one in four come, and most of them vote yes. The count is properly done, and Corrick watches every bundle. Gethin, who took the core's first lock at Wayford's hall in April, says the threshold is a trick.

On Monday the younger members want to petition the Assembly to lower it. Corrick argues against them: a low threshold would let any committed faction remove an official in a week when the people who would keep him are too tired to come. "We lost because the people who'd have voted with us were in the fields. Next time we hold it in a better week, or we deserve to lose." He wins the room by four votes, and nobody enjoys it.

Chapter 101: First Steps

On Sunday 22 September, through Amara's eyes, the summer has gone in the golem, flying the coast, while Imani worked her own body in the dome through months of exercises Amara was never in it for, which is the strangest thing that has ever been done to her. Today she asked for Imani and Harrold and nobody else. Not Emeric. She wants to walk to him, not be helped to him by him.

Bram put two rails between the beds by the tree in the summer. She stands. Her legs shake, and the flagstone is cold, and she can feel that it is cold, which the golem never could; that is the first thing she says aloud. Four steps between the rails, Harrold walking backwards in front of her with his hands up and not touching her, Imani behind. On the fifth she sits down on the bronze circle in the floor on purpose and laughs.

Through Harrold's eyes, afterwards, he goes out through the pass-box and stands on the gallery a while before he goes home, and tells no one. Emeric, who can see his father's face from across the castle, doesn't ask.

On Monday 7 October, through Emeric's eyes, Keller's report is printed in full. The Assembly ordered it and the king asked for it in the same hour. It names the skipped approval: the independent check of the temporary works, postponed and signed by the wrong man. It names the responsible officials: Piers Wenlock, the resident engineer who signed it, and Elspeth Marlowe, the highway office's deputy who moved the pour forward and sent the crews to Fallow Bank. It names the budget pressure: a highway line paid by milestone, with a sum on opening, and the Corps' line the Assembly trimmed in December, which left two crews where four had been drawn. And it names the king's public date of 15 June, which every witness gave as the reason the pour moved and which no office had asked for. It blames no mage; the Corps' piers are sound. It doesn't mention the Church. The design is sound.

That evening Kaldo News asks him to speak on the sets, and he says yes before Ferra can. It is the first time a king of Randillia has spoken on television. He reads the paragraph about his date aloud, says it was his and nobody else's, and withdraws it. The road will open when the inspectorate signs the bridge and not a day before, and he will not name a date for any works again. He says sorry once. Ferra, watching in her office, notes that he never tried to share the blame with the Assembly's milestone money, which the report also blamed. Wenlock and Marlowe are moved off works, not dismissed, because the report blames the pressure as much as the people. The injured are paid in full while they heal and a sum besides. The safety inspectorate changes its own rule: no temporary works carry load until a checker who doesn't answer to the job's schedule has signed, and no opening date is published for any public work until the inspectorate has set its own sign-off date. In the morning the queue at the Milford ford hasn't moved.

Chapter 102: The Line on the Sheet

On Tuesday 1 October, through Ferra's eyes, the third quarter's ledger reaches her desk over the first packet link: Ambrose's small routers between the bridge machine's room and the counting house send the figures broken into numbered pieces, out of order, and they come out whole on her side. Eleven pages. She checks every figure by hand anyway. Tarrowmede's hill parishes are blank for the third quarter running, and she prints the word and the reason. In Ivo's expenses there is an odd line, glass plates fogged in the dark, twice, the second time on purpose, and nobody knows why. Saban's man in the north writes of a sound under Ostrel on a feast night that the pilgrims call the Church's bells and he doesn't. Ferra adds it to her page under the pass road closed on feast days.

On Wednesday 9 October, through Ada Lovell's eyes, the ride room opens. Kaldo has no core big enough for a real machine yet; the young are pups and the only bigger cores are spoken for. So the first thing the room drives is a four-legged walker about the size of the pup whose core runs it, which keeps the beast's own four-legged gait, carries about a man's weight, and is meant for hauling shut cases and kit over the grey beaches and the marsh where no cart goes, so nobody has to walk into a cordon where a young may or may not be dead. Hattie Roan, hired off one letter while the bridge was paused and her crane stood idle, calls it the dog. The pilot sits in a seat that tilts and pushes, wearing the cap, with a screen fed from the walker's two harvested eyes, linked by radio across the yard. Nothing goes back into anyone.

Bren Tallis goes first, because he is a knight and drives guard frames on the wall. He hates it. He can't feel the weight; it's like walking a dog on a long lead in the dark. He drives it into a wall and is sick in a bucket afterwards. Hattie goes second. She has driven loads she couldn't feel from a cab her whole life: you watch the hook, not your hands. She walks the dog across the rubble course and back, puts a shut case down where Ada chalked a cross, and asks what it's for. Then Ada cuts the link mid-walk, and the core plants all four feet and locks, and the dog stands where it stopped. Hattie says it's the first sensible thing a machine has done all year. Emeric watches the last run from the door and tells Ada her tables are good. Nobody says the word for the bigger machines.

On Saturday 19 October, through Ferra's eyes, Kettrin rings from the orcs' coast treaty-house. For three nights a brood came up the Sun River's mouth to the ford, and orc wardens on the bank with spears and Sabir boatmen on the water with fire pots held it together, because the ford is the only road between their lands and neither people could hold it alone. On the third night the brood went back into the river mouth and the sea. Nobody killed one. Kettrin reports it the treaty-house's way: the dead are on the roll by name, and the roll is theirs, not Kaldo's. Ferra writes "dead: on their roll." What Kettrin wants Kaldo to hear is that two peoples held one ford together, and nobody alive at the treaty-house has seen that before. She asks for shut cases for next time; the roll has already voted that if they ever kill one, the head will go to Kaldo sealed. The peg boy carried water to the ford line all three nights on Bram's blade, which fitted. Ferra puts the third pin on the board and the third dot on the sheet and draws a line through the three. For the first time it is a line, and it slopes: a hundred and twelve days between the first two attacks, ninety-six between the second and the third. She lays it beside her air column and says nothing, and neither does Emeric behind her.

On Monday 21 October Duvrenne signs the first leave for drums over an ally's ground: talk, a fix for anyone in trouble, positions for Duvrenne's own bands, Duvrenne's own copy of the sheet showing where every drum sits, and the right to recover any that fall. Kesrath signs; Alina witnesses. That evening Ferra says it to Emeric in private. "They've agreed to radios. They haven't agreed to an eye. You know that and I know that and Kesrath doesn't." He says he knows. She says knowing isn't paying. Nothing else is said.

Chapter 103: The Hall

On Monday 18 November, through Maudie's eyes, Cinderhale has had pumpkins on its steps at Hollow Night for the first time, off the south road's carts from Milford, and its priest has preached that the easier casting his flock feels lately is the Light's favour. In October the clinic got the town's first television set. Today the waiting room watches the Milford road open on it, on the inspectorate's date and not the king's: the first farm trucks crossing the rebuilt bridge, electric, and the king nowhere in the picture, at his own wish.

On Saturday 23 November, through Corrick's eyes, a mill faction books Cinderhale's hall for a meeting against the hours inspectorate, under the same booking rule the League uses. The younger League members plan to bar the door. One of them, Arlo Mercer, looked after Bryony's family after the killings, and he has every reason to hate the people the hall will let in. Corrick stands in the doorway with his own people behind him and the faction outside.

"You're letting them use our hall?" "It's the town's hall. They booked it under the same rule we use." "And Bryony?" "Bryony deserved a law that would protect her. Not one that only protects us." He moves aside. The faction speaks. Arlo leaves. Another supporter says he won't give to the strike fund again, and he doesn't. The meeting goes on, and Corrick doesn't chase anyone. Afterwards, on the steps, Kezia asks whether he regrets opening the door. "I wish they'd come to hear it." "That's not what I asked." "No." He thinks of the line he once wrote about enemies keeping their protections, and doesn't say it.

On Monday 2 December he starts the winter's visits, house to house. In the lower town a tired dyer agrees with every grievance he raises and still says he can't miss another day's work to vote. Corrick thanks him, leaves without a word about cowardice, and walks to the next door in the rain.

Chapter 104: On Her Own Feet

On Thursday 19 December, through Imani's eyes, the Year 22 budget passes on the hospital's set. Brask gets its river power station, its sewers working on the first bank since October. The Corps' line passes with an inspector written in for every crew, and no works line anywhere carries milestone money. The Capital Line's piers are funded, and so is the grove line at the soak racks' full capacity, with Bram's second rack ship. Ferra keeps the far board out of the debate. Three points make a line, not a cause, and she won't put a chart in front of a vote she would argue against herself. Imani has spent thirteen weeks watching Amara go from rails to a stick to no stick to the gallery stairs, which she climbed twice on Monday to prove she could. At the grove closes the knights stand the December watch without their coats and argue again whether it's the air.

On Wednesday 25 December the Long Table sits as every year: the family and the six; Zale and Ilenne up from Aumere, Ilenne giving without a tally; Maeve and Mira; and Runa, fourteen, whose gifts this year are all things you can eat, because she has learned the golem never could. Imani knows what is coming, because she set the day, and she hasn't told Emeric, because Amara asked her not to.

Through Amara's eyes, she comes in from the hall door, not in the golem, on her own feet, with no stick, Harrold at the door and not touching her, and walks to the chair at Harrold's right. Emeric stands up so fast his chair goes over. She sits down. She tastes the goose. She says it's overdone, which it is. Nobody makes a speech. Emeric doesn't try to take Ferra and Imani out before the pudding this year; Ferra notices and takes his hand under the table instead, and Imani says "after the pudding" anyway, to keep the custom. Kaldo News doesn't ask to carry the table this year. Amara asks for a second helping, Rowena says she shouldn't, and Amara has one.

Where the Story Stands After Chapter 104

Kaldo has a real airport, two escort jets and a liner that carries paying passengers to the old capital in an hour and a quarter. The maglev has been tested with each of its lifts cut in turn and has carried its first passengers. The Corps has cut through a ridge the old railway couldn't, in a season, and learned that what holds a job up is never only the digging. The first highway is open. The relay net's first phase is finished, its second has begun with Duvrenne's leave, and Ferra has told the king plainly that the eye Duvrenne didn't agree to is a debt. Six menders work in the clinics, and they all clean before they close.

The year's hardest lesson came from a bridge. The king said a date in a happy moment, his own institutions bent to meet it, and fourteen men were hurt when the staging gave. An inquiry he didn't run named his date among the causes, and he took it back on television and promised never to name another. The inspectorate, not the crown, wrote the rule that will stop it happening again.

In Cinderhale the recall failed on a threshold Corrick refused to lower, and he opened the town hall's door to his opponents and lost friends for it. An imperial clerk has seen Randillia's freight double and its neighbours' nurses moving south, and has a printed receipt in his pocket. Seventy seeds have gone to four peoples past the wall. The far board holds three pins, and Ferra's chart is a line that slopes downward. Nobody has killed a beast abroad, so nobody can yet prove to Ilenne that they aren't Kaldo's. At home one of the Isle mother's last five young has grown wings on the March marsh, and the hunt for it has begun.

And Amara, who rode a golem for seven years while her body healed under glass, walked into the Long Table on her own feet and asked for more goose. The year ends with the king held to account by the things he built, and with his mother sitting at his father's right hand, tasting her food.
