Chapter 31 - The Wrong Wall

The foundry ran on both shifts, and Emeric could hear it from the hall, the low, even breathing of a thing that finally worked. Five days since the last coalition wagon had turned off the ford road and not come back. Five days since Keller's word had come down from the capital and the market had gone honest overnight, buyers who had spent a season pretending Kaldo did not exist now sending factors to ask, politely, whether the barony had grain to spare at a fair figure. It had. It had more than it had ever had. Ferra's ledgers, which for a year had been a record of subtraction, had begun to run the other direction, and Vantic, the Ducatis man who sat in on the accounts with the patience of someone counting another man's money, had stopped clearing his throat before he spoke.

By every measure Emeric knew how to take, he had won.

He stood at the window that looked down the slope to the wall and could not make himself feel it. The wall was finished. Rubble core, quarry stone facing, lime mortar going grey as it cured, the empty rune slots still empty because he had never had cause to fill them, one gate above the ford and a graded approach a man could not cross without being watched from the tower the whole way up. He had designed it against an army. He had spent months of labor and a frightening share of the barony's credit building a thing to stop soldiers from crossing the ford, and no soldiers had ever come, because the enemy that had nearly killed Kaldo had never needed to cross the ford. It had worked the road instead. It had bought a courier and buried his reports and let the barony strangle quietly while he laid stone against a charge that was never coming.

That was the part he could not put down. The wall was not wrong. The wall was excellent. It faced the wrong direction, and he had not known it faced the wrong direction the entire time he was building it, and there had been nothing in his method to tell him.

He tried, the way he tried everything, to make it a problem he could hold.

He had Ferra pull the courier accounts and lay them beside the two years of dispatch records Keller's clerks had unsealed, and he sat with them a full evening, cross-referencing dates, tracing the gaps where a true report had left the barony and no report had arrived, building the pattern the way he had built the pattern that hanged the coalition's case. It was good work. It proved, past any argument, that the road had been worked against him from the first month of his exile. Before the farm turned. Before the plague even broke. Someone had wanted Kaldo dark before there was anything in Kaldo worth keeping dark.

And then the work simply stopped producing.

He could prove the road had been worked. He could not prove by whom. Every line he traced ran back to a bribed courier and stopped there, at a man who had taken money from a hand he had never seen, passed through a go-between who had passed through another, the trail thinning at each remove until it went to nothing the way a river went to nothing in dry country, spreading and shallowing until there was no channel left to follow. Cobb had taken coin. Whoever sat above Cobb had filed the false nothing-to-report and would say, under any question the crown could put to him, that he had filed what he was told and been paid market rate for filing it, and both of those things would be true, and neither of them would touch the hand that had wanted him dead.

He turned the papers over and started again, because starting again was what he did when a problem refused him, and it was the wrong instinct, and he knew it was the wrong instinct even as his hands did it. There was no data here he had not already extracted. He was running the same delve on the same seam a second time, hoping the vein went deeper than it did. It did not go deeper. He had built his whole method on the faith that a thing understood well enough could be reduced to its parts and rebuilt, that there was no problem in the world that was not, underneath, a problem of insufficient information, and this was a problem of insufficient information he had no way to gather. The information was a man's intention, and the man was near the throne, and there was no seam to open him along.

"You've read them four times," Bram said. He had come in without Emeric marking it, which happened, the smith moving quieter than a man his size had any right to. He set a cup down by Emeric's elbow and did not ask whether it was wanted. "The paper's not going to say a new thing on the fifth."

"I know what the paper says."

"Then you're not reading it for what it says." Bram lowered himself onto the bench across the table, slow, favoring the knee that had never come right after the winter. He looked at the spread of dispatches the way he looked at a bad weld, without heat, seeing only the flaw. "My lord. I've watched you take a plague apart and a harvest apart and a room full of lords who wanted you gone apart, and every one of them, you found the joint, and you pulled it. This one's got no joint you can reach, and you're sat here trying to pull it anyway because pulling is what your hands know."

Emeric said nothing, because there was nothing to say that was not agreement, and agreeing out loud would not have made it more true.

"A man wants you dead," Bram went on, plainer than anyone at court would have dared, plainer than Keller, who chose his words the way a jeweler chose stones. "Wanted it since before you got here. You can't build the wanting into a ledger. There's no seal on it and no route it travels you can mark. It's just a man, somewhere, deciding a thing about you, over and over, and you can't reach him and you can't out-figure him, because he's not a lock. He's a person, and you." Bram stopped, and reconsidered, and set it down more gently than he had picked it up. "You've never been at your best with people. You know that about yourself. It's cost you before, and it'll cost you here, because this whole trouble is a people trouble, top to bottom, and people is the one seam you've never learned to read."

It landed the way true things landed, without drama, a fact stated and then just sitting there in the room being a fact. Emeric had known it about himself for a year. He had built a courier system with a forgery-proof mark precisely because he could not trust his own read of which men would stay bought and which would break, had solved the human problem with a machine because the machine he could trust and the read he could not. And the machine had still failed him, once, when the alternate route he had believed safe turned out to run through a man who was not, and a report he had counted as delivered had gone nowhere, and he had learned the same lesson he kept being taught and kept setting down: that he could build a fence around the problem of trusting people, but he could not build his way out of having to.

"I know," he said finally. It was not much. It was true.

The relief was gone by then. It had lasted five days, which was longer than he had expected once he understood what Ward had understood, and now it was gone, and something colder had moved into the place where it had been.

He walked the wall that night because the wall was where he thought, out along the parapet above the ford with the river loud below and the barony a scatter of banked fires behind him, and he made himself do the thing he was worst at, which was to stop looking at the enemy and look instead at himself.

The enemy he could not reach. That much was decided, a wall he could not build against because he could not find the ground to set it on. But the enemy did not need to be reachable to be reasoned about, and one thing about it was plain and had been plain in Ward's voice at the table five nights ago, and Emeric had been too full of winning to weigh it. The enemy worked in the dark and worked patiently and worked, both times, through a single narrow way. An ambush on an ordinary outing when he was seven, his mother's carriage on an open road and men on it before anyone understood they were under attack, and by the end his mother dead and every guard dead but one, and only Ward left to carry him out of it alive. An ambush on the road to Kaldo years later, dressed as bandits, meant to finish what the first had not. Two attempts across all those years, patient and unhurried both, and he had never once built anything to protect against the hand behind them.

He had built for the barony. He had built walls and foundries and irrigation, and a courier system and a legal case, and every one of them protected the thing Kaldo was, and not one of them protected the single point on which the whole of Kaldo now rested. If he fell, all of it fell. The farm did not know how it worked without him. The foundry did not. The defense of the place, such as it was, lived in his head and nowhere else, and the enemy, whoever held the patient hand near the throne, had already shown across two attempts that it understood this better than he did. It had never once gone at the walls. Both times it had gone at him.

He stopped at the tower above the gate and put his hand flat on the cold stone and felt, faint under his palm, the Pull, the mana-presence of the barony breathing away below him, the way he always felt it, the way every mage felt the density of the living world when they held still enough to notice. And he thought, standing there, that he had built a wall with slots for runes he had never filled, a defense with an empty socket where its teeth should be, and that he had left it empty because he had been thinking about armies. He had been thinking about the wrong wall the entire time. The wall that mattered was not the one below him. It was the one he had never thought to build, the one that stood between a patient hand and the single undefended man the hand kept reaching for, and there was no version of that wall that could be allowed to rest on him alone, because he was the thing it was meant to protect, and a defense that depends on the survival of the man it defends is not a defense. It is a habit of luck.

It could not depend on him. That was the whole of what he carried down off the parapet, the one clean line he had cut out of a night of turning it over. Whatever he built next had to hold when he was not there to hold it. It had to work in a room he was not standing in, answer to hands that were not his, keep the barony and keep him without needing him present to keep either. He did not yet know how a thing like that was made. He knew only that it was the next thing, the first thing he had ever set out to build, not to make Kaldo larger but to make it survive his own absence, and that the work of it would be nothing like the work that had won him the year.

The winning work had been all seams and joints, plague and harvest and ledgers, problems that lay still and let themselves be taken apart. This would not lie still. This had a hand behind it that thought and waited and adapted, and every wall he raised, it would study, and go around, the way it had gone around the ford. He had spent a year learning that he was cleverer than any problem the world set in front of him. He walked back to the hall in the dark understanding, for the first time since the ship had left him on this island, that cleverness was going to turn out to be the smaller half of what this cost him, and that he had built the wrong wall once already without ever knowing it, and had no way, yet, to be sure he was not about to do it again.
