Book 3, Batch 4: Ninety Days

Chapters 31–39 | Tuesday 29 February – Sunday 9 April, Year 19

The Assembly has struck the emergency exception, and from the night of 28 February any emergency over a works or a force needs the Assembly's own authorisation through its committee. The king read the recommendation against his own power word for word and walked out the members' door. The crown note has been the realm's one paper since 10 January, conversion of old debts comes on 8 May, and Vantic's window is still buying unpaid claims on the western plains with her tower pledged behind it. The money houses' men wear new coats. Corrick and the king have met in Cinderhale with respect and no forgiveness. The review's second part waits for the orc families' member.

This batch is the first war done the new way. An order served lawfully across a baroness's weighing table is answered with an order to stop the commissioners by force, and four commissioners die on the river below Fairhythe's chain. For the first time the king cannot act and explain later. He asks the Assembly for an authorisation and sits through two days of debate in silence, and what he gets is ninety days, a field command under the Assembly's committee, a surrender officer who is not him, and a rule that force comes only after a proclamation has been read.

The campaign is real and so are its costs. Fairhythe opens without a death, the king lifts a river chain off its posts in front of a town, and a requisition of grain carts leaves two villages eating their seed. The Hearth stops a convoy of guns by pulling their locks in the night and surrenders itself at dawn. And under the tin works at Stannary, an order to close a dangerous stope is ignored, an old passage is quietly opened, and the timbers start to talk.

Chapter 31: Served

On Tuesday 29 February, at the castle's breakfast table, through Ferra's eyes, she reads the Assembly's new emergency procedure twice. Her year under censure ended on 23 February and her personal power to halt crown emergency spending came back, but the procedure passed with the strike puts every stay on spending under an emergency authorisation in the hands of the committee's own reviewer, because that spending is the Assembly's now, not the crown's. Ilsa has held the seat for a year and keeps it. Ferra's pen can still stay the crown's ordinary relief and its own works; what it has lost is the crown's own emergency spending, which the strike ended. Ferra tells Emeric and Imani she is the only Chancellor in the realm's history to get her power back and lose most of its object in the same week. She laughs. Imani does not. Emeric says the institution is working. Ferra says that is what she is afraid of, and means both halves.

On Wednesday 1 March a porter of Fairhythe's quay, dismissed in January for drunkenness, swears to the Inspectorate what is in the porters' lodge's back room: rifles the Act made the crown's, never surrendered, and a ball mould. The circuit grants an order of entry under the Act the same afternoon, covering the porters' lodge and the weigh-house arms room, to be executed by four commissioners of the Inspectorate with a magistrate's clerk travelling by barge from the lower quay. On Thursday at noon a commissioner serves it across Baroness Sayer's own weighing table, in front of her clerks and a cargo of Tarrowmede wheat. She reads it, signs for it, and says the commissioners are welcome at her tables when they come by the road and ask.

The same week, in Cinderhale, through the widow Tench's eyes, one of Pelham's men knocks with a slip for dues already paid. She shows him the receipt and the court's stay, and he leaves the slip on her table anyway. On Thursday a constable from the circuit serves him for contempt at the mill lords' counting room, and on Friday the court fines him. That evening, with the court paper beside the demand slip and the receipt on her table, Maudie comes with her next petition. Tench signs it, and will not say the word Hearth, though Maudie does not ask her to.

Through Sayer's eyes, on Thursday night she writes her first order to her chain captains in her own words: no crown man lands at her tables without her leave. She means the commissioners, but she does not say commissioners. On Friday a crown barge with the Inspectorate's inventory clerks and their crates comes up behind a grain barge. The captain at the chain hesitates over whether a barge counts as a man landing, and lets it through. It ties up below the upper stair, the clerks are refused the stair by porters with clubs, not guns, and they go back down. Nobody is hurt. On Saturday 4 March, in her hall, Sayer writes the order that leaves no room: the commissioners are not to pass the river chain, and the watch is to stop them by all force, fire included. It is dated two days after service. Fairhythe's notary attests it in his register that afternoon, as every order touching the quay's tolls has been attested for a hundred years. The courier reads it on the way upriver, makes a fair copy at an inn, delivers the copy, and keeps the signed original in his own coat, to cover himself.

The order of entry is signed by the Fairhythe circuit's magistrate on the porter's sworn statement, which is firsthand: he stood in the back room. That is what Fairhythe's liberty asks for under its old charter, the same test the Dunholt duty officer will explain to Corrick a month later. The commissioners go by barge because the order names the route: the upper town's public stair is the crown's landing above the chain, and the road in runs through Sayer's own quay gates. The barge flies the Inspectorate's pennant.

Chapter 32: Lantern Night

On Monday 6 March, at the river wall, through Dacey's eyes, it is her first Lantern Night in Kaldo. She writes Bryn on her lantern. The king asks her whether he may write Bryn's name on his. She tells him one lantern each, and that he should write his own. She is not being cruel, and he does not take it so. He writes nothing on his and lets it go up blank, because what he would write is a long list, and the list is the review's. Imani sees, and says nothing.

After Ferra's count, Armand reads one sheet from the wall: the first year of his weekly readings of the class. Every core has grown. The pace follows density, air and hours rather than where anyone read when they came in, and the steepest line belongs to the knight who read lowest of the Crestless. No core he has read has shrunk in a year, and he says what that is worth: a year is not a life.

Through Emeric's eyes, Runa writes two names, Veya and Orren, in the school letters she learned this year, the first words she asked to be taught, and holds her lantern a long time before she lets go. Abloh's dress coats for the fleet's twelve hundred were issued that week, and the crews wear them to the wall for the first time; Abloh tells Ferra it is the only order he has ever filled on time. From the wall Ferra reads the year's graduates by field, not as one number, and somebody cheers surveying and nobody knows why. Sorrel's balloon goes up tethered above the square with two children in the basket and Sorrel swearing at the burner. Fennick's display is the best yet, and his last rocket lands in the river, which is where Ferra's rule says it should land, and he is sulky about it. Late on the wall the king reads Sayer's polite reply by lantern-light, from Ferra's pocket: welcome when they come by the road and ask. Somewhere on the Tarrow the commissioners' barge is tied up at the lower quay, waiting for morning. He does not know there is a second order. Nobody outside Fairhythe does.

Chapter 33: Four

On Tuesday 7 March, in mid-morning, through the eyes of Ruth Penhale's grown son, who is poling the commissioners' barge for hire because nobody else would, the barge comes up the Tarrow on the tide with the four commissioners and the magistrate's clerk aboard and the order of entry in an oilskin. Eighty yards short of the chain, the captain fires the chain battery's swivel gun, and his crew fire after it. The barge never reaches the chain. Four commissioners die. The clerk is hit in the arm and lives. The Penhale boy goes over the side and swims, and lives. That afternoon the news comes by the lower quay's set to the field command's line room in Kaldo and on to the castle, and Ferra writes the four names in her own hand before anyone else does.

On Wednesday 8 March, in Keller's court, through Keller's eyes, he sits a preliminary hearing. He issues named warrants: for Sayer, for the order, and for the chain captain and his named gun crew, for the firing. He issues a preservation order on Fairhythe's notary register and the chain battery's powder book, served by bailiffs with a magistrate the same day, and protection orders for the clerk and the Penhale boy. He refuses a blanket warrant on Fairhythe's watch. The order makes some men liable, he says, not every man in a coat. He does not convict anyone, and says that too.

The chain captain can see the pennant, the same crown barge, and two of the clerks his own porters turned back from the stair on Friday, at a range where he knows them, with the clerk standing in the bow holding up the oilskin. He fires at eighty yards because he reads not to pass the chain as stop them before they can try: the tide is with the barge and he thinks they will cut the chain. That is what he will say at trial, and it is why Sayer's murder count will fail on her intent.

Chapter 34: Ninety Days

On Wednesday afternoon, 8 March, the king stands at the crown's place in the Assembly and makes the first request under the new procedure in one sentence: an authorisation to end the armed resistance at Fairhythe and in the Cocan March, on terms the Assembly sets. The committee takes it to the floor the next morning. Through Corbin's eyes, on Thursday and Friday, the Tarrow member who argued to keep the exception speaks first: he was afraid of Sayer's porters and was right to be, and he is glad now that it is the Assembly writing the terms and not a man in a hurry. He votes for it. The mill lord asks how long, and the crews' man says ninety days, with renewal only by this floor. Corbin reads Keller's draft of the surrender officer's instrument whole, because, he says, the men behind the walls should know what is offered before anyone shoots. A March member speaks against it as a war on his own people; Agatha Vell is in the gallery, and he sees her and finishes anyway. The king says nothing for two days.

It passes late on Friday 10 March, printed by name, the March's members against. The terms run ninety days, from Saturday 11 March to Thursday 8 June, renewable only by the Assembly. They cover Fairhythe's liberty and the Cocan March and any armed body that joins them. Ward holds the field command under the Assembly's emergency committee, with Kaldo's field company and the Crestless under him, and his requisitions are reviewed by Ilsa. The surrender officer is Magistrate Ada Quarrie, named by Keller and never by the king, with a narrow instrument: anyone excluded from the pardon who disarms and gives up all control may be offered terms only on her written finding of imminent risk to civilians, never for murder or torture, with hostages released unharmed, exile for life by a named route, and her finding read aloud and published within a day. A proclamation offers pardon and pay to any man in plain service who lays down his arms and walks out, excluding murder, torture, hostage-taking, and command of an armed body after the proclamation has been read to it. Force against a held place may come only after the proclamation has been read at that place. Every use of force is reported to Quarrie as the reviewing officer, who may sit an inquiry with two committee members and publish its findings. The king's own force is under the same terms as any officer's, with no special rule. And the pay, the pardon pay and pager bands for duty staff are voted Authorisation lines. On Saturday, in Keller's court, Quarrie, a plain, careful southern-circuit magistrate known for reading her findings aloud as she writes them, is sworn, and asks Keller one question: whether her finding can be reviewed. He says yes. She says good.

On Thursday night the king tells Ferra a committee is a day late. On Friday night, when it passes, she tells him it was three. He says nobody died in the three. She says the upper town spent them digging. On Saturday 11 March, the day the Authorisation begins, in the crown book's counting house, through Tullio's eyes, he sends the March's pass guards their quarter's pay to Pryor's steward by the crown book's ordinary courier. He keeps the duplicate receipt, as he has kept every receipt for thirty years, and files it with the others. He does not think of it as a war. He thinks of it as a loan to a client whose collateral is the only road to Ostrel.

The instrument Corbin reads carries one more term: where an inquiry touches what the surrender officer herself decided or negotiated, she gives her evidence as a witness, and a circuit magistrate Keller names sits in her place for that part. The night before the vote, by set, the king asks Ward whether the field company could be on the March edge by morning, on exercise. Ward says not without the vote, and that the March would read the exercise correctly. It is the second time someone with the standing to say no has said it to him.

Chapter 35: The Window Shuts

On Monday 13 March Saban's man at the Gate reports that Church carts are coming through by night and going up to Pryor's keep and the old Holt fort on the pass, and that the March's changers have started taking stones as well as coin at the exchange. Saban says something is being built that runs on stones.

On Wednesday 15 March, through Imani's eyes, three hundred pager bands are issued under the Authorisation's line: the second generation of the citizen's band with a radio receiver keyed to the relay net and a strip of short text, which buzzes, shows a few words and a set's call sign, so the wearer answers on the nearest set. They go to the duty staff the Authorisation needs at any hour: the field company's duty officers, Imani's duty surgeons and Sister Inga's crush team, Quarrie's two clerks, the circuit's duty clerks and bailiffs, and the remand house's duty clerk. Imani likes it for her surgeons and does not want it on patients yet, because a band that buzzes a frightened man in the night is a different thing from one that only listens.

On Tuesday 14 March, at close of business on its thirtieth day, through Vantic's eyes, her window shuts. The clerk's last count: claims presented about a hundred and twelve thousand Gold Rand, face accepted about eighty-one thousand, coin paid out about eighty thousand one hundred and ninety, of which about twenty thousand one hundred and ninety was drawn on Duvrenne's credit secured on her tower. The auditor, who is not hers, seals the book. She does not know her loss yet, because the claims settle as the shippers pay, in notes redeemed at the county offices on Thursdays. In the close count one serial turns up twice; on the dust plate one of the pair makes no figure, and under her thumb its border is flat. The first forged crown notes are a sign-painter's in Halverne, working alone, about forty passed, and Saban's man has him by the Friday. It has nothing to do with the houses, and the system caught it before anyone needed it to. She walks home and does not look up at the tower.

On Wednesday 15 March, by Kettrin's set from the treaty-house, through Ferra's eyes, the doors' families say they have read the record in their own hands. Their member is Petrok, the warden, Brega's grandson, and Brega herself will come with him to see their dead, as they said she would after the zone had held a winter. They will sail on the trade hull's April run from the Sun Shore. Torben will not come. Ferra tells the review's clerk within the hour. On Monday 20 March the Issue Board publishes its estimate: a seasonal coin shortfall this winter of about ninety-five thousand Gold Rand across the western plains, of which Tullio's calls were one pressure point and Vantic's window carried about eighty-one thousand. Ferra reads it aloud to Emeric and points at the gap between the two numbers, and is careful with it: the two figures count different things, so the gap is not all hunger, but some of it is farms that went without, and she has sent for the county ledgers to say how many. He says the Board should publish that too. She says it just did, and the ledgers will follow.

About sixty thousand of the coin the window paid out is the House's own, ring-fenced from depositors; about twenty thousand is Duvrenne's credit, a debt secured on the tower, not a loss. What the House will lose is what never comes back: claims on shippers who do not pay and claims that turn out false, plus the credit's interest, less the fees. The independent auditor's book carries every claim from purchase to redemption, so whatever the number turns out to be, anyone can check it.

Chapter 36: The Chain

On Thursday 16 March, through Ward's eyes, the field company comes down on lower Fairhythe's quay from two hulls. The herald reads the proclamation at the quay and from the hulls by loudhailer, with leaflets after it, and by noon the lower town opens its quay: barge families, carters, and the lower town's own changers, who never liked paying the upper town's tables. The upper town shuts its gate above the stair, porters in their coats on the wall, the chain across the river below. On Friday, two upper-town porters who came down in the night and laid their coats on the quay are being beaten by lower-town men at dawn. Ward stops it himself with his hands and has the beaters held for the circuit. The proclamation's pardon is either for everyone who lays down or for no one, he tells the quay, and he is not asking. On Saturday the herald reads the proclamation at the chain from a boat and at the upper gate from the stair. Nothing comes back but a porter's bottle.

On Sunday 19 March, mid-morning, through Ruth Penhale's eyes from her barge, her son's wet shirt still drying on the rail from Tuesday, Ward's plan is a cutting party at the chain posts under the field company's cover, which means a firefight with the chain battery. Emeric offers another way. Ward thinks about it for a whole minute and agrees, because the proclamation has been read at the chain and nothing in the terms forbids it: the crown's force is under the same terms as any officer's. Through Emeric's eyes, he changes in full on the lower quay, the first time since the Isle that the form has been used as the crown's force in front of a town. He walks into the river to his chest, takes the chain in his jaws and both hands, lifts it off its posts and lays it on the bank, links and posts together. The chain battery's men run before he is halfway across. Nobody is touched. He changes back on the quay in a borrowed coat. Ruth says to nobody that Sayer was fair to her once, about the barge feud, and is wrong now, and that she does not like a king who can do that either.

On Monday and Tuesday the upper town's porters walk down in twos and fives, lay their coats and rifles on the quay, give their names and are paid, and Fairhythe's weigh-house clerks release the holds on grain settlements themselves. At the Gate, Pryor's steward draws the lesson: a lord who has read the Church's histories now knows the dragon is real, public, and lawful when he waits for a herald, and that his own ground will need what Ostrel's carts have been bringing up the pass. On Wednesday 22 March Keller's bailiffs serve the warrant at Sayer's hall door with the field company. She has the notary's register brought out to them herself, because it is the law of her house that the register goes where the law asks. She does not know about the courier's original. She asks to walk to the quay rather than be carried in a cart, and walks.

Chapter 37: Two Villages

On Monday 20 March the field command moves up the March edge toward the Cocan foothills. The March road's own carters will not hire to the crown, so the field command requisitions March grain carts and their teams at the posted rate from Cray Bottom and Stile Green, the two villages nearest the road, whose carts are also how their last winter grain comes down from the hill stores before the spring roads open. On Tuesday, in the Chancellor's office, through Ferra's eyes, she puts the villages' grain book in front of Ilsa and argues that the order strips their stocks for three weeks. Ilsa declines a fast stay on the field command's written evidence: the campaign cannot move without the carts, and a stay would leave the field company on the March edge with no supply line. It is a defensible call, Ferra says so, and hates saying it. She would have signed the stay in a minute, with a condition. She does not have the pen. That week the grain stays in the hill stores, Stile Green, Agatha's village, and Cray Bottom eat seed grain by Friday, and Agatha's neighbours blame the crown.

On Friday 24 March, at Stannary's engine house, through Treloar's eyes, after Fairhythe he writes to the field command that the works are open to the Inspectorate. Most of his wardens walk out under the proclamation and are paid; a dozen go over the boundary ridge into the March. Some of his miners still trust him, because the pumps used to run. He is not in the March's plot; he is in debt. On Monday the Inspectorate's commissioners and the crown's mines inspector go through the works, and the pumps are failing: the engine's coal is short and two rods are cracked, because the mint's farm has paid nothing since January and the house has no coin. On Saturday 25 March Merryn Pellow kills a pup on a peat creek behind Saltreach about one point six metres long, the biggest on the Register yet, with a ring of grey dead flakes round an old shard cut it had found under a bank; its mark reads born, under the Isle, as before. Spring has brought them size and range, but none has passed the first stage. On Monday 27 March a causeway shepherd sees Coldharbour's hired men, laid off by houses that no longer want armed porters or wardens, come home along the causeway with their coats turned and start taking sheep and toll from the moor roads, while Malloch's household knights ride out and take a share instead of stopping them, and Malloch writes to two foreign houses whose sell-swords are on imperial credit.

On Tuesday 28 March, at the emergency committee, through Agatha Vell's eyes, the committee overturns the requisition on Ilsa's own referral. Reeve Bessa Lorne of Stile Green speaks: what they ate and what they will not be able to plant. Their loss is the story, not the order. Agatha, in the gallery, condemns Pryor in the same breath as she mourns his pension clerk, who would have sent a cart of grain on a word and then sent his bill. Ferra, beside her, says nothing, because she knows exactly what she would have signed.

The committee can overturn the requisition a week after Ilsa declined a fast stay because it now has two facts she did not: Reeve Bessa Lorne's grain book with the stock count taken on Friday, seed grain eaten and no longer a forecast, and the field command's own return for Monday 27, showing hired carts from the lower plains on the road, so the villages' carts are no longer the only way to move. Ilsa refers it herself on those two facts.

Chapter 38: Dunholt

On Tuesday 28 March, in the morning, in the League's back room in Cinderhale, through Corrick's eyes, one of the returned ex-watchmen, the one who left for a season in July, comes back from driving a cart to Dunholt for a mill with what the river depot's carters say over their beer: about two hundred crates billed as plough-shares, the shape and weight wrong, and a convoy of six wagons and a spare team booked for the dawn after next, Thursday 30 March, with Fulk Garside travelling with it. Corrick calls the field command at once, from Cinderhale's post office, on the public line the proclamation printed for reports. The duty officer, paged off his supper, logs it and explains the conflict: the liberty writ needs a firsthand sworn account of what is inside, the Inspectorate's entry stops at the edge of a duke's liberty, and the Authorisation's writ covers the March's armed parties, not a duke's documented freight. He tells Corrick exactly what evidence would change the magistrate's authority, and that the circuit magistrate next sits at Dunholt on Thursday morning, after first light. He does not shrug.

That night, through Kezia's eyes, she holds a veto over any action. They check the schedules overnight. A carter's son asks whether they can ask the carters, and is told that if they ask, the shipment will leave; then the carters take the risk and the League decides, and Corrick hears how official it sounds. Why every lock: the magistrate sits after first light, the waybill shows six wagons and a spare team so the load can split, and a writ by noon is too late. They write a notice for the first crate. Kezia lifts her veto: it is not theirs to decide, and she is deciding it anyway. On Wednesday from about ten at night, through Gethin's eyes, the crew is in the depot inside a four-hour window: five pairs, about four minutes a firearm, forty locks a pair, counted. Gethin's rebuilt hand slips on a cold part, and he takes his partner's help. They take two labelled exemplars and the office day book, bag and label the locks for matching in court, and nail the notice to the first crate last. There are no knights and no night glass; they work by shuttered lanterns.

At two on Thursday morning, through Kezia's eyes, the depot clerk comes back with the waybill book. Kezia remembers Liss Whitlow and the other carters, goes back, takes the book from the clerk's coat while he is at the privy, passes it out of the window to the lookout on the bank road, and is caught in the yard by the depot's night men. The night man rings the field command's line, and the duty officer pages the circuit's duty bailiffs, who are at the depot gate by a quarter to three. At dawn, through Corrick's eyes, Gethin wants to go back for her, and Corrick refuses to break into another building. He surrenders with a sworn statement under his own name, and Gethin goes with him. The magistrate receives the bagged locks, the two exemplars, the day book, the waybill book, which Maudie hands in at the courthouse door unasked, and Corrick's statement, each logged with its source and handler. The liberty writ issues by noon. Fulk heard the alarm and is gone before dawn on a hired horse, south over the hill roads toward the March, the convoy's own road, three days to the Gate and passing within a day's ride of Stannary. Corrick, Kezia and Gethin are remanded to the old shire gaol at Kestle, whose duty clerk wears a pager band and whose gatehouse keeps the circuit's field set. At the depot, Liss Whitlow reads the notice on the first crate, looks at the bailiffs, unhitches her team and walks it back up the road, and the other carters do the same. Six wagons of guns without locks and no foreman. The convoy does not leave.

The duty officer also offers what the law can still do: a watch on the bank road at the liberty's edge from first light, two field men and the circuit's duty bailiffs who are lodged in Dunholt for Thursday's sitting; the wagons followed onto crown road and stopped there, where the Act reaches without a liberty writ; and a preservation order asked at the sitting. Corrick relays it. Kezia's answer is that the depot has more than one gate, the waybill shows six wagons and a spare team, the drovers' road forks within a mile into the hill tracks, and two men at the edge can follow one wagon, not six. Nobody proves her right or wrong. The count runs one way all through: two hundred crates, one long gun in each; two hundred locks taken, forty to a pair; and the magistrate's 198 locks bagged, two exemplars and two hundred firearms.

Chapter 39: The East Stope

On Thursday and Friday, 30 and 31 March, the circuit magistrate's count at the depot matches what the Hearth handed over: a hundred and ninety-eight copies in the crates, two exemplars in the court's bag, two hundred locks, every barrel bored to the Church-traded ball the Gate's men use, which only an order made for the Gate would need. The carters' wagons are released that afternoon. On Monday 3 April Wystan is charged on his works' knowledge by the circuit's prosecutor, his own knowledge not proven.

On Sunday 2 April, in the castle's kitchen, through Runa's eyes, she turns twelve. She knew it as the week the creek ice broke at Coldspring, and Veya always said the second day of it, so Ferra put 2 April on her card. Emeric is home from the field command for one night, and his present is a dead radio, a field set with a burned valve and a cracked board, and a box of parts. They fix it at the kitchen table. She is better at it than either expected, and finds the cracked joint by smell, hot shellac. Afterward she asks him to tell her about Orren's knock again, two quick and one slow, though she knows the story. Ferra and Imani give her boots and a book, and Imani gives her a flash lamp with a shutter, for reading under the blankets, she says.

On Wednesday 5 April the crown's mines inspector writes an order closing Stannary's east stope until its supports are replaced, serves it on Treloar and posts it at the shaft head. Through Treloar's eyes, there is no coin for timber, and the east stope's face is the richest tin in the works. He tells the shift captain to keep a crew on it, short-timbered, until the timber comes, and writes nothing down. From Monday 3 to Sunday 9 April, the shift captain, Kenver Tregarth, has his crew put up fresh timber lagging at the lower door of the old haulage incline, below the flooded sump everyone calls impassable, and behind it repair an old dry ladderway. Anyone who sees the lagging reads it as repair work. It is on no plan the house has given anyone. Since Dunholt's convoy failed, Pryor needs a way between Stannary and the March that does not pass the field command's road post.

On Friday 7 April, at her mill, through Mabel's eyes, she hears from a carter that Fulk is gone and nobody knows where. Corrick's note from Kestle, saying he cannot prove her brother guilty or afraid, goes into the stove unopened. She sends the League its flour order the same afternoon, priced in notes at the posted rate. She weighs it on the scale Fulk gave her. On the Sunday night, 9 April, the east stope's timbers talk, and nobody in the town above is listening.

Nobody official sees the fresh timber for what it is. The inspectors walked the works on 27 March, a week before the lagging went up, and the closure order is served and posted, not walked. The drawings they carry are the house's own survey, which marks the incline below the sump as abandoned and flooded and does not show the Rise at all, and the shift captain's gallery plan copies it. Fresh timber in a works under an order to retimber is the one thing nobody questions.

After the radio and Orren's knock, Runa asks whether the offer still stands. She makes Emeric fetch Ferra and Imani so she can ask it properly. It does; the form is still in Ferra's drawer. She keeps her name, her button and her parents, and says so before she signs. Since Lantern Night she has kept a second book, letters to Veya and Orren that nobody reads.

Where the Story Stands After Chapter 39

The March Emergency Authorisation runs from 11 March to 8 June, renewable only by the Assembly. Ward holds the field command under the Assembly's committee, Quarrie is surrender officer and reviewing officer for every use of force, force against a held place may come only after the proclamation is read there, and the king's force stands under the same terms as anyone's. It has been used once, lawfully, when the king lifted Fairhythe's chain with nobody touched. Fairhythe is open and Sayer is on remand, unaware that the courier kept the signed original of her standing order. Four commissioners are dead. Stannary has submitted, but its east stope was closed by written order on 5 April and worked anyway, its pumps failing, and an old ladderway has been repaired behind fresh lagging on no plan. Pryor holds the March, with Church carts going up to his keep and the Holt fort by night and his changers taking stones. Tullio has paid the March one tranche and kept the receipt. Coldharbour's men are drifting into banditry under Malloch's eye.

Corrick, Kezia and Gethin are on remand at Kestle after pulling two hundred locks at Dunholt and surrendering at dawn; Fulk is gone south toward the March, Wystan is charged, and Mabel burned Corrick's note unopened. Vantic's window shut with about eighty-one thousand accepted and about twenty thousand drawn on her tower, her loss not yet known, against the Board's estimate of a ninety-five thousand shortfall. The first forgeries were caught by the note's own design. Three hundred pager bands are on duty staff. Ferra has her power back and nothing to use it on, and Ilsa's defensible refusal of a stay let two villages eat their seed before the committee overturned the requisition.

The young have grown with spring but none has passed the first stage: thirteen killed, one starved, and the mother unseen. The count stands at day 198 with four sales, and nobody has said Ostrel. Petrok, the orc families' member, and his grandmother Brega sail on the April hull, so the review's second part can begin, and day 120 of the note falls on 8 May. Runa is twelve, with a mended radio and a shuttered flash lamp.

At the lesson on Saturday 1 April Waylon shows the drop, from the whole into the partial form in one breath and back again. Six months after his first lesson he is the first of the line to master both forms, four weeks before Emeric. His father, on the same call, still comes back from the whole by way of a man.

This batch shows what restraint costs when it is written into law. The ninety days work. They cost three days at the start, two villages' seed, and a king who has to wait for a herald before he lifts a chain. And underneath the town that submitted, a house with no coin is working a stope it was ordered to close, which is where the next batch begins.
