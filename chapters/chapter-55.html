Chapter 55 - The Cursed Shaft

The problem was ore, and it was a good problem, the kind a man wanted to have, which did not make it less of a problem. The foundry at the rapids ate iron the way the plague had once eaten people, steadily and without malice and faster than anyone had planned for, and the mended road from Grayrock, which had seemed a triumph the season they finished it, now delivered exactly as much ore as it had been built to deliver and not one skip more, and the foundry's appetite had outgrown the road inside a year. Emeric did the arithmetic on it one grey morning over the shipping tallies and found the answer he already knew, which was that he was not short of money, he was short of throughput, and the two felt the same in the hand and were nothing alike on paper. His treasury was a strange animal now, fat and starving at once, every Rand that came in going straight back out into stone and iron and wages before it had time to grow warm, so that a man reading his books would have called him rich and a man watching his coin-boxes would have called him poor, and both would have been right, and neither could buy him a wagonload of ore that did not exist. The road ran full. The foundry ran hungry. Somewhere upstream of both, in the grey hills, was the actual constraint, and Emeric had learned long ago that when a system starved you went to the place it was thinnest and looked, so he went to Grayrock.

He rode up the river road with Ward at his shoulder and a small party behind, the hills closing in the way they always did, bare stone shouldering through thin grass, ore country and little else, and he watched the head-frames and the spoil heaps resolve out of the distance a mile off exactly as they had the first time he came here as a stranger to buy a widow's loyalty. It had been cheaper than it should have been, that loyalty, because Bryda Renner had been a woman counting a cold sum over the body of the husband Emeric had killed in open field, and she had done the arithmetic of it without flinching and found that the man who had widowed her offered her town more than the war that had made her a widow ever had. He had never once mistaken that for warmth. It was not warmth. It was a clear-eyed woman refusing to spend her people on a grudge, and it had held for two years because it was built on something harder than feeling, and he trusted it more than he trusted men who smiled at him.

She received him in the same hall, and they went over the workings the way they went over everything, plainly, the two of them saying little while the numbers said the rest, and the numbers said what Emeric had ridden up to hear. The veins Grayrock worked were the shallow ones, the safe ones, the courses a man could reach without dying for them, and they were skimmed near to nothing. Renner had skimmed them, the spring musters had skimmed the last of what he left, and now the town brought up ore the way a man scrapes the last fat from a cold pan, with effort out of all proportion to the yield. To get more iron out of Grayrock you did not need more hands on the same veins. You needed the deep, and the deep in Grayrock was closed.

"There's a lower working," Bryda said, and something in the way she said it, the flatness she put over it the way a person puts a lid over a thing that smells, made Emeric set down the tally he was holding. "Renner sank it the year before you came. Sank it deep, deeper than any shaft in the district, on a survey he paid a fortune for and never showed anyone. And it killed men." She did not soften it. He had never known her to soften anything. "Not falls. Not water. They'd go down whole and hale and not come up, and the ones who came up to tell it said the same thing every time, that the air went thick and the lamp went low and a great tiredness came over them in the dark, and the strong ones dragged the weak ones out and the weak ones died anyway, and after the third time no wage Renner could offer would put a man down that hole. So he sealed it and let the town call it what the town wanted to call it, and the town calls it cursed, and I'll tell you honestly, my lord, I've never had a reason to argue with them."

Emeric knew what it was before she finished. He knew it the way he knew a great many things in this world, which was suddenly and completely and from a distance no one around him could see across, because the description was not a mystery to him, it was a diagnosis, and he had the framework and they did not. There was no curse. There was dead air, the heavy spent stuff that pooled in the bottom of a deep working with no draft to move it, air that had given up whatever in it a man's blood could use and kept only the part that killed, and it had no color to warn you and no smell to warn you and no violence to warn you, it simply lay there at the foot of the shaft being patient, and a man climbed down into it and grew tired and sat to rest and the rest went on being restful right up until it was death. It looked like a haunting because it behaved like one. The mine was empty and the strong men died in it anyway, and to a town without the one idea that explained it, an idea nobody on this continent had ever had reason to hold, cursed was not a stupid answer. It was the best answer available. It was simply wrong, and Emeric was, as far as he knew, the only living man who could stand in that hall and know it was wrong.

He did not say so at once. He had learned, at the cost of nearly failing an entire barony over a river and two families, that being right was the cheapest part of the work and the least persuasive, that a truth handed to people who had no framework to catch it landed as arrogance or sorcery or both, and that the standing to be believed had to be earned in the world and not argued for in a hall. So he asked questions instead. How deep. How long the tiredness took to come on. Whether it came at the same depth every time or crept higher on still days. Whether a flame guttered before a man did. Bryda answered what she knew and did not pretend to know what she didn't, and every answer she gave him fit the diagnosis tighter, the depth, the stillness, the lamp going low, the flame as the first thing to die because a flame needed the same living air a man did and needed it sooner, and by the end of it Emeric was certain past the reach of doubt, and was also turning over a second thing, quieter and colder, that he did not say to her at all.

Renner had paid a fortune for a secret survey. Renner had sunk the deepest shaft in the district on the strength of it. And when the shaft killed his men, Renner, a baron so short of coin that he had skimmed his good veins to ruin and then marched on Kaldo in a gamble that got him killed, had not filled the cursed hole in and walked away from it. He had sealed it and kept it, and let a story grow over it that kept every other man in the district away from it, and a man does not guard an empty grave. You abandon a working that has nothing in it. You seal and story and guard a working that has something in it you cannot yet reach. Emeric filed that thought where he filed the ones he was not ready to act on, and it sat there through the rest of the survey, humming faintly, the way a wrong sum hums before you find the error, and he let it hum and said nothing.

Bryda gave him the shaft freely, which surprised him and then did not. To her it was the cursed hole that had killed her townsmen and embarrassed her treasury, a liability she was glad to make someone else's, and if the strange prince who had already taken her loyalty and her ore wanted to take her curse as well, she was a practical enough woman to let him and be rid of it. She gave him more than the shaft. She gave him Renner's papers, a strapped bundle of the dead man's records and correspondence and the survey among them, pulled from a chest she plainly had not opened in months, handing it across with the mild distaste of a widow passing on a husband's effects she had never wanted to read and now never would. "It's his hand and his dealings," she said. "I've no use for it and less liking. If there's anything in it your clerks want, they're welcome. I'd sooner it lived under your roof than mine." Emeric took the bundle and thanked her and did not open it, because there was nothing in a dead provincial baron's letters that could not wait for Ferra and a quiet evening, and he set it with the baggage to go back to Kaldo, not knowing, because he had no reason to know, that he was carrying home the thread that would eventually lead up out of these hills and across a kingdom and into a room he had not yet learned to fear.

The question of the shaft he settled the same night, alone, the way he settled the questions that mattered, which was by refusing the answer everyone expected him to give. The reasonable thing, the lordly thing, was to have the hole ventilated by other men's labor and then send other men down to prove it safe, and the reasonable thing was exactly the thing he would not do, and he knew why the moment he examined it, and the knowing was old and specific and lived in a part of him the transfer had not touched. He had stood once at the head of a village that would not believe him about water, and he had wanted, in the cold engine of his mind, to prove himself right by letting a warned family drink and die, and he had not done it, and the not-doing was the closest thing he had to a spine in this world. He had told both families the same true thing and let their own choices sort them, and stayed clean, and it had nearly cost him everything and had in the end saved the barony, and the lesson he had taken from it was narrow and permanent. He would not spend a man on a danger only he could read. It was the one rule he had that was not an engineering rule, and it was not negotiable, and it meant that the man who understood the dead air was the man who had to go down into it, because to send anyone else was to send him blind into a thing Emeric alone had eyes for, and Emeric would rather die of his own knowledge than live off another man's ignorance.

Ward hated it with a completeness that was almost restful to be near, the way a wall is restful, and said so in the flat few words he used for the things he cared about most. "You're the barony," he said. "You go in a hole that kills men and don't come out, the barony's in the hole. That's the whole of my objection and I'll not dress it up." It was a good objection and Emeric could not answer it, because it was true, he was a single point of failure in a system he had spent two years engineering to have none, and the irony of the man who preached distributed redundancy climbing personally down the one shaft in the district that killed whoever entered it was not lost on him and did not move him. Some things a man did not delegate, and the reasons were not always reasonable, and he had made his peace with being, in exactly this one way, unreasonable. "I'll not go in blind," he told Ward, which was the true thing and the only comfort he had to offer. "I can read the air the way I read stone. I'll feel it thicken before it can touch me, and I'll come up the moment it does. I'm not going down there to be brave. I'm going down there because I'm the only man who can go down there and be careful." Ward looked at him a long moment with the flat unreadable patience of a man who has decided to lose an argument he is right about, and then went to see about ropes.

They opened the seal in the grey light of the next morning, four of them at the head-frame and the whole silent weight of the town's belief pressing on the place from a distance, no one come to watch because no one came near the cursed shaft, which suited Emeric, who had never wanted an audience for the parts he was going to get wrong. The timbers came away and the dark stood there behind them, patient and complete, breathing up out of the ground a coolness that a frightened man would have called a presence and Emeric called, accurately, a temperature gradient. He lit a lamp and held it into the opening and watched the flame, and the flame at the mouth burned clean and yellow and steady, which told him only that the mouth was fine, which he had known, because the killing did not happen at the mouth. It happened at the bottom, in the still pooled dark a hundred feet down and more, where the dead air lay waiting with a patience it had kept through three years of being called a demon by people who had never been given the one word that would have made it only chemistry. He rigged the harness and checked the rope and checked it again, and set the lamp to his belt where he could watch it die if it meant to, and put his hand flat against the cold stone at the shaft's lip and let his sense go down into it the way it went down into everything, slow, the rock resolving under the faculty the way a dark room resolves when the eye gives up on the lamp and simply learns the dark, and he felt the shaft fall away beneath him into a depth his eyes would never have followed, and somewhere down at the bottom of it, past the reach of even the sense, the thing the whole hill had been guarding waited to be found by the one man alive who could survive the finding.

Then he stepped off the edge, into the dark that had killed better men than Renner had ever commanded, and began, hand under hand, to go down.
