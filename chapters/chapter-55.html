Book 2, Chapter 16: Flood Slow

The first bottle came up out of the passage mouth at ten to six on the Sunday morning on the end of forty fathoms of line, and Finn, kneeling on the Challenger's casing with his sleeves pushed up, poured it into the lime water and watched it go white.

Not cloudy. White. Milk white, in the time it took him to swirl the jar once.

"That's a lot," said Bram, beside him.

Finn took the second jar off the rack and the strip of lead paper out of its packet with the tongs, and held the strip over the bottle's neck where the water was still fizzing up out of it, and it went black. Not grey; black, from the edge in, like paper going into a fire.

"And that," said Bram.

Finn looked at the black strip. He'd done this test a hundred times in the tank on the Cradle, in the winter, with Imani standing over him with her watch, on water they'd fouled on purpose, a little at a time, with a measured pinch of this and that from Bram's cupboard. He'd never once seen the paper go like that. He'd never seen it go past grey.

"Rotten eggs," he said. "And the other. The choking one. Lots."

"Is it the same everywhere?"

It wasn't. They'd drawn six bottles from six marks on the line, from the passage mouth in toward the dome, and the two nearest the mouth were only cloudy; and the four past them were white, and black, and the last one, the deepest, had come up warm in Finn's hand, so that he'd nearly dropped it.

There was no test for whether there was enough to breathe. Bram had said so in the shed in the winter, and again last night at the table: there wasn't a mundane test for it you could trust with a gas that burned. You couldn't put a flame in it. So you didn't ask the water whether it was safe. You assumed it wasn't, until it was proved, and planned for that.

Bram took the sheet with the marks and the six results on it and went across the Challenger's casing to the watch boat lying alongside, where Taurre was waiting with his slate; and Finn knelt on the wet casing with the black strip in the tongs and looked at the water of the passage mouth, green and still and perfectly ordinary, and thought about a man in a bell sixty feet down at the far end of it, breathing.

Taurre made his call at half past six. He made it in the watch boat with Bram and Emeric and Noll and two of his own people round him and Ilenne putting it into Kaldo, and Finn heard all of it from the casing because Taurre didn't lower his voice. It was his water. He said so first.

The dome first. The work dome by the seal, the hard room Haurin's crew had worked from, the one that was failing: nobody went near it until it couldn't fail. It would be flooded, slowly, through its own vents, over the morning, until it held the sea at the sea's own weight. A full dome couldn't break. That was the order. Until its load was falling, nobody went down the passage at all, Haurin or no Haurin.

Then one route. The passage side, the side of Haurin's bell away from the dome. One diver and one standby. For minutes at a time, and back.

And a line. Taurre drew it on his slate, and Ilenne said it in Kaldo, and Noll said it again in figures for Finn: a mark on the air. When the Challenger's air came down to the mark, everybody came out. Whatever the dome was doing. Whatever Haurin was doing. Everybody.

"How much above the mark have we got?" said Emeric.

Noll had the bank's gauge readings on a card. He'd had them since four, when he'd gone round all ten flasks with a lamp.

"Sixty thousand litres, near enough," said Noll. "She holds seventy-five full. The rush took fifteen yesterday. She's been on top all night and I couldn't run the compressor; Taurre won't have anything of ours making a noise in his water. Not after yesterday." He looked at the card. "Taurre's mark's at twenty. That's for one try going wrong, and both men out, and the standby bringing the other out if he has to, and Haurin across, and Finn's stops. Everything over twenty's what we've got to work with." He put the card in his pocket. "A full bank'd give us four hours, five. A fifth short, call it three. Three and a half if nobody breathes hard."

"And the dome?"

Taurre answered that one himself, through Ilenne, short.

"By noon," said Ilenne. "He says the dome is full by noon, if the vents keep clear. Maybe one. Then it can't break."

Nobody said what everybody on the boat was thinking, which was that the bell's air had been going bad since twenty past three yesterday afternoon, and that noon was a long way off.



The bell's own air was the trouble, and it was Bram who said so, at the table in the guest rooms at seven with the passage drawings spread out.

"It's got gill plates," said Bram. "Same as all their bells. Making air out of the sea. And the sea round it's foul now, so they're making less, and what they're making's bad. And it's got a store, Taurre says. Bladders. For the first hours." He put his finger on the bell on the drawing. "That'll be about gone. So we feed him."

The feed was a third hose. Off the Challenger's manifest, through its own regulator, down the passage to the bell, and in through the water door, a slow steady trickle of clean air a hair above the bell's own pressure, so that the old air bled out at the top of the water sheet in bubbles and nothing came in from the sea. Bram would set the flow on a limiter. He'd built it the night before out of a needle valve and a brass orifice the size of a pin's head and he'd tested it on the casing at five with Noll timing it.

"And he shuts his gill plates off," said Bram. "At their valve. So they stop pulling the foul stuff in."

"That's the oxygen," said Emeric.

"That's the oxygen. Then there's the other thing." Bram looked at Finn. "Every breath he takes, he breathes out what he doesn't want. In a shut bell it builds up. Feed or no feed. The feed pushes it out slow, but not fast enough on its own, not on what we can spare off the bank." He put both hands flat on the table. "He needs a scrubber. Something that drinks it."

Finn knew what he meant. They'd had scrubbers in the shed, the mask's cartridge, the spent-stone kind. He said so.

"Stone cartridges are for a mask," said Bram. "One man for an hour. This is a man in a bell for most of a day, and his air's wet, and the bell's dripping, and I've not got enough of anything to fill a box that size." He was quiet a moment. "But I know who drinks bad air with a dish."



Neris was at her trough when they came round behind the kilns at eight, Bram and Ilenne and Finn, and she looked at the three of them the way she'd looked at the land men on Thursday, Bram said afterwards, slate from the throat down; and then she saw Bram's face, and stopped stirring.

Bram told her. He told her about the bell and the man in it and the air, and what he wanted, and why him, and why her: because she'd said her lye drank the bad air in a kiln cellar if you left a dish of it out. He said he wanted that, in a box, that a man could work in a wet bell for half a day and that wouldn't turn to mud or spill. Ilenne put it over. Finn watched Neris's face while she did, and saw it change, at the man in the bell, and change again at what Bram wanted, and then go very still.

She said something.

"She says yes," said Ilenne. "She says she knows Haurin. Everybody knows Haurin. He mends things." She listened. "She says one thing."

"Anything."

"She says the watch writes it down. That it's hers." Ilenne's voice was very level. "Not the guild's. Not the guild's mark. Hers. Neris. Her name, on the watch's record, on the slate. She says they took her soap and put their mark on it. She says this one has her name on it or she doesn't make it."

Bram turned round to the watch boat's runner, who had come with them for exactly this sort of thing, and said, "Tell Taurre."

The runner went. She came back in half an hour, running, with a slate; and on the slate, in the watch's cut marks, was a line that Ilenne read out to Neris in her own speech, and that Neris made her read twice.

The charge for the bell: made by Neris of Sael, by name, for the watch.

Neris read it again, to herself. Then she took off her grey gloves and went to her kiln.

Finn watched her do it, because there was nothing else for him to do until noon and he couldn't sit still.

She'd had the beads for a week, she said through Ilenne: little round things of grey clay the size of peas, hundreds and hundreds of them in a basket, rolled for a glaze she'd meant to try. She fired them empty first. The kiln was still hot from the night before, and she raked it up and put the beads in on a tray and shut it; and while they fired she made the slurry in a tub. Shell lime, slaked, wet, the most of it. A little of her lye through it, measured with a horn spoon. Stirred, with her gloves back on, until it was like cream.

"That's soda lime," said Bram.

He said it quietly, half to himself, standing at her elbow watching the tub. Ilenne didn't put it over and Neris didn't ask.

"It's what they'd use," Bram said to Finn. "Where... in the books. For this. For drinking breath out of air. Lime and a little lye." He watched her spoon. "She doesn't know it's got a name. She just knows it works."

When the beads came out she let them cool till she could hold them. Then she tipped them into the cream and turned them over with her hands until every one was coated, and drained them, and spread them on a tray, and put the tray back in the kiln's cooling end, where it was warm and not hot.

"Not hot," said Ilenne, putting over what Neris said without looking up. "If it's hot the lime goes dead. Warm. Dry. So the lime stays in the clay. So air goes through and it doesn't fall to mud in the wet." She paused. "She calls them..." Ilenne said the word. "The little lungs. That's what it means. Little lungs."

And then Neris did a last thing that Bram had to have explained three times.

She took a small handful of the beads out of the drying tray before they'd gone in, and dipped them in a dish of something dark that she'd ground from a lichen off the reef, the stuff she used for a glaze, and set them aside on their own. They'd go at the end, she said. The last of the bed, at the outlet. When the bad air started getting through the lime, past the end of it, the lichen beads would turn.

"Turn what?" said Bram.

Neris held up her hand and made a colour. Not with her skin; with her fingers, the way the hand signs went: a slow change, closing and opening. Then she said something to Ilenne.

"She says they change," said Ilenne. "Like a face. When it's time to change it. She says she doesn't know how long. Nobody made this before. She says change it early. Don't wait for the beads. The beads are only so you know."

"It's a warning," said Bram, slowly. "Not a gauge."

"She says that," said Ilenne. "Yes."

Bram took out the notebook. Not his; Tess's, the cloth one, with nine pages of questions and four of answers in the front. He turned to a clean page at the back and wrote at the top of it, in his big slanting hand, Neris of Sael, and underneath it everything she'd done, the beads, the firing, the cream, warm not hot, the lichen at the end. Finn watched him write the name at the top first, before any of the rest.

By eleven the canister was built: a sealed clay jar the size of a man's thigh, baffled inside, with a little hand bellows lashed to its side. You worked the bellows and it drew the bell's air in at one end, through a pad of damp felt to catch the dust, through the bed of little lungs, through the lichen beads at the outlet, through a second pad of felt, and out. Two spare charges beside it, sealed in wax. Neris showed Finn how you opened it and changed the bed, with her gloves on, and made him do it twice.

"He works it himself," said Bram. "Haurin. He works the bellows."

"Why?"

"Because it'll give him something to do with his hands," said Bram, "besides looking at that water door."



At ten past one the dome's load was falling, and Taurre opened the route.

Finn went in at twenty past.

The Challenger lay in the passage mouth on her ballast, hovering, nose in, at sixty feet, with her floods lit: two great white eyes of light boring down the passage into the dark. Finn went out of her by her side hatch on the hose. One hose to the mouthpiece in his teeth, off her manifest through the first stage, so that what came down it was the bank's air a margin over the sea's own push round him; and at his mouth the second stage, which gave it him at exactly what the sea pushed where he was, breath by breath, so that he didn't have to think about it. A small flask on his back for the swim out, if the hose went. A lamp. A line. The third hose coiled on his arm with Bram's limiter on its end in a clay cup, and the canister hung from his belt on a lanyard, heavy, knocking against his leg.

And behind him, on his own tether, the standby.

The standby hadn't come out of the side hatch. He'd come out of the bottom of her, through the belly lock, the little flood-and-drain chamber Bram had built into her keel for the changed, which nobody had ever used in earnest; and he'd come out on its own mask with the lock's lifeline going back into her belly and a second knight in the flooded lock behind him to tend it, because out of that lock you went in pairs. He was one of the K Endeavor's four flight knights, a big quiet man who'd eaten his stone at the castle and braced the way Zale braced, and he could go down and up without a stop, which Finn couldn't. He hung in the water ten feet behind Finn in the floods' light with his tether in one fist and waited.

On the surface above, the watch boat had merfolk at its rail looking down at the floods. Finn could see them, small and pale through the water, their faces turned to the white light boring down into their sea, and he couldn't read their faces, and he didn't need to. He'd seen the same look on people at home watching a fire they knew they shouldn't stand so close to.

"Bank," said Noll's voice, in the earpiece of Finn's helm, small and tinny down the hose's speaking tube. "Fifty-nine and a half. On the gauge. A fifth down from full, and we started a fifth down. Breathe slow."

Finn breathed slow. He went down the passage.

The passage was black rock, worn smooth, a tunnel twice a man's height with the floods behind him throwing his own shadow ahead of him long and thin on the walls; and the further he went the warmer it got, and the water went from green to yellow-brown and then to a brown that the light didn't go into far. He could taste it at the edge of his mouthpiece. Eggs and struck matches. He didn't breathe through his nose.

The bell was at the end of it, where the passage turned. A tall round thing of glass going up into the dark, tied down by a heavy chain to the rock below, and in the side of it nearest him, at the height of his chest, a round water door: a sheet of water standing in the hole in the glass, clear, upright, with the light going through it.

Haurin was on the other side of the sheet.

Finn saw his face first, close to the water door, lit white by the floods from behind Finn's shoulder: a merfolk man on his legs in the bell's air, young, broad, with his hair loose and wet and his eyes huge in the light. His colour was all wrong. It went over him while Finn watched, in waves, grey and dark and grey, too fast. His mouth was open. He was breathing fast and shallow, the way you breathed when the air was thick with what you'd already breathed.

And he wanted out.

Finn saw it happen. Saw him see the land man in the water with a light behind him and a hose in his teeth, and saw what he thought it meant, and saw his hands come up toward the sheet of water in the door. If he put his hands through it, he'd be in the sea. If he went through it into the sea he'd swim, because he was a merfolk and that was what he did, and his gills would open, and they'd take this water. This brown, warm, stinking water. Finn knew exactly what that would do. Bram had told him on the casing in four words.

Finn put his hand flat against the sheet.

Not through it. Against it, from the outside, the palm pressed to the water's face so that it bulged round his fingers and held. And he shook his head. Slowly, hugely, side to side, so that the whole helm went with it. No.

Haurin's hands stopped an inch from the sheet. His face came close to it. He said something; Finn saw his mouth move and heard nothing at all. He said it again, faster, and his hands moved, and Finn had forty words of his language, perhaps, from the aft deck and the primer, and none of them were for this.

But he had the signs.

He took his hand off the sheet and laid it across his own eyes, flat, the way Ilenne had taught them on the aft deck every second evening for a fortnight, the way Zale had done it at the yard with his wife-to-be holding his wrist. The slate. For fear. For something is wrong here. For I am afraid.

He held it there. Then he took it away and looked at Haurin through the sheet, and pointed at himself, and laid it across his eyes again.

I'm afraid too.

It was true. That was why it worked. He'd been afraid since the bottle went white, and he was afraid now, here, at the end of a dark passage in poisoned water with a hose in his teeth and a man about to drown himself in front of him; and Haurin, through a sheet of clear water at arm's length, saw that it was true.

Haurin's hands came down.

He stood in the bell with his chest going and looked at the land man in the water. Then, slowly, he put his own hand across his own eyes, and held it there, and took it away.

Finn nodded. Then he did the thing he'd come for, before either of them could think about anything else.

He passed the third hose through the sheet first. He pushed its end through the water door, the limiter on the end in its clay cup, slowly, so the sheet closed round the hose and held, and Haurin took it from the other side with both hands; and then the air came. Finn saw it. A thin steady stream of bubbles going up from the top edge of the water door into the dark, the bell's old air bleeding out, and the bell's air sheet bowing very slightly outward toward him and holding. Bram's limiter. A hair over the bell's own pressure. Not enough to push the sheet out. Enough that nothing came in.

Then he made the sign for the gill plates. Ilenne had given it him last night, at the table, with his hands in hers, a dozen times: the four fingers drawn across the side of the neck, the gills, and then closed into a fist. Shut.

Haurin looked at him. Then he looked up, at something in the bell's roof that Finn couldn't see, and reached up, and turned something, hard, with both hands.

Then the canister. It went through the sheet heavy, with a lurch, and Haurin caught it against his chest and nearly went over backwards. Then the spares, one, two. Finn showed him through the water with his own hands how the bellows went, squeeze and let go, squeeze and let go, slow; and pointed at the lichen beads at the outlet end, in their little window; and made the colour sign Neris had made, the closing and opening, and pointed at Haurin's face and then at the beads, so he'd know. They change like a face. When they change, it's time.

Haurin sat down on the bell's floor with the canister in his lap and worked the bellows.

"Bank fifty-two," said Noll in his ear. "Come back to the hold, Finn. That's the order. Out of the deep. Come back up and wait."

Finn put his palm flat against the sheet once more, against the water, and held it there.

Then he went back up the passage on the hose, and the standby went with him, and Haurin's face in the bell's water door got smaller behind them in the light until the passage turned and it was gone.



The hold was a shallow ledge on the passage mouth's outer lip under the watch boat, twenty feet down, where the water was clean and green and the light came down from the surface, and he hung there on the hose with his hand on a ring of rock and waited.

That was the rule. He didn't wait at depth. Every breath at sixty feet cost the bank nearly three times what it cost at the top; Noll had made him say it back to him a dozen times. So between trips he came up to the hold and breathed slow, and the bank lasted, and the dome filled.

It filled slowly. Taurre's people reported it down the speaking tube through Ilenne every half hour: so much water in, so much load off, the vents clear. Noll called the bank every quarter hour, in his flat voice, the figure and nothing else.

"Forty-six."

"Forty-one."

The standby went down once on his own at three, on Finn's time, because Finn's minutes at depth were getting long for his stops and the standby's didn't matter: down the passage to the bell to look, through the sheet, and back without a stop, as easily as a man going down a stair and up again. He came back and made the sign for well across the water at Finn: Haurin working the bellows, the beads at the outlet beginning to turn, the colour Neris had said they'd start. Finn signed back: change the charge. The standby went down again with a spare and came back. Haurin had changed it himself, he signed. Early. Before the beads had finished turning. And then had sat and watched the beads instead of the water door.

"Thirty-six," said Noll.

"Thirty-one."

That was all that came down the tube. Finn hung on his ring of rock in the green water and listened to nobody saying it. Thirty-one. Taurre's line was at twenty. The rush had taken fifteen yesterday, in under a minute, getting Ressar out. That was fifteen thousand litres he'd have had to hang here on now, and didn't.

"Twenty-eight," said Noll. And then, after a pause that was longer than his pauses: "The dome's full. Taurre says. Full and holding. It can't go." Another pause. "Go and get him, Finn."

Finn went and got him.

He went down the passage fast, with the standby behind him, to the bell. Haurin was standing up at the water door with the canister set down on the floor behind him and his face close to the sheet, waiting; he'd seen the floods coming. His colour was steadier. Not right. Steadier.

Finn showed him the second mouthpiece. It hung on a short hose off his own second stage, on his chest, for exactly this: a second mouth for a second man. He held it up so Haurin could see it through the water. Then he put his own back in his teeth and breathed on it, slowly, twice, and took it out and held up the spare again and pointed at Haurin's mouth.

Air. Not water. Across the bad stretch, on air, mouth to mouth, until the water was clean.

Haurin understood. They breathed air on their legs, the merfolk; Ilenne had breathed it for two years. He looked at the mouthpiece, and at the brown water between the bell and the clean green a long way down the passage, and Finn saw him decide.

He came through the sheet with his eyes shut and his mouth already open. Finn had the mouthpiece in it before the water closed over his face. Haurin bit down on it so hard Finn felt it in his own chest through the hose, and clung to him, an arm round his shoulders and his other hand clenched in the harness, and they went up the passage together like that, slowly, the two of them on one hose with the standby behind, through the brown and the yellow and into the green; and the moment the water went clean round them, Finn felt Haurin's gills open against his own neck where they were pressed together, and Haurin let go of the mouthpiece and breathed the sea.

His people took him at the passage mouth. Three of them, from the station, waiting in the green water with a sled; and they took him from Finn's arms without a word and turned away with him, slowly, up, by their own rule, on their own count, because a body that had breathed compressed air at sixty feet for most of a day had to give it back slowly, and they knew that better than anybody. Finn hung on the hose and watched them go up into the light.

"Bank twenty-four," said Noll. "Come up, Finn. Do your stops. Do every one of them."

He did every one of them. The standby went straight up past him through the water to the belly lock and in, and Finn heard the lock drain down the tube, and the standby came out of it on the inside dry and breathing and needing nothing, and Finn hung on the hose at ten feet and then five for his minutes, by the dive team's table, with the water going from green to bright and the watch boat's keel over him.

When he came up the last of the way Imani was kneeling on the Challenger's casing with the oxygen bottle in her lap, and she took his helm off him before he'd got his hand on the rail, and put the mask on his face, and held it there.

Finn breathed it for a minute. Then he pulled it off, and leaned over the side of the casing, and threw up into the sea.

"That's normal," said Imani.

"It's not," said Finn, with his forehead on the casing.

"It's normal for you," said Imani. "Put the mask back on."



The merfolk's diving station was a wet dome on the inner wall below the watch house, with a bell inside it for land bodies and for their own when they needed air, and Imani got there at six in the evening in a pilot's boat with her bag, because she wanted to see Haurin, and because she'd been told twice that she couldn't and had decided that that meant she'd have to ask in person.

They let her in. She wasn't sure afterwards why. She thought it was the revolver at her hip, logged at the water door, and the bag; she thought she'd looked like someone who wasn't going to go away.

The healers were women. All four of them. They worked in the bell on their legs, at a long low table with a merfolk man lying on it under a fur, and they didn't stop working when she came in, and she didn't ask them to.

Haurin lay on his back with his eyes shut, breathing the bell's air in long slow breaths. His colour was grey and steady. One of the healers had her hands flat on his chest and was counting something in a low voice; another had her fingers on the inside of his wrist and her eyes on a small glass at her elbow, a slow sand-glass, and another was writing on a slate. Imani watched them for a while and knew within a minute that they knew more about what was happening inside him than she did.

They said so, when Ilenne came in behind her and put it over. They said it kindly. The deep did things to a body that had breathed air at depth for a day. It put the air into the blood by slow degrees, and it had to come out the same way, or the blood would boil with it in the joints and the brain; and they knew how slow, for their own bodies, to the breath. They'd been bringing their own people up out of deep work for longer than anybody had written anything down. Haurin would lie here two days. They'd tell him when he could swim.

Imani said that was more than she'd have known to do.

Then one of them, the eldest, asked her about the air in the bell. About what Haurin had breathed. The feed, the beads, the bad air going out at the top of the door. She'd heard from the watch. She wanted to know how the land people had known that a shut room fills with a man's own breath and that you had to take it out, not only put new air in.

So Imani told them. She told them about Kaldo's mines, and the bad air in the bottom of a shaft that put lamps out and men to sleep, and the miners' rule; about the cave dive, and the cartridges in the masks; about what the body did when it breathed its own breath back. She told them about the headache that came first, and the fast breathing, and the hands going numb, so that they'd know to look for it. The eldest listened to all of it with her hands folded, and when Imani had finished she said something to the healer with the slate, and the healer wrote.

"She says she didn't know the headache came first," said Ilenne quietly. "She says they always thought it was the cold."

Imani looked at the slate going down in the merfolk's cut marks and thought that this was the first time anything she knew had been written down by anybody in Aumere.

The eldest had one more question. She'd heard it from her own people at the passage mouth. Two land men had come up out of that water. One had hung on his hose at the hold for half an hour, coming up a little at a time, as a body should. The other had gone straight up past him into the ship's belly without a stop, as no body should, and climbed out dry. She wanted to know how.

Imani said she couldn't answer that one.

The eldest considered that, and then said something to the healer with the slate, and the healer wrote that down too.

Oussa came in at seven.

She came straight through the water door into the bell on her legs with her hair still wet from the swim down, and stopped, and looked at the man on the table; and her colour, that had been the flat grey of the shallows when Imani glimpsed her in the Keepers' hall the night before, went up through her all at once, warm, as if somebody had opened a lamp. She went to the table and knelt by it and took Haurin's hand in both of hers.

Imani watched where her hands went.

She hadn't meant to. She'd been watching it in all of them since the door, without knowing she was doing it, and she saw it now with Oussa and knew what she'd been watching. Oussa held his hand. She put her other hand flat on his chest, over the healer's, and then on his arm, and on his hair. And her hands came nowhere near his throat. They went round it. Every time. Without thinking. The way you'd keep a hand away from a hot pan on a stove you'd cooked at all your life.

The healers did it too. She'd seen it and not seen it. All four of them, working on him for hours: chest, wrist, arm, face. Never the throat.

Never touch the throat. It was the second courtesy. Ilenne had taught it on the aft deck. Imani had learned it as a rule. She hadn't known until now that here it wasn't a rule at all. It was just how hands went.

There was a woman at the station who wasn't a healer, and Imani noticed her an hour after Oussa came, when Ilenne and Zale came in through the water door together for news.

She was a deep-house woman. Imani could tell that much by now: the coat with the dull shine, the stones at the wrist, the way she stood. Older than Ilenne, younger than the eldest healer. Her hair was bound up in a way none of the others' was. She'd been working all evening, carrying, fetching, holding a basin, at the far end of the bell, quietly, the way some court women did, Ilenne said later, at the station; they came up from the floor and worked there because it was the one place up here where a woman of the great houses could be useful without anybody saying she shouldn't.

And she hadn't watched the patient. Imani realised it all at once. Every time Imani had looked up from Haurin, the woman's eyes had been on Imani. On her hands. On what she did and how she did it. Not unfriendly. Intent. The way Imani herself watched a new surgeon in a theatre to see what kind of surgeon he was.

They didn't speak. There was nothing for them to say; Imani had no words for her and the woman offered none.

Ilenne came to the table with Zale behind her. Zale had come down the stages for it; he was wet to the waist and grey round the mouth from the cold of the shafts and he stood at the table's foot and looked at Haurin with his hands in his pockets, the way he'd have looked at one of his own crew pulled out of a sea.

"Is he all right?"

"He's all right," said Imani. "They say two days."

Zale let out a breath. He put his hand on Ilenne's shoulder, briefly, the way he did now, a man not sure yet whether he was allowed to; and Ilenne turned her head and looked up at him.

Her colour slipped.

It was very small. Imani saw it only because she'd seen it before and knew where to look. At the throat, under the line of the jaw, just for a breath: the blue. Deep-water blue, dark, there and held back down again, in a bell full of merfolk who could read a colour across a hall.

Imani glanced at the deep-house woman at the far end of the bell.

The woman had seen it. She was standing with a basin in her hands, quite still, and her eyes weren't on Imani any more. They were on Ilenne's throat. Then they came, slowly, to Zale; and then they went down to the basin, and she went on with what she'd been doing as if nothing had happened at all.

Ilenne said, on the way out, that the woman's name was Orrane. She was a widow, of one of the deep houses. She came up to the station sometimes. That was all Ilenne said, and Imani didn't ask more.



Taurre came at nine and said his finding once.

He said it in the station's bell with the healers and Oussa and Haurin and the land people there, standing at the table's foot with his slate, because it was the station and a man the watch had got out of the water was lying in it, and that was where the watch said such things. Ilenne put it into Kaldo for Imani.

The clicks had been the glass. The held glass of the structure behind the seal, losing its holding under the heat, little by little and then all at once. The failure had begun at the structure.

That was all. Not whose. Not the seal, or the bench that had passed it, or the house that had built it. Not the land ship. He said what the water had shown him, and stopped.

Imani thought, standing there with her bag at her feet, that he'd chosen every word of it.

"And the land ship?" she said. "The nodes?"

Ilenne asked it. Taurre looked at Imani with his colour level.

"He says the nodes have her on the wall," said Ilenne. "Loud. Before it went. That's still what they have." She listened. "He says getting a man out of a bell is a good thing to have done. He says it doesn't say anything about the wall."

Imani nodded. She'd known he'd say that. It was true.

On the table, Haurin opened his eyes and looked up at the bell's glass roof, and at the green dark beyond it, and at Oussa's face; and Oussa's hands went past his throat to his hair, and stayed there.
