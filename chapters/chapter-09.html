Book 2, Batch 9: Two out of Three

Chapters 67–74 | Tuesday 1 September – Sunday 20 September, Year 17

The fleet has left the dwarf holds of the Sea Reach. At the Anvil, Emeric saw his own corner mark on a two-thousand-year-old war order and began an inquiry he once thought unnecessary. Mochi, the king slime, followed him to the fleet and is now aboard, unbonded and adored. Runa is out of her cuff, growling at Mochi and sleeping beside it. Ledra has gone home, so the fleet has no interpreter, and Kaldo has promised no coal past the wall for a term. At home, Keller's audit found Cinderhale's armed watch paid lawfully, and Emeric sent the only spare half-company to Wayford rather than to Cinderhale, where Corbin wanted observers.

The fleet now turns to the Sun Shore and the Sabir, a people who solved the desert a thousand years before Kaldo dug its first well, and whose answer is law: nobody draws water without a share. Kaldo's first act on their river is to fill its tanks without one. The court's engineer, not Emeric, names what more pumping would do to the water under the sand, and the court's own procedure, not Kaldo's rifles, decides who was robbed. The stop carries one social conflict, over who gets water at the court's quota hearing, and one mystery, rising from the dry edge where the wells are dropping.

On that dry edge there is a charity that does everything Kaldo does, with no stones, out of stubborn kindness: water without a share, healing without a fee, orphans read to at night. Its High Mother feeds Emeric before she lets him talk. He agrees with two of her three tenets, bets money that she is real, and tells her the worst thing he ever thought about doing to the nobility. She agrees with every word. Meanwhile, at home, a girl who kept the minutes is dead, because a half-company went to Wayford instead of Cinderhale.

Chapter 67: The River Gap

From Tuesday 1 to Saturday 5 September, the fleet travels about 710 miles: east along the coast to the Sun River's mouth, through the river's gap in the coastal range, and south up the river through the basin. On the leg Ferra writes down why they are going. The fleet needs water and food, since the dwarves' stores were iron, not bread. The relief pair's rendezvous will be on the Sun River in October. The Sabir are the winged couriers' trade partners, and the road to the next stop runs through their court. And the Sabir built the old water works of the ancient common lands, one record Kaldo can read in stone.

Mochi has spent the day warming on a crate in the sun on the K Endeavor's deck. Runa growls at it, lies down beside it to make a point, and wakes up on top of it. Imani finds them in the morning and wakes neither. At sea, on Kaldo's own deck where no host's leave is needed, Imani reopens the log she closed at the orcs' sea mark for a third session. Emeric calls one hand this time, rather than having it come unasked, and it comes more slowly than at Aumere. He eats Dorrit's whole pot of broth and sleeps through the next relay climb. Runa smells fire on the deck before she sees anything, goes to the far rail with Mochi, and stays there until he has gone below. On Wednesday a squall hits the coast while Runa is on deck for the wind. Ward, crossing to the K Endeavor, clips her harness to the jackline himself and stands between her and the rail until it passes, saying nothing at all. She stops being afraid of him that afternoon and never says so.

The desert lies beyond the relay chain's last drum, so Ferra spends four relays to reach the river anchorage, launched over water at each dusk with Sorrel and a dragon watch at the rails. Five remain. On Wednesday night they lie at the river mouth's deep water under the orc house's pilot, as they did in July, and Kettrin sends word that the relief pair's fallback berth still stands. Zale's ship and the K Tam cannot climb the gorge's falls, so they stay at the river mouth together. On Thursday the air hulls fly low through the gap over the falls and come out over the basin: the river a green ribbon, the salt flats white, then sand. Sorrel's morning flight reports nothing to the west but empty sand, which is how a dry-edge town looks from a mile off. Ferra writes "nothing (?)". On Friday evening they set down on the river below the court's city. In one line to Harrold, Ferra moves the rendezvous: with no Sabir coast to stand off, the relief pair will come up the gap to the Sun River at the court's reach, 1 to 10 October, with the river-mouth fallback unchanged. Harrold acknowledges in one line.

At dawn on Saturday the K Endeavor tops up her distillers' feed from the river, as every hull has done at every anchorage since Aumere. A court water clerk in a reed boat sees the hose in the water. The river above the city's intakes is a protected reach. Her throat darkens; she thinks they have come to steal it. Nobody aboard has the words to answer. The court sends down Ghassan, its water engineer, with Qamar, an agricultural scholar who has never seen a hull and wants to see the greenhouse deck: a clerk who suspects, an engineer who measures and a scholar who is curious. An interpreter is hired from the city's orc treaty-house by mid-morning, after an hour of Emeric's fading orc words making things worse. She is an orc who puts Sabir into orc, and she takes Ledra's empty berth under contract.

At the court's table Ferra buys a season's river share for the fleet's intakes, paid in bar iron, which the Sabir lack. The clerk writes the hulls into the river roll, and Ferra enters it as a purchase, not a fine. When the clerk asks which law covers humans at all, Ghassan reads an article from the court's book as a curiosity: water owed to humans and withheld until humans come asking, never used. Nobody aboard knows what it is worth, and Emeric does not ask. Then, hearing that the dry edge's wells are dropping, Emeric starts to offer Kaldo's drilling and a well stone. Ghassan stops him with three things: every new well in a district draws the same water as the old ones; more water on the low fields brings the salt up; and every drain must send its brine somewhere that somebody owns. Emeric's own line about aquifers comes second. The court's engineering office has noticed the dry edge's wells falling faster than any rain account explains and the ground sinking at two stations, and Ghassan is riding west to measure. Emeric asks to come. Ghassan agrees, in exchange for Kaldo's levels and a second pair of eyes, and because he wants to watch the man who knew about aquifers.

The court's caravan masters ask Ferra for conjuring water stones as canteens. She declines. Cheap water stones would break the court's hold on water, which she says aloud; that stones are not for sale at all, she writes only in her book. The court is not fooled about who Kaldo is. The charge about the old promise reached it months ago, and it knows the fleet's king is aboard, though it says nothing yet. Kaldo has paid in iron for water it already drank, and an engineer has told a king what wells do to the ground before the king could tell him.

Chapter 68: Seven Empty Waterskins

At home on Sunday 6 September, in Wayford, the half-company Emeric sent stops a mob at the grain store. Nan Coombe, the store's clerk, would have been under the door when it gave way, and for a moment she is, until a knight pulls her out by the belt. That night she writes a letter to the king. Nobody counts the lives saved here against those lost elsewhere.

In the court's city, Ferra visits Gisela, a human woman of about fifty from a household that has served the Sabir for generations, who keeps a house of other people's lost things beside the orc treaty-house: ancient weapons, failed experiments, bones of creatures nobody has seen alive, a jar she says is cursed, and forbidden books. She is playful, secretive and sharp about prices, and she collects because she fears things are being forgotten faster than anyone can save them, though she will not say so yet. Her door is propped open against the wind by a grey slab of carved script that a caravan scavenger brought in years ago from stonework a sandstorm bared on the dry edge. Mochi goes still on Emeric's shoulder and pulses at it. Emeric notices, Gisela notices him noticing, and the price of the slab goes up. She will not sell. Ferra wants a copy, and Gisela wants something of Kaldo's that could be lost. The trade they reach is one of the fleet's rubbings of the Isle's mural in exchange for Armand reading the slab under her eye and leaving his notes with her. Ferra calls it extortion. Gisela calls it a library. Armand finds the same script as on the Isle's ancient works, mostly a maintenance table he can half read, and one word he cannot place, a word for the one who sits, which he writes in the margin with a question mark. Under his breath, thinking of the Isle mural's top panels where figures bend over their beds laying seed, Emeric murmurs to Armand, "The sowers' hand, then." Ferra hears it and asks him that night, and he tells her about the panels. To everyone else they remain only the makers, and their writing the makers' script. Gisela also tells Emeric that the Children's founding is younger than the Children say, and will not tell him how she knows. Across the square, Neris argues about glass flux in the market.

At dusk the west road party sets out with Ghassan's survey and the court's escort: Emeric, Ferra, Imani, Bettany, Maeve, Ward, Arthur, Mira, Bren, four knights and the interpreter. Sabir caravans travel from dusk to dawn. Ghassan's levels show a well that has dropped a hand's width since spring.

At sunrise on Monday, at a registered caravan station about thirty miles out, Imani sees seven waterskins hung upside down on the well-post, one for each family on the station's list that will go without water today, and the station's clerk reads the seven names aloud. They are families driven off the salted low fields, walking west to the temple wells, where water needs no share. The court granted them a one-time travel share at this station, but the station's book says it has already been drawn. In the families' camp, Bettany's ward does the saving. An old man is past saving when she reaches him. The rest recover with shade, wet cloth, rest and rehydration salts mixed in the families' own bowls so they can make them again. Imani mends nothing all morning. Kaldo pays for urgent relief, with water casks hopped out by the K Discovery by day. Wafa, the station's young tally-keeper, keeps her own count in a knotted cord because the clerk keeps the book. Privately, because people tell Imani things, she says the share left on carts at night under the station's seal, with men camped at a ruined caravan halt five miles off. Two of the families' young men went after it yesterday and have not come back.

The objective is to recover the water and the two young men without treating every armed man on the dry edge as a criminal. That night the court's escort lays mirage across the approach, and the knights walk in behind a shimmer that looks like empty sand from a hundred yards; the Sabir are the best in the world at hiding in plain sight. Their night glass shows the halt in grey, and shows the mirage exactly as the eye does, because a mirage bends light. Then a frightened child in the camp sees the knights at the shimmer's edge and screams. Ward orders the knights to shield the child rather than keep the dark, and takes a club across the shoulder doing it himself. Arrows come, and the knights' rifles answer at range, at legs and at the cart team, never into the shelters. Most of the raiders turn out to be dry-edge men whose own station shares were cut, drinking the water they stole. One man is different: a runner who carries the camp's tally tokens to whoever is buying. He breaks for the dark, and Bren puts a tag round into his shoulder, dye, fennel and sense-stone flour, as he runs. Nobody chases him.

On Tuesday the two young men are found tied and thirsty, but alive, with three carts of casks under the station's seal and four captives, one with a ball in his calf that Imani closes in the cart's shade. Captives and casks go to the court's officer at the station, and Kaldo judges nobody. The water goes back to the seven families, and Wafa watches the clerk's face. Somewhere on the dry edge, a runner is going home with a green stain in his shoulder that a mana sense can follow.

Chapter 69: Noor's Well

On Wednesday 9 September, at Noor's settlement on the west road, outside the court's districts, Maeve meets Noor, a widow who married in from a caravan family and holds her late husband's well share, the only one not held by the headman's kin. Iddo, the headman's son, leads the shaming: it has not rained since she came. He half believes it, because it suits him. The seven families pass through on their way west, and the Children's water carts come out from the temple to meet them as they meet every family walking that road, priests in sand-coloured linen handing out water to anyone, no share asked.

On Thursday a line of cloud passes high over the dry edge in the afternoon without breaking, as it does every season. Emeric lays out his plan in four sentences. His heat will drive an updraft beneath the line, his cold will chill the column's top so the vapour condenses, and Maeve will shape the droplets together until they are heavy enough to fall. Shaping is cheap. The lightning will be the storm's own. Maeve has not changed into a dragon, so Emeric carries her up in a Band suit rig. Inside the column, the back of her neck prickles before each stroke, and she shouts the times down the field set seconds before the lightning comes, without knowing how. Emeric takes it for a good ear. On the ground, Ferra writes down every time Maeve calls. The rain falls first and hardest on Noor's well, in front of everyone. Maeve puts the heaviest shaft there, the only easy part of the afternoon. A curse that waters her field does not hold up.

It costs most of an afternoon and leaves Emeric drained for two days, and it cannot be done on demand or end a drought. For the settlement, two humans changing the weather is terrifying rather than impressive. The children hide from him. When the station clerk hears of it on the road, her throat darkens to the collar. Afterward Emeric sits grey on a bench in the Children's kitchen-house with shaking hands while Iddo watches.

That evening High Mother Hadra comes to the kitchen-house in person. She is Sabir and old, her crest scales gone grey-white, forty years at the Children's head and knowing every orphan's name, a caretaker by nature, much as Amara is. She feeds them before she lets anyone talk: chilled glasspod and crackle-salt flatbread, which Emeric salts at the end as Tahl taught everyone at the Guest Fire. He eats too much, and Mochi eats more and flattens on the warm bench like a pearl pancake. Ferra says, "Fully charged." Hadra sees Mochi on his shoulder and is kinder to him than to anyone else in the room. Every child on the dry edge knows the caravan story that a king slime will not stay with the greedy, and it is part of why she trusts him so quickly. They talk about water law and what the court is. She does not know he is a king, and she gives the K Discovery leave to stand on the temple's caravan ground by day.

Chapter 70: The Man Who Burned the Wells

On Friday morning, 11 September, Noor's settlement's own elders strip Iddo of the well he tried to take. Kaldo punishes nobody. The lasting fix is mundane and local. The settlement's old water-finder, whom the elders trust, shows them the clouds that pass every season, and Maeve shows them dew traps, stone heaps and sheets that catch the night's dew. The yield is small but real. Kaldo holds back its well stone until Ghassan's survey is done. Iddo watches Kaldo leave and says nothing. Wells here are limited by recharge, contamination, legal access, upkeep and who can maintain them after the fleet goes, and Emeric is learning the difference between an aquifer and a commons.

The evidence points at Mukhtar, a dry-edge water broker who buys stolen station shares from bought clerks and sells water by the skin. Three seasons ago, when the court opened emergency wells on the dry edge, he burned them, setting their timber wellheads alight and pouring burning pitch down the shafts so the families had to buy from him. The captives name him. Wafa's cord shows the station's share left on his carts. The tagged runner's dye trail leads to his camp. The casks smell of the same pitch as the last burned well. Ghassan's officer has a warrant. Emeric is flat on a cot, drained by the rain, so it is Ward's call: stop the next attack and capture Mukhtar if practicable.

Mira and two knights follow the dye trail through the noon heat, ahead of Ward and the court's officer, to an emergency station where families have taken shelter from the heat in a stone cistern house, half underground. Mukhtar's men have barred its door and are firing its roof timbers, because the families inside gave the court his name. Mukhtar himself is on the roof with a pitch pot over the air vent. Mira calls to him through the interpreter's single shouted word to put it down. He tips the pot. She fires once. He goes off the roof, and the pot goes with him, outward. Arthur and Bren break the door, and Bren's hands burn on the bar as he pulls people out, coughing, into the heat.

Imani tends the dead man and the burned. Ferra, arriving, takes the families' testimony before anyone can tell them what they saw. Ward owns the order and puts it in writing to the court's officer that afternoon. Mira does not eat that evening. Maeve sits beside her on the guest house step and does not make her talk. Mira is changed by having chosen.

That night Harrold's message arrives by relay. On Thursday at Cinderhale, the League met with no observers present, the mill lords' watch fired, and three people died, among them Bryony Fenwick, who kept the League's minutes. Keller's bailiffs came at once for the shooters, and Bryony's side book holds one watchman's station and weapon number. Still grey from the rain, Emeric reads again the minutes line from 29 August: Cinderhale observers declined; Wayford preferred. Then he works through the tyrant's arithmetic aloud to Imani. The mill lords' paymasters are in Tullio's book. A writ, the Crestless brought home, and it would be done in a month. Then he counts who pays for it: the people inside first, the League, the towns, the next king, Kaldo's own law; and last, the old peoples watching, every people past the wall waiting to see whether a human king reaches for a list. Imani says, "Choose it knowing." He does not choose it. He says he will ask Keller for the road that is not that one.

On Saturday the K Discovery hops the shore party to the temple's caravan ground, with Armand, Bram and Dagny aboard from the city.

Chapter 71: The Children

From Saturday 12 September, the temple of the Banked Sun stands about eighty miles out on the west road: a domed hall over a deep shaft, the Deep Lift, where far below the water glows through itself like something buried and burning, the glow pulsing with the draw. The Children call it the Sun. Around it stand a healing house, an orphanage, kitchens, a school, a caravan shelter and guest house, and the temple wells, the only water on the dry edge nobody needs a share to draw. Orc wardens keep its doors. When a K Discovery deckhand goes down with heatstroke on the caravan ground, the Children's healers have him in shade and cooling before Imani crosses the ground, and they do it well. Imani tells their senior so, which costs her nothing and is true.

At the shaft head works Hila, a young Sabir woman who is the temple's shaft-keeper, self-taught on the Lift's surface machinery, who talks to the brake band. Emeric loves her on sight the way he loves Tess at a bench. She is a sincere believer, not a spy. Beside her is Ulf, the dwarf on the temple's machinery contract, a ledger-keeper's son, grumpy and honest, paid partly in control stones he cannot get anywhere else. He and Bram argue about a gear train for an hour and like each other. On the shaft head's rail, Mochi goes still and pulses. Emeric says nothing.

On Sunday comes the first sermon and its three tenets: a life is a season, a people is the year; every thirst is a debt someone hoards; the Sun must be fed. Emeric nods along to two. "Two out of three. That's better than most governments." Ferra writes something in her ledger and will not show him. Then comes the Giving. A priest who delves lays a hand on a new joiner and asks, "Open to the Sun. Hold nothing back. Do you give yourself to the morning?" Every delver in the party feels it, Emeric most of all: the Light, the Church's consent trick, arrived at here independently. He says nothing and privately starts a list, a laboratory log of everything he read on his other world about what makes a cult: the Giving and the Light, charity that outspends its income, a leader nobody may doubt, pilgrimages nobody returns from, and the third tenet nobody explains. Linnet Shaw runs a betting pool over the field sets from the K Discovery's radio room, and Saban even wires a stake from home. The crew bets on a week or two until the bad part. Emeric bets that it is real. Ferra asks why he would bet on something he knows the signs of. "Somebody has to." She writes that down too.

On Monday Ferra finds that the charity outspends every box, gift and tithe she can count, in a single day. She goes looking for the rest and finds it at night on the caravan ground: the temple's water carts going east under bonded seals to court families' estates in the western canal districts, whose old wells are dropping. That is why court nobles fund a cult that preaches against them, and why a groundwater survey threatens a secret. She tells Emeric and nobody else. That evening a clerk at the station's well turns away a dry-edge widow on the old year's last quotas, and Hadra tells her own story: a girl on the dry edge, a dry year, a court clerk turning her village away from a well. She re-wraps Emeric's hands, burned that afternoon on the shaft head's brake band while helping Hila, scolding him as she does it.

At home in Cinderhale, on the Monday night after the funeral, Corrick wants the League to march on the watch house. Maudie stands in the door and says no, and they do not go. Corbin stays for the League's next meeting, at the back, and does not speak. From that night he works with the League without commanding it.

On Wednesday, Imani treats Uri, an orphan of about eight in the Children's house, cheerful and eager to be a priest, who has a festered thorn in his foot. She lances and mends it, explaining every step first, and he follows her for the rest of the day. She notices his high mana density the way she notices everything, and writes nothing yet. He asks her whether kings are real. That evening Emeric tells Hadra about the Wayford cellar as it was: the rag bales, the eight oak chairs with straps and neck cradles, the racks of charged stones, fifteen people held, Tansy in the eighth chair, people kept as batteries so one man could have stones, and the night he decided there would be no more nobles. Hadra is sick with anger, the first person outside Kaldo to react the way he did. Then, ordinarily, they argue whether lime or ash makes the better mortar. She is crying with anger across the table from him, and neither of them hears what is in the room: she is running a cellar of her own, bigger, cleaner and holier. The reader should.

Chapter 72: The Morning

On Thursday 17 September, a quiet evening, Emeric and Hadra trade likes and dislikes. She hates the smell of the court's ink; he hates being thanked; they both like watching people eat. Amara calls on the relay dial and asks who has been feeding him, because he sounds rested. Nobody, he says. Ferra, beside him: "The cult lady." There is a long pause on the line, and that is all.

That night Hadra offers him a memory as a gift, the way she would share one, and he says yes. It shows a Given child walking up into the morning light, unafraid, a mother's hand on the child's hair as she goes. It is built, and Hadra believes every word of it, and it is aimed, without her knowing how well, at the mother he lost at seven. He checks nothing while he is inside it. Afterward he walks it with the four questions Amara taught him, from her father Jerome's practice. The back of the hall is empty. There is no smell in a desert temple at dawn. The child's hands are blank. Everything is seen from one spot, a little behind the mother, where Hadra would stand. He tells Ferra and Imani and says he hopes there is an innocent reason, a picture for children. Imani does not answer. Ferra writes it down.

Chapter 73: The Well Court

On Friday morning the K Discovery hops the shore party back east. Ghassan, Qamar, Bram and Dagny have had soil pits dug all week on the low fields, with old maps from the engineering office's chest made by Yazan, an engineer retired after a downstream dispute. Nadi shows his low parcel and says nothing about the two breeding animals he had to sell. Emeric recognises the salting. Privately he thinks of ancient Mesopotamia's salted fields; aloud he says he has read of farming countries that salted themselves, and he plans pipe drains for every field. He is wrong. Qamar walks him along the saltbrush belts, deep-rooted shrubs grown in dense mana that drink the shallow water down faster than any pipe, and pipes go only where a belt cannot. He leaves knowing a drain can be grown, and says so. The pilot is drafted: soil maps by depth, measured irrigation, test drains, a lined brine channel past the herders' basin to a salt flat run as evaporation pans, and herders paid for the crossing in a court-guaranteed well share. It needs a joint sitting of the court, the agricultural houses and the canal districts, each with a veto on its own ground. Ghassan asks for it, and the court sets it for after the water year turns.

The court's astronomers fix the first rains for Thursday 1 October, when the water year turns at dawn, so the quota hearing sits in the old year's last fortnight, on Friday 18 September at the great well. Wafa can prove the station's book was altered, her cord against the clerk's ink, but appearing would expose her family to Mukhtar's kin, so Kaldo means to bring her through the crowd inside a ring of knights. The square is full, and word has run ahead that a Kaldo knight killed a Sabir man on the dry edge and that the foreigners are taking sides in the court's business. Mukhtar's cousins push the crowd at the ring, and the safe passage collapses into a fight on the well's steps. Ferra chooses which records to save, Wafa's cord and the station's book, over her own ledger's desert pages, which go under the crowd, and her wrist is wrenched. One baton round goes off at a man swinging a hook at Wafa. When a knight levels his rifle for a volley into the crowd, Imani steps in front of the muzzles and says no: these people have reason to be angry, and the court's guard is already moving. The volley is not fired.

The bench will not accept Wafa as Kaldo's exhibit. She is sworn in the court's own order, after the petitioners already listed. The court's own clerk reads her cord, and the court's own surveyor checks the station's tank marks against her count. Her testimony holds. The station's share is restored to the seven families, and the court, not Kaldo, strikes the station clerk from the roll. But the spectacle costs: two canal districts withdraw their signatures from the joint sitting's request until after the year turns. The court then reviews Mira's shot, hearing Ward's written order, the families' testimony and Ferra's record. The bench splits. Some praise her; others reject any foreign fighter deciding things on their land. It is recorded as lawful defence of the families, by a narrow bench, on one condition: no Kaldo firearm may be carried in the court's districts or on the dry edge for the rest of the stop, except at a court officer's call. Ward accepts it in front of the bench.

The court's caravan masters say bite-madness has killed two caravan hands this summer, since they hire werewolf guides on the desert's edge. Emeric begins to promise them the dried series that saved Runa. Imani stops him at the bench. It was raised in Bettany's animals and given to one half-werewolf child, and nobody has seen it in a Sabir body; the Sabir are not built like anyone Kaldo has dosed. She offers the method instead, and a trial using Sabir volunteers' blood in Bettany's animals, with results before any promise. Emeric takes his words back in front of the bench, and the caravan masters like her better than him for it. That night Ferra rewrites her lost desert pages from memory, figure by figure, while Imani holds her mended wrist still and tells her to stop.

Chapter 74: A Man with a List

On Saturday 19 September, Hadra seats Emeric on the Children's council: water allocations, the orphanages' needs, and a dispute between two dry-edge families over a goat and a cistern. He is good at it and loves it. Ferra lends her ledger system to the Children's registers, and within the week their distribution is fairer. One of the registers her system tidies is the orphanage's, in which the healing house has noted each child's strength. Nobody remarks on it. Across the council table, half joking, Ferra asks what he would do if the High Mother turned out to be a monster. He says he would still get her out of it alive, and means it. Ferra's pen scratches, Imani's tea goes cold, and the court ink Hadra hates scents the allocation sheets. The council's first case is a dry-edge family evicted from a station plot that morning on a technicality, their share written in a dead man's name, under the court's new quotas. The Children take them in. He is angry, good at the work, and happy, and he lets himself be.

On the call day, by field set at the guest house with Linnet patching from the K Discovery, Harrold's flat report includes an exception read out with its clause. On Tuesday at the Isle works, Tess reports in her own words, the shaft foreman found warm rock and a second beat. Tess asked for a pause. Harrold, as regent, signed a limited exception under Emeric's own clause for fuel and defence in an emergency, because the relief pair's run home and the Answer Tower need the Isle's oil, and wrote the date and reason in his own hand. Tess has the beat's rate in her own book. Emeric cannot revise it; it is his own clause, used lawfully. In one line of his report Harrold mentions that the capital's first filtered water ran through the filter beds into the lower city's mains on Wednesday, with Weekes's utility mages on the boost, and that he drank from his hand at the standpipe with the Isle paper in his coat. Corbin reports the bailiffs, Bryony's side book, and a witness named Pim placing the watchman, and he does not say "I told you," which is worse. Emeric asks Keller whether a man who arms against the crown's peace is a rebel. Keller answers: "A rebel, Majesty. Once the realm says so. The realm isn't you. And in the old law, so is the man who knowingly pays him." Ferra minutes it.

That evening, after his first council day, Emeric tells Hadra the tyrant's way out, plainly and a little ashamed: call every noble in, read them all, keep the ones worth keeping. "I could do it in a season." He knows which houses he would call first, and that is the part that frightens him. "And then I'd be a man with a list. Everybody who ever did anything terrible had a list and a good reason." She asks, "And the ones you raised?" "Grow up. Disagree with me. And they'd have watched how I handle disagreement." She does not answer. She makes him sleep.

On Sunday at dawn the table is set for two. Hadra tells him she agrees with every word and that she has never heard anything so true. She is moved. Her own list takes the poor and the willing, and she does not hear it. The reader does. He is grateful, goes out to look at the shaft head with Hila, and does not think about it again that day.

Where the Story Stands After Chapter 74

Kaldo has learned that water in the desert belongs to somebody. It bought a river share in iron for water it had already drunk, was corrected by Ghassan on wells and by Qamar on drains, and saw a court insist on its own procedure rather than accept a foreign rescue as proof. The seven families have their water back, Wafa's testimony stands on the court's own reading, the station clerk is struck, and Mukhtar is dead by Mira's chosen shot, recorded as lawful by a narrow bench. Emeric now sits on the Children's council. Hadra trusts him, has fed him every night, has shown him the Morning, and agrees with his list, without knowing he is a king, which the court knows and has not said. He has checked her gifted memory and found it built, and hopes there is an innocent reason. Ferra knows about the water carts flowing east to court estates. Imani has seen Uri's density and written nothing. Ferra's ledger system now tidies the orphanage's register of each child's strength. The betting pool stands, with everyone except Emeric betting on the bad part. Mochi has pulsed at the shaft head and at Gisela's slab, and Armand's word for "the one who sits" waits unplaced. Ghassan's survey has found dropping wells and sinking ground and no cause yet.

At home, Bryony Fenwick is dead at Cinderhale, Keller's prosecution is under way, Maudie has kept the League from marching, and Nan Coombe's letter from Wayford is on its way. Keller has told Emeric that the realm is not him. Harrold has signed a lawful exception under Emeric's own clause to keep drilling on the Isle despite warm rock and a second beat that Tess wanted to pause for. The relief pair is due on the Sun River between 1 and 10 October, the Festival of the Last Dawn falls on 1 October, and the fleet has five relays and about sixty tonnes of food.

This batch shows Emeric being kind, competent and happy among people who do the same work he does, and it lets the reader hear what he cannot yet: that the woman who agrees with every word he says about men with lists has a list of her own.
