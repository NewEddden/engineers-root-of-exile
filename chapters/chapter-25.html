Book 3, Batch 6: The Courts

Chapters 48–57 | Friday 9 June – Thursday 31 August, Year 19

The March Emergency Authorisation lapsed at midnight on 8 June with no armed body in the field. Imani is home from Pryor's keep. Under it the king was censured once, for the gravity press at the gate, and found lawful once, on the Black Moss, where five hundred men who stayed died in fifteen minutes; abroad he is now the gold-eyed demon king of Kaldo. The Rule on Royal Force, written the night of the gate inquiry, lapsed with the Authorisation. The Isle's hollow is sealed under two keys. The summer belongs to the courts: Pryor, Sayer, Tullio, Treloar, Wystan, Cuthbert, Sir Edwin Hartley's killer, Davey Corve's father, and the Hearth.

This batch is the reckoning by law, one trial after another, with the king mostly in the gallery or absent. The Rule on Royal Force becomes standing law that binds every king after him, and Keller finally rules on the question he reserved eighteen months ago. Maudie wins her case with the tools Corrick once called too slow. The Hearth is tried and the prosecution loses its conspiracy count, but not its case, and its sentences run longer than they sound because the old law gives no credit for remand.

The verdicts are not tidy. A jury splits nine to three, an acquittal lands on the man most people think knew, and three people hang in a gaol yard at dawn. The king refuses forty League members a pardon for the man he admires, in public, because it would be the very favour the League exists to end. In between, a machine runs its first stored program and a jet engine runs for four minutes and stops every hammer in the yard.

Chapter 48: The Report

On Monday 12 June, through Ferra's eyes, Quarrie's full report on the gate is printed whole in Kaldo News: every witness's evidence, including Ward, the tower men, Hal Belk, Emeric, the stable boy, and Runa's nose with Quarrie's note that it was evidence and not a verdict; the three findings as given on 25 April; and one recommendation the April finding did not make, that the Rule on Royal Force outlive the Authorisation that made it, because the next authorisation will have a king in it too. The Moss's report is printed beside it: lawful, no censure, lawful and clean are not the same word. The report also answers the public's question of why the citizen's bands did not find Imani: the first bands know no position, and the inquiry recommends against a position register for them, so the Sand Room's dots stay dots, two keys are needed for a name, and nothing keeps a history. Emeric wanted the register and says so to Ferra that night. He does not get it.

On Thursday 15 June Quarrie grants Davey Corve his case-by-case pardon and publishes it: he opened the corridor and moved the walking sick and the eleven conscripts while the hall party took the powder. He is a free man and a crown witness. On Monday 19 June Hal Belk and the other tower men are paid from the crown's purse at the gate's town office. Hal asks the clerk whether the king will come. He does not. He sends a letter, which Hal keeps and does not open for a week.

On Wednesday 21 June, through Corbin's eyes, the crews' man moves the Rule in the Assembly, and it becomes standing law. Any authorisation the Assembly ever gives carries it automatically: the king's own force, by person or by power, moves only after the proclamation is read at the place, the field commander countersigns in writing before he leaves the ground, and a written order is filed with the authorisation's reviewing officer. Since the strike there is no royal emergency force outside an authorisation at all, so the Rule covers every case there is. Corbin says for the crown that the crown does not oppose it. That night the king tells Ferra once that it will cost some future king a day he does not have, and it might be him. Ferra says it might be someone worse than him, which is the point. He signs it in the morning.

The same week, in his working room, Keller rules on the question he reserved in January of the year before: a court of the crown can review the crown's own force from now on, and the Rule says how. The Church hulls of 4 January came before any rule reached the crown's act in its own sky, so the king's written account of the twenty-eight stands on the record without a finding. Emeric asks him to make one anyway. Keller refuses: the court does not judge by a law it did not have. Emeric says that is not the same as being cleared. Keller agrees, and writes that down too.

Chapter 49: Hers

From Monday 19 to Friday 23 June, at the circuit in Cinderhale's courthouse, its hearth scrubbed white, through Maudie's eyes, the dues case is hers from first to last, and the page does not cut away to Corrick in custody. The assessment register establishes the lawful dues, the demand slips show what Pelham ordered collected, and the receipts show the prior payments. Opposing counsel attacks the destruction of the collection rolls. Maudie answers with the separate trail of records, and does not pretend the burning helped her case. The court strikes Pelham's double charges and upholds the genuine assessments. Its judgment explicitly refuses to endorse the burning of the rolls. The valid dues are payable in notes at par. Maudie has won with the tools Corrick once dismissed as too slow. It is her judgment, not his vindication.

On Saturday 24 June, the midsummer quarter day, the widow Tench pays her valid dues in notes at par at the court's office, comes home, and puts the receipt in the tin beside the old demand slip.

Chapter 50: Grievance Isn't a Conspiracy

The Hearth is tried at the circuit's court at Kestle, beside the gaol, with a jury drawn by lot from another district's card register. Keller steps aside, saying he sat at the table that decided not to pay them and will not sit at the bench that decides what they owe. Judge Agnes Fairlie of the southern circuit presides, with two other circuit judges to sit for sentence. From Monday 26 June, through Maudie's eyes, the prosecution makes the best case it has against the League: Corrick's past League leadership, identical grievances and notices, Kezia's use of the League's publicly collected dues evidence, and the timing of the fire. Maudie admits each link. The prosecutor says the League and the Hearth shared their enemies. Yes. Their dates and their demands. Often. And she expects them to believe she knew nothing? She expects them to prove she knew a plan; grievance is not a conspiracy. The separate meeting place, the sworn witnesses, the public minutes and the absence of any knowing agreement defeat the conspiracy count against the League's officers, though the judge does not declare open accounts an automatic defence.

Through Kezia's eyes, the covenant is tested and not blessed. The night man testifies that the masked request was frightening, and the crown produces the wages and roster showing his employment continued. The court examines the Dunholt carrier contracts, the property inventory and the forfeiture of the guns. The finding is narrow: no innocent loss in the defined categories, and no innocent injury, which is not a judgment that the Hearth had any authority to choose its targets. The prosecutor asks Corrick whether he would accept another group destroying the League's hall with equal discipline, and he has no answer that exempts his own group without exempting theirs. On Wednesday 5 July the court records five participants who never came forward. The two ex-watchmen have pleaded guilty before testifying, and seven others have submitted and been sentenced or fined. Corrick will not name the five; he does not lie, and as an accused he cannot be made to give evidence against others. Afterward Kezia reminds him he wrote that they would answer for the act, and five of them have not. He says he will not send them to a house with a lock. She says he decided that for them too.

That week, on the capital bench, Sir Edwin Hartley's killer is tried. Through Corporal Piet Neate's eyes in the gallery, the shot man testifies, and so does the hall's orderly: murder, unanimous, and death, three judges to none. Neate does not feel what he expected to. The appeal is automatic.

On Thursday 6 July the crown calls Ward on the field command's log of Corrick's call from Cinderhale's post office. Afterward, in the corridor, with Corrick between two bailiffs, Ward tells him that a day sooner with that statement and he would have had a detachment on the March road, Garside on the box, the guns in the crates, and a duke in a dock with a witness. Corrick says it was three days to the Gate, Ward knows who ended up behind its walls, and there was no warrant until they broke in to get him one. Neither is proven right.

Chapter 51: A Year

From Monday 10 to Friday 14 July, on the capital bench, through Davey's eyes, he testifies against his father: the stair, the shot, Matt Lowrie. His father is convicted of Matt's murder and gets twenty-five years, not death, the bench's reasons being the match handed to his son unbidden and the fourteen saved. In the cells after sentence his father asks whether his mother is eating. Davey says she is. Nothing else is said, and it is the last time they are in a room together. His father goes to Stillwater's therapeutic community, not its review wing. Imani offers Davey a place in the hospital's training ward. He says no, takes his pay, and goes home to the March.

On Friday 14 July, at the court at Kestle, through Kezia's eyes, the Hearth's defendants have admitted the entries, the destruction and the unlawful taking, offering necessity only in mitigation. Treason is rejected, because nobody made war or knowingly aided a rising, and the judge distinguishes their conduct from Pryor's. The prosecutor asks for three years. The majority gives Corrick and Kezia a year each and Gethin six months, citing their pleas, Corrick's and Gethin's surrender, Kezia's going back for the waybill book that cleared the carters, the evidence preserved, no innocent injury, and the weapons shipment stopped. The dissent, read aloud, would have given more, and cites the five by name. Kezia's map of the Stannary workings is not among the reasons, as she asked. She looks at Ward in the gallery, he looks back, and that is all. The old law gives no credit for remand, so Corrick and Kezia will be out on 14 July next year and Gethin on 14 January.

That evening, on the League's wall in Cinderhale, Maudie pins up the nominal terms and the real ones side by side: fifteen and a half months for Corrick and Kezia, counting from 30 March, and nine and a half for Gethin. A year can lawfully mean fifteen or sixteen months behind a lock. Her complaint is public and looks forward, and lawfulness does not answer every question about proportion. A third paper goes up on the League's wall, two columns of months. On Saturday the three go to Turnstone, the open prison on its own rail spur.

Chapter 52: Neither

On Tuesday 18 and Wednesday 19 July, in the hospital board's room in Kaldo, through Sister Inga's eyes and then Imani's, the morbidity review on Cal Pascoe sits, with the board's surgeons, Inga, and a March physician the board invited because Cal's people were the March's. The facts: the relic accepted on a weighed, sealed and sensed case; Ward's written protest; the tie placed blind; the only surgeon who could see it taken; and Cal dead of a slipping branch nobody else could find by ordinary means. The finding neither absolves nor condemns her entirely: a defensible risk decision, with a death in it. Imani says the one thing she can say. She will never call trying to save Cal the wrong impulse, and she will never call the risk costless. The board writes both down. One practice changes, Inga's idea: no single surgeon is ever the only one who can see a temporary tie, and every blind tie gets a second surgeon's ordinary mark, a stitch of coloured thread at the branch, the moment it is placed.

On Monday 24 July, in the March's interim reeve's hall at the foot of the pass road, through Mother Brannagh's eyes, the Medical Pass Office sits for the first time, with five seats: Imani, Ward, Brannagh for the March, a Saint Ilda's-trained healer the March's families name, and the health committee's clerk. It settles on searches made by both sides together: any relic or blessed object carried to a patient is searched by both sides' searchers with the bearer present. And it sets penalties both sides pay: a crown escort who bars a blessed visitor without a search, or a bearer who carries an undeclared stone, answers to the circuit. Brannagh says Cuthbert betrayed the blessing, not the other way round, and will not hear it said otherwise. Ward and Imani each lose a point, and sign the same paper.

Chapter 53: Never Your Years

From Monday 17 to Friday 28 July, on the capital bench, through Agatha Vell's eyes in the gallery and then Pryor's, Pryor's Charter brief is read whole at his own request: three hundred years of a road kept open and widows paid, and a Kaldo majority that could undo it in a sitting. It is not stupid, and the page lets it stand. The prosecution's case is the keep, the queen, the powder and the transfer moved up. Treason, unanimous. Death, three judges to none. The judgment concedes in a paragraph that the Assembly is unequal, and says that is a reason to change the Assembly, not a defence for holding a queen within reach of powder. Agatha testifies to nothing and sits through it. When he is sentenced she thinks of the pension clerk who sent a cart of grain on a word, and that she will be voting for the four seats against everything he stood for, and she does both.

From Monday 24 July Sayer is tried. On Wednesday 26 July her courier produces the signed original of her standing order from his own strongbox. The defence cross-examines him as a man shifting blame, which he is, and the notary's register and the date carry the proof, with the original only matching them. Ruth Penhale's son testifies to the eighty yards. Through Ruth's eyes, on Friday 4 August, the murder count fails: the shots came before the chain, the order said the chain, and the jury cannot find she ordered those particular killings. Treason is proven: she intentionally directed armed resistance to a lawful entry. Death, two judges to one; the published dissent would have given her the realm's longest term, because the four deaths were not hers in law. At sentence Ruth Penhale speaks against the hanging: Sayer was fair to her once, and the dead do not need another body. The bench hears her, and sentences Sayer anyway.

Chapter 54: No Royal Favour

On Monday 7 August, by Pim Barrow's hand, forty League members petition the king for clemency for Corrick, Kezia and Gethin. On Wednesday 9 August he answers them in the League's hall in Cinderhale, by his own choice rather than at the castle, through Pim's eyes. No. Pim says he could. He could, the king says, and he would like to, and that is the reason not to: the next man the court puts away will not have forty names and a king who likes him, so if there is mercy for Corrick it has to be mercy for anyone in his place, by law, not by him. And Corrick would not take it; Kezia would hand it back. The forty understand that a pardon would turn Corrick's case into the king's gift. Nobody argues the last point. That week workers spit at the inspectorate door, and the broadsheets say he admires Corrick in private and lets him rot in public.

On Wednesday 9 August, in the Motion Shop's machine room beside Tess's clean room, through Isa Brann's eyes, the bridge machine runs: valve racks, a core plane of ferrite rings threaded by the women of Abloh's thread room, and Ottoline's card reader. Merrin Sallis loads the first stored program, thirty-odd words that add, test and loop, held in the machine's own rings, and it runs. Then it does its first useful job: the Issue Board's weekly redemption round, every county office's Thursday turned into a weekly roster with coin carriage between them, which a room of Ferra's clerks had said would take a month. It is done before lunch. Merrin, Isa, Ottoline, Tess, Wren and Ferra with her ledger are there, and Dov, who signed the fourth transistor board yesterday and not the fifth, and is right. Emeric stands at the back and says nothing, because he knows a dozen better ways to write it and will not. Amara holds the first punched card longest. After lunch the machine runs its second routine, the Board's serial log against the week's returns, and prints two duplicate serials: the Halverne sign-painter's last two forged notes, still going round five months on. It is the first time a machine finds a forged note in Randillia, and it takes a page.

That week, through Keller's ruling on the Church inquiry, Cuthbert gets fifteen years. The inquiry closes on Friday 11 August with nothing proven on the evidence available. The Sealed Confidences Article bars one source, Cuthbert's confessor at Saint Ilda's; the stones' provenance, the Gate's receipts, the stone count, his travel and the letters are all examined and cut both ways, Ostrel's reprimand to Saint Ilda's among them. Keller writes that later evidence could change it. Imani reads the ruling and says the Article did what she argued it should, and that she hates it.

On Saturday 12 August, in Turnstone's visitors' garden, through Corrick's eyes, the king visits. The law was failing Cinderhale, Emeric says. And now Corrick is in a house with a lock. The court put him there, and Emeric cannot undo it because he admires him. Then Emeric tells him he might not see it yet, but he is in the funnel; he will see it from the other end; it is not a long sentence, and when he is out the real work starts again. Corrick asks what funnel, and Emeric does not explain. Corrick says Maudie counted the months, and the king's law calls this one year. Emeric says he knows. "Then don't call knowing enough." Corrick is irritated about the funnel all the way back to his workshop.

Chapter 55: Nine to Three

From Monday 31 July, on the capital bench, through Vantic's eyes from the public bench behind her brother, Tullio is tried on two counts, stated to the jury before it deliberates. The first, not capital, is proved by the receipts, every tranche to the March from 11 March with its duplicate. The second, capital, turns on whether he meant to further a rising, and on one sentence: release the next tranche only while the guest remains at the Gate. Both sides read it, the prosecution as keeping a hostage in Pryor's hands, the defence as keeping the queen in the kingdom where she could be got back, and the defence's reading is made real by the dawn move, when Pryor tried to move her toward Ostrel against Tullio's condition.

The jury retires on Wednesday 16 August. On the first count, guilty. On the capital count, the first ballot is nine guilty and three unconvinced. A further review of the evidence brings no agreement, and on Friday 18 August the judge declares a mistrial on the capital count. On Monday 21 August the independent prosecutor formally declines to retry it, with no further admissible evidence of intent and thirty years already secured. Tullio gets thirty years on the proved count, and the crown book is seized. Emeric cannot order a retrial and does not try, and Kaldo News prints the prosecutor's letter whole.

On Wednesday 23 August, in the gaol's visiting room, Vantic understands what she felt on the bench when it was enough for thirty years and not for the rope: relief, first, and she cannot say so. He blames Pryor. He still thinks he was protecting the kingdom's money. He is alive to hate her. She walks out of the visiting room with her coat buttoned wrong and does not notice until the street.

Chapter 56: The Engine

From Monday 7 to Friday 18 August, at the circuit at Kestle, through Kezia's eyes as the evening sheet reaches her at Turnstone, Treloar is tried. A Grayrock engineer gives the finding. Treloar is acquitted of the plot. Kezia's signed timber warning is admitted, and though the defence attacks her as a convicted saboteur, its date and measurements are weighed on their own merits. Treloar's geologist argues for an old fault. The ignored closure order of 5 April is found a substantial cause of the nine deaths. Twenty years. Kezia hears it from the evening sheet at Turnstone and goes back to her bench.

On Saturday 19 August, in the Sky Yard's long shed, through Sorrel's eyes, the Mark 1's engine sits chained to its bench and a ground starter cart spins it up, mundane first, with no air stone. Bram is on the fuel and Tess on the jet pipe temperature gauge, the one that matters. It lights, spools for a long time, and runs for about four minutes at a fraction of its thrust before Tess calls the temperature and Bram shuts it down. Every hammer in the yard stops. Sorrel stands beside it with her hands over her ears and will not leave. It is the opposite of the silent gull she wanted, and she is going to fly it. Through the long shed's open doors while it runs she can see the runway going down west across the rise: two kilometres of concrete poured since June, lined up so every approach comes in over the farms and the Mere and never over the city, the lamp posts going in along its edge, the tower's brick at half height. She walks a finished stretch at dusk and will not let anyone call it done.

From Monday 14 to Friday 25 August, through the eyes of a juror who dislikes the result, Wystan is tried: the disguised freight, the Gate's ball, the production outside the state contract, and the dealers' route against the defence's delegated technical approvals and forms signed by Fulk. The prosecutor argues an attentive beneficiary ought to have known, but the charge requires proved knowledge. Acquitted. The juror dislikes it and respects the standard. Wystan is free to stand for a seat.

From Saturday 26 August, by post between Turnstone and Cinderhale, Corrick's muttered line that Fulk was a coward reaches Mabel in a letter about the League's flour. She writes back one line, the first since April: neither of them knows whether her brother ran from guilt or from danger. He apologises by return, for the accusation, not for doubting Wystan. Mabel keeps the letter from a man she is not speaking to.

Chapter 57: The Hanging Morning

On Monday 28 August the full bench dismisses the appeals of Pryor, Sayer and Sir Edwin's killer. On Tuesday, in the Assembly's committee room, where she has asked to be heard, through Imani's eyes, she argues that a man in custody can no more escape than her patients could, so the rope protects nobody. Sir Edwin Hartley's sister answers her: her brother was unarmed, in a hall with no stones in it, and the man who stabbed him chose to. Neither wins. The committee records both and changes nothing, because the sentences are the courts'.

The hanging is to be in the yard of Kestle gaol, before the gaol's governor, a magistrate, the condemned's chosen witnesses and the press, not at Pryor's Gate, where a gallows would become a shrine for the brief his followers are already printing. At dawn on Thursday 31 August Pryor, Sayer and Sir Edwin's killer are hanged. Imani spends the morning at the Pass Office at the foot of the pass road, signing a March midwife's passes with Brannagh across the table. She signs at the hour the trap falls at Kestle, and neither she nor Brannagh mentions the time. That afternoon Pryor's followers print his brief again, and keep it.

Where the Story Stands After Chapter 57

The Rule on Royal Force is standing law from 23 June: any authorisation the Assembly ever gives carries it, so no king's own force moves without a proclamation read at the place, a field commander's signature and an order on file. Keller has ruled that courts can review the crown's force from now on, and has refused to make a finding on the twenty-eight from before any rule existed, which the king knows is not the same as being cleared. No position register for the bands was built. Davey is pardoned and home in the March. Hal Belk and the tower men have been paid.

The courts have spoken. Maudie won the dues case without endorsing the fire, and Tench has her receipt. The Hearth lost its conspiracy count but not its case: Corrick and Kezia are at Turnstone until July next year and Gethin until January, longer than their sentences sound, and five members are still unaccounted for. Davey's father got twenty-five years, Treloar twenty, Cuthbert fifteen, and Tullio thirty after a nine-to-three mistrial on the capital count. Wystan was acquitted for want of proved knowledge. Pryor, Sayer and Sir Edwin's killer were hanged at Kestle on 31 August. The Church inquiry closed with nothing proven. The king refused the League's petition for mercy in public and told Corrick he is in a funnel he cannot yet see, and Corrick told him not to call knowing enough.

Imani's review neither absolved nor condemned her, and the Medical Pass Office now makes both sides search together and pay for their failures. The bridge machine has run its first stored program and caught its first forged notes, and the Mark 1's engine has run for four minutes beside a runway going down across the rise. The young have not grown past the first stage, though one has reached about two point two metres with vane-buds, and the beast line lapses on 13 October unless the Assembly renews it. The count stands at day 342 with five sales, and the switch holds. The Titles Act's first vote comes in the autumn.

This batch shows the law doing its work, which is not the same as justice being done. Every verdict here was reached properly. Some of them hurt the people who wanted them, some were softer or harder than the facts deserved, and the king's admiration counted for nothing in any of them, which is the point.
