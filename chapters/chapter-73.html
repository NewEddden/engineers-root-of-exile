Chapter 73 - Iron for Clay

Bram heard the number before Emeric reached the part about the pipe, and set down the pattern he was scribing, and did the thing he did when a problem was large enough to respect, which was go quiet and let the size of it arrive fully before he opened his mouth.

"Four thousand," he said. "In a town I've been feeding water sized for eighteen hundred."

"Four thousand two hundred. And climbing. The intake's already running thin. I felt it before Ferra put a number to it, and I read it as a fault in the pipe, because I would rather it have been a fault in the pipe than a fault in me." Emeric said it flatly, because Bram had no use for a lord who dressed an error, and because saying the true thing plainly was the only apology the man respected. "The clay network held. It held perfectly. It's carrying exactly what I built it to carry to more than twice the mouths I built it for, and an honest system doing its honest job at the wrong scale is how you bury a town without ever seeing the mistake that did it."

He had come to Bram and not to a mason because the answer was not more clay. The clay network had been the right thing in the plague years, cheap and fast and mundane and buildable by hands that had never laid a pipe, and it had done its work, and it had reached the end of its work. Clay cracked at the joints under pressure a growing town's draw put on it, and clay could not be run at the pressure a separated supply-and-waste system needed if the clean line was going to stay clean against the waste line the whole length of the town, and clay above all could not be made in the volume four thousand mouths and their middens required in the time the burial ground gave him, which Imani had told him that morning was not much time at all.

That was the other reason he had let Bram sit with the number before speaking. Imani had gotten to him first, with the burial-ground survey she had run without being asked because a physician who had read the Sanitation Protocol and then watched a town double did not wait to be asked, and she had laid it out with the particular calm she used for the things that frightened her most. The ground he had dragged the dead away from in the plague years was creeping back toward the water table under the weight of the new dead, the ordinary dead of a bigger town, and the new upstream houses of the rapids district sat above intakes that had been the top of the town when he cut them and were now the middle of it, and the waste of forty-two hundred people was going into a drainage plan drawn for eighteen hundred and the difference was not a smell, the difference was a return address for the exact thing he had spent five years teaching this town it need never suffer again.

"So it's iron," Bram said. It was not a question either. He had built the foundry that made the answer possible and he knew what the answer was before it was spoken, the way a man who forges a key knows every door it fits. "You want to pull clay and lay iron. The whole network. Supply and waste both, run separate the length of the town, sized for the count you actually have with the margin the fire taught you. And you want it before the ground gives Imani the thing she's afraid of."

"There's iron past the debts now." Emeric had turned that fact over a hundred times since the road opened and it still had the taste of a thing that should not be true, a barony with metal to spare, when the whole early plan had been built around never having any. The foundry surplus that had freed the district, the plate that had built a ship and would arm a guard, was also, quietly, the first time in the barony's life there was enough of the base material of civilization to lay it under the streets instead of only into weapons and walls. "For the first time I can build the civic layer to match the production layer. I've been making iron faster than I've been making anything for the people the iron drew here. That stops. The pipe comes first, before another furnace, before anything with an edge on it, because a boom town that can arm a hundred men and can't water four thousand is a town that solved the wrong problem and I am done solving the wrong problem in the right order."

They built the plan across three evenings, Bram and Ferra and Imani and himself, and it built cleanly because the hard thinking had all been done years ago and only needed rescaling, which was its own quiet lesson, that a thing built teachable and mundane the first time could be grown without being reinvented. The clean supply would run in iron from the high intakes, resited above the new upstream houses now that the town had grown a new top, down through the districts under pressure the clay had never held, to standpipes close enough to every quarter that no household carried water far enough to be tempted back to the river. The waste would run in its own iron line, wholly separate, wholly below, pitched to carry itself out past the resited burial ground to a discharge downstream of everything living, and Imani drew the separation herself and made him promise the two lines would never share a trench, because a physician who understood why deaths clustered by well understood better than anyone what a cross-connection was worth in coffins.

And it would run, all of it, on the weight of the river and nothing else. That was the line he would not cross, and he crossed it in his head a dozen times a night before refusing it each time, because there was an easier answer sitting right there and its ease was exactly the danger. He could have set a water stone at the intakes large enough to drive the whole network at pressure in every season, dry or wet, and been done, and the town would have had water that never faltered, and the town would have had water that never faltered because a single crystal in a single vault said so, and the day the crystal failed or was taken or ran dry the town would discover it had forgotten how to be watered any other way. He had watched, from the far side of a life he no longer fully believed he had lived, what happened to systems that grew a single point everyone depended on and no one guarded because it had never once failed. He would not lay one under his own streets.

"Gravity feeds it," he said, and made himself say the rest of it to the room so it would be a thing they all held and not a thing that lived only in his caution. "The grade does the work. The river's weight does the work. Every foot of this network runs on the same physics that runs a stream downhill, and a man who understands a stream can maintain it, and a town that loses every mage tomorrow still drinks. The mundane system is the system. That's not the cheap version of the plan. That's the plan."

Imani asked the obvious question, because she was the one who would answer to the town if the plan had a season in it that killed people, and the season she meant was the dry one. Gravity fed on the river's weight, and the river's weight in the deep dry of a bad summer was less weight, and a network sized to run on the flow could run short exactly when a hot dry town needed water most and needed it cleanest, when every low pool went foul and every tempted household eyed the nearest standing water. That was the real failure surface of the whole design, and he had known it was coming and had wanted her to be the one to name it, because a cost a man names for himself he is tempted to talk his way around, and a cost the physician names for him he has to actually answer.

"That's where the stone goes," he said. "Not driving the network. Lifting the floor under it. A small water stone at the intakes, sized for the dry-season shortfall and nothing more, that adds to the flow when the flow drops and does nothing at all the three seasons the river carries the town on its own. It never becomes the baseline. It can't, because I'm sizing it too small to be the baseline on purpose, so that if it fails in a dry summer the town has less water and not no water, and less water we can ration and haul and survive, and no water we cannot. The magic raises the floor in the worst season. The floor holds the town every other season without it. That's the rule for this and it's the rule for all of it. Supplement a thing that lives without you. Never build the thing that dies without the supplement."

Imani looked at the small figure he had written for the stone, the deliberately insufficient figure, and understood it, and he watched her understand it, the recognition of a man who had built a bigger margin than he needed everywhere the failure was mundane and a smaller one than he could have where the failure would be magical, on purpose, so the shape of the whole system taught its own lesson to anyone who ever had to maintain it. She signed off on the dry-season number. Bram signed off on the iron. Ferra had already sized the cost against the surplus and found it fit with room, which was the first time in the barony's history that the phrase found it fit with room had ever been true of a project this size, and she said so with the flat satisfaction of a woman who had spent years making numbers not fit.

The building of it was the part that would hurt, and he made himself hold that too, because the census had cured him for the moment of the habit of skipping to the finished thing. He was going to tear up a town that worked to lay a town that would work better, and a town does not stop drinking while its water is torn up. They would trench it district by district, the clay staying live in every quarter until the iron beside it was ready to take the load, the disruption rolling through the streets like a slow wound that healed behind itself, mud and open trench and hauled water and complaint, for a season or more, in a town that had just gotten used to comfort and would not thank him for taking it away to give it back improved. That was the cost that had no magical answer and no clever answer, the plain cost of rebuilding the thing you stand on while you stand on it, and he would pay it in exactly the currency the town would resent, which was patience, theirs, spent on his promise.

He thought, laying the first resited intake with his own hands above the new upstream houses because he wanted the town to see the lord in the trench and not only the lord in the hall, that this was the shape of the whole arc he had not yet lived, and did not yet have a name for. Every good thing drew people. The people broke the good thing by being more than it was built for. Fixing it built a bigger good thing that drew more people, and somewhere out past the edge of the plan the same number that let him lay iron under his streets was being read in halls he had never entered as the measure of a subject who had grown too capable to leave in peace. He could not see those halls from the bottom of a trench. He could only see the pipe, and the pipe he could get right. So he got the pipe right, iron for clay, sized for the living and not the dead, run on the weight of a river that would carry the town long after every crystal in it went dark, and he let the halls wait, because the water would not.
