Book 2, Chapter 34: The Unopened Letter

On Wednesday the tenth of June, at noon, with the tank yard still burning behind the port and the lower ward's people still coming back across the bridge with their bedding on their backs, a deputation of the burned streets came up to the castle and asked Corbin to round up the old faith.

There were nine of them. He counted, because counting was a thing to do with his eyes while he listened. A cooper, two women from the rope walk, a carter with his arm in a sling, a man from the fish market who did the talking and four others who stood behind him and nodded. They'd come up the Gate Avenue together in their working clothes with the smoke still on them, and the steward had brought them to the long room because Harrold was at the dome and somebody had to sit in the chair, and Corbin was in it.

He'd slept two hours. His back was still cold. He sat at the head of the long table with his hands flat on it, where Harrold sat, and listened to the fish market man tell him what everybody in the rope walks and the net lofts and the whole of the port knew: that somebody had known which tanks were full. That the Church's people had come out of the sky and gone straight for the full ones and nothing else. That somebody in this city had told them. And that everybody in the port knew who still went to the two chapels by the old customs house, the ones that took their sermons from the cathedral at the capital and not from Doran, and prayed for the Church's success at every Sunday office, out loud, with the windows open.

"Round them up," said the fish market man. "That's all. Hold them till it's sorted. Nobody's asking for more than that."

"How many?"

The man looked at the others. "Sixty. Eighty. With the families."

Corbin looked at the table.

He knew what it looked like from inside. That was the thing he couldn't say to nine people with the smoke on them. He knew exactly what it was to be a man that the Church had found a use for, and to be used, and not to know it; he'd been that man for most of his life, in a little room in the cathedral with a priest's hand on his shoulder, and he'd done what he'd done because of it. He knew what the Church wanted. It wanted a city that had a king who'd gone away to the cursed sea to start arresting its own faithful in the street the morning after the Church had burned its oil, so that every pulpit in the kingdom could say so on Sunday. He knew it the way he knew his own back.

"No," he said.

The fish market man opened his mouth.

"I'll find who told them," said Corbin. "Whoever it is. If he goes to those chapels, I'll find him there, and if he doesn't, I'll find him wherever he does go. I'll not hold sixty people because they pray wrong." He looked up from the table. "That's the Church's work. If I do it for them, they've won twice in one week."

The cooper said something under his breath. One of the women from the rope walk put her hand on his arm, and he stopped.

They went. They weren't happy. He hadn't expected them to be. He sat at the head of the long table in the empty room after the door shut and looked at his hands on the wood, and found they weren't quite steady, and kept them there until they were.



He had one lead by the evening. It came from the tank yard's gatekeeper, who'd been on the gate since before Corbin was born and had lost his eyebrows in the night.

The day before the raid, the Tuesday, about the middle of the afternoon, a man had come to the yard's gate with a works pass and asked for the foreman. A thin man, in a clerk's black coat, with ink on his fingers and his hat in his hands. The gatekeeper knew him by sight. Prewitt, his name was. A tally clerk at the timber wharf. Went to the chapel by the old customs house every Sunday with his wife; the gatekeeper had seen them going. And the gatekeeper had looked at his works pass and not liked it, and not known why, and sent him away; and the man had stood at the gate a while, as if he were going to argue, and then hadn't, and gone.

"Didn't look right," said the gatekeeper. "The pass. Couldn't tell you what. Didn't look like the others."

Corbin sent Osric's men for Prewitt at first light on the Thursday.

The house was in a row of narrow brick houses behind the timber wharf, the fourth one, a narrow brick house with a green door and a front window boarded with planks; and the door was open, and Prewitt's wife was in the front room with a broom, sweeping up glass. She was a small grey woman in an apron. She looked at the knights in the doorway and kept sweeping.

He wasn't there. She said so. She said he'd gone out at five to the wharf, as he always did, and then she said he hadn't, and then she sat down on a kitchen chair in the middle of the glass with the broom across her knees and went quiet.

They found him at the relief kitchen in the lower ward at eight.



Corbin was there. He hadn't meant to be; he'd meant to stay in the chair and let Osric do it. But he'd sat in the long room for an hour after the knights went out with his hands flat on the table and found he couldn't, and gone down the Gate Avenue to the lower ward on foot, and he was standing at the corner by the lower ward's pump with two of the home knights when Osric's runner found them.

The relief kitchen was in the old sail loft by the river steps, where the lower ward's people who'd been flooded out were being fed. There was a line out of its door and down the street, a long one, two hundred people and more with bowls and children, the whole of the bottom of the ward that had come back across the bridge to find its houses full of river mud. And Prewitt was in it. Halfway down, with a bowl in his hands, in his black clerk's coat with his hat pulled down.

He saw Osric's men before they saw him. Corbin watched him see them: a little thin man in a line, looking up from his bowl at three knights in blue coming along the street, and his face going white, and then the bowl going down on the cobbles.

He ran into the line.

Not away from it. Into it. Corbin understood it a second later and thought it was the cleverest thing he'd seen a frightened man do: he went into the middle of two hundred people with bowls and children, ducking, shoving, a hat going down among the heads, and the whole line broke round him and moved, people shouting, a child crying, a woman with a pot on her hip turning round to see what it was; and in the middle of them there was nothing to see but backs and a black coat now and then.

Osric had his rifle up.

Corbin saw it from the corner. Osric at the end of the line, thirty yards off, with the self-loader at his shoulder and his cheek on the stock, tracking; and the black coat going through the crowd ahead of him, in and out of the backs, toward the river end of the street where the old warehouse stairs went down to the steps.

Osric could make the shot. Corbin had seen him on the range. A black coat between two shoulders at thirty yards was a shot Osric could make with his eyes shut, and the man would go down and it would be over.

Osric lowered the rifle.

He did it slowly. He took his cheek off the stock and brought the muzzle down until it pointed at the cobbles, and said something sharp to the two knights beside him that Corbin couldn't hear, and the two knights went into the crowd on foot after the coat, and Osric went after them, with the rifle across his chest and his hand off the trigger. He hadn't looked round at Corbin. He hadn't needed to ask. There'd been a woman with a pot and a child behind the coat, and beyond them two hundred more, and a round that went through a clerk's coat at thirty yards went on going.

Corbin went down the warehouse lane instead.

He didn't know why. He knew the lower ward; he'd walked every street of it with the stewards' reports in the spring, every alley and stair, because Harrold had told him that a man who read a ward's numbers should know what its numbers walked on. And the warehouse lane ran down behind the sail loft to the same steps the old warehouse stairs came out on, by its own back stair, and a man who knew the ward and was running for the river would come down one or the other.

He came down the back stair.

Corbin was at the bottom of it. Prewitt came round the last turn at a run with his hat gone and his coat flapping and saw him there, a tall man in a grey coat standing on the bottom step with his hands at his sides, and stopped so hard he sat down on the stair.

They looked at each other.

"Mr Prewitt," said Corbin.

Prewitt didn't try to get up. He sat on the stair breathing as if his chest would come apart, and looked at Corbin's face, and Corbin saw him know who it was.

"I didn't," said Prewitt. "I didn't tell them. I didn't, I swear on..."

"I haven't asked you anything yet," said Corbin.



Tess came across from the Works at noon because Corbin had sent for her, and stood at the end of the long table in her work coat with the pass between two fingers and turned it to the window; and Corbin, watching her, saw her know it was forged in about the time it took her to pick it up.

"Not mine," she said.

"How do you know?"

"Because I make them." She put it on the table and laid her own pass beside it, out of her coat pocket, and put her finger on the corner of each. "The stamp's right. Somebody's copied it well, it's a good copy. The number's wrong. We issue in hundreds by the office they're for, and the tank yard's run is the four hundreds, and there's no four-seven-three; we're up to four-sixty." She moved her finger. "And the card. Ours is the mill's card, from the paper mill above Wayford, that we buy by the ream. This is a stationer's card. Thicker. It's the wrong white." She looked at Prewitt, sitting at the far end of the table between two knights with his hands in his lap. "Did you make this?"

Prewitt looked at his hands.

"Yes," he said.

"It's quite good," said Tess, and went back to the Works.



The letter was in his coat. Osric's men had found it when they searched him, in the inside pocket, sealed with a plain wax wafer and addressed in a careful clerk's hand to the foreman of the port tank yard; and Prewitt had seen them take it out and gone so white that one of the knights had thought he'd faint, and put a hand on his shoulder.

Corbin didn't open it then. He didn't know why not. He put it on the table in front of him, sealed, and sat and looked at it all through the afternoon while Prewitt sat at the other end of the table with the knights, silent, and in the evening he sent for Doran.

He opened it in his own office on the Saturday morning, with Doran in the chair by the window and Prewitt standing, because Prewitt wouldn't sit.

It had taken him two days to come to it. He knew that. He'd sat with the sealed letter on his desk on the Thursday night and the Friday and not opened it, because it was the only thing Prewitt had, and because a man of the old faith who'd been caught running with a sealed letter the week the Church burned the port could have anything in it, an order, a map, a name, a list of the tanks; and because the moment he broke the wafer he'd know, and he'd have to do whatever it was he'd have to do. And because Prewitt had asked him, on the stair, and again in the long room, and again on the Friday, in the same small voice, to read it with a priest there. Not to be confessed. For a witness. Someone who'd say afterwards what was in it.

So Doran sat in the chair by the window in his old black cassock with his hands in his sleeves, and Corbin broke the wafer with his thumb, and read it aloud.

Sir. You don't know me. I go to the chapel by the old customs house. On the Sunday before last I was at the capital for my sister's wedding and heard the office at a chapel there after, and the curate there, after the office, said to some men by the door that the Church would come for Kaldo's oil when the fleet was far. I don't know when. I don't know if it is true. I can't take it to the castle, because they'd ask me how I heard it and where, and hold me with it. I'm sorry for the pass. Look to your full tanks. A friend of the yard.

Corbin read it twice.

Then he looked up at Prewitt, standing in front of the desk in his black coat with his hat in his hands, and Prewitt looked back at him; and Corbin understood, looking at him, every single thing that had happened to this man since the Sunday before last.

He'd heard it. At a wedding, in the capital, from a curate at a church door. He'd come home with it. And he'd been afraid, quite rightly, that if he walked into the line room or the castle's gate and said I'm of the old faith and I heard in a chapel that the Church is coming for the oil, the first thing the castle would want to know was what else he knew, and the second was who he knew, and he'd be in a cell by evening as the Church's man. So he'd written it down. He'd forged a works pass so that he could get into the tank yard and give it to the one man who could do something about it without asking how he knew. He'd gone to the gate on the Tuesday afternoon. And the gatekeeper had looked at a stationer's card that was the wrong white and sent him away.

And that night the Church had come, and gone for the full tanks.

"Your house," said Corbin.

Prewitt's mouth moved.

"The neighbours," he said. "On the Wednesday. In the night, after. They knew where we go on Sundays. They came in and went through everything. They broke the front window." He looked at the hat in his hands. "Hettie was in the back. They didn't touch her."

Doran hadn't moved in the chair. Corbin didn't look at him. He looked at the letter on the desk, the careful hand, a friend of the yard.

Two days. He'd had the warning in his hand for two days and not read it, while the whole of Osric's company and half the castle went over the wrong man; and the man had had it for a week before that and not been able to give it to anybody, because of what the city would have done to him if he had. The city had done it to him anyway.

"You'll go home," said Corbin. "Now. Osric will walk you."

Prewitt stood there.

"The pass," he said. "It's forged. I..."

"I know it's forged. Tess says it's quite good." Corbin folded the letter and put it in the desk's drawer. "Go home."



He went to the Prewitts' row himself that afternoon with a glazier.

He hadn't told anybody he would. He took the glazier from the yard behind the castle stables, a big slow man named on nobody's list, and a pane cut to the size Osric's man had paced out that morning, and walked down the Gate Avenue and across the timber wharf with the glazier beside him carrying the glass; and the street saw him come. The whole row was out on its steps by the time they reached the green door. The regent's son, in his grey coat, with a glazier.

Hettie Prewitt opened the door. She looked at him, and the glass, and stood aside.

Corbin held the pane while the glazier took the planks off. He held it while the glazier set it and puttied it and ran his thumb round the putty, and it took the best part of an hour, and the street watched every minute, and kept its mouth shut, and so did he. When it was done he paid the glazier on the doorstep, in coin, in front of the street, and the glazier counted it and touched his cap, and Corbin nodded to Hettie Prewitt in her doorway and walked back up the row toward the wharf.

Behind him, he heard a door open on the far side of the street. And then another.

He didn't look round.



Tess drew the dam on the Monday.

The fifteenth. She'd been up the gorge twice since the Wednesday with the survey crew, once to see it and once to measure it, and she'd known by the end of the first time that there wasn't anything up there to mend. The first stage was a heap. The plant house at its foot was a black hole in the gorge's floor with the river going round it. She stood on the north ridge with the sheet on a board and drew the first stage again on top of where it had been, from the measurements, in pencil, the whole long elevation of it and the plan, and it took her most of the day.

Then she wrote across it, in ink, in capitals, from one side of the sheet to the other.

NO LIQUID MANA STORED AT A DAM'S FOOT. EVER.

And under it, smaller, because it was the answer and not the rule: an Answer Tower. A harbour-class one, like the harbour's own, on the rebuilt dam's crest. If somebody stood on those ridges again and stacked the reservoir, they'd be stacking it under a gun that was looking at them, with the water at its feet. She drew the tower on the crest and its field of fire up both ridges, and wrote the number of rounds it would want beside it, and sent the sheet to the long room for the regent's seal.

It wasn't what she wanted to be doing. What she wanted to be doing was the Great Answer Tower, on the castle's hill, whose generators were meant to have run on the port's reserve, and the reserve was smoke. And the relief pair needed fuel. Hulls five and six, in the yards, that were meant to go out to the fleet in the autumn with stores; the regent had sealed them to load and stand ready on Saturday, on the figures Ferra had sent from the Rim. Tess had the figures on her bench. She'd read them twice. There wasn't enough oil in Randillia to fill them and the tower both, now, unless it came from the Isle.

So she spent the Tuesday on the Isle's bore map.

She had it from the Works' records, the big linen one the Isle works had sent across in the winter with every bore they'd sunk on it, numbered, with its depth and what it had brought up; and she laid it out on the long bench in the drawing office under the north windows and went over it with the regent's order beside it, which asked the Isle works to raise their output, and worked out bore by bore where more could come from.

And in the north-east corner of the sheet, under the bore numbers, there was a line.

Faint. Old. A surveyor's line, in brown ink, from before the works; somebody's first walk over the ground, by the look of it, with distances in chains along it and a bearing at each end. She'd never noticed it. She'd never had a reason to look at that corner. It ran from the north-east headland down to a point inland and stopped, and the point it stopped at wasn't on the bore map at all. There was no bore there. There was nothing there. And the line's last distance, when she ran a rule along it, didn't match the scale of the sheet it was drawn on.

She sat back on her stool and looked at it.

It wasn't anything. A surveyor who'd been careless, years ago, on a sheet somebody had used for something else afterwards. She'd seen a hundred lines like it. She took the Works' book down off the shelf and put it on the day's page, in her own hand, plainly: Isle bore map, NE corner: an old survey line, brown ink, ends at no bore; last distance doesn't match the sheet's scale. Noted. Not explained.

Then she put the book back and went on with the bores.



Wren was on the Motion Shop's bench when Tess came through on the Thursday, where she'd been told to be, and she was furious about it.

She'd been furious since the Wednesday morning. She'd wanted to go up the gorge with the survey crew, and Tess had said no, and she'd wanted to go out to the north field where the hull had come down, and the regent's knights had said no, and she'd wanted to help Bram's yard men clear the dome's glass, and Orla had said no, because Wren was the only one in the Motion Shop who could keep the chair's motors and the golem's joints and the new arm Tess was building for the Well's crane all going at once, and with Tess up the gorge and Bram on the other side of the world somebody had to. So she'd been kept at the bench. Everybody in the city had a story about that night except her.

"I held a soldering iron," said Wren, when Tess stopped by her. "All night. For the line room's board, when Ambrose's people kept shorting it. That was my night."

"That was a useful night."

"It was a boring night."

"Most of the useful ones are," said Tess, and looked at the arm on the bench, and found two things wrong with it, and told her.

On the way back to the drawing office she went through the line room. Ambrose's people were at the switchboard with their headsets, and the big set at the end hissed with the relays, and on the board by the door where the night board's faults went, there was a new line at the top in Saban's square hand, chalked and then gone over in ink so it wouldn't rub off.

The beat's wander changed, from 10 June. Any drum's last gasp raises the alarm, caught or not. S.

Tess stood and read it. The heartbeat that told the Well every drum was alive had wandered in a pattern since the spring, so that nobody listening could time anything to land just after a beat. The Church had learned the pattern and timed a whole raid to it. Saban had changed it the morning after. He'd said in the spring that changing it would tell them they'd been heard. They knew now.



Harrold tried it in the castle yard on the Saturday night, the thirteenth, after everyone had gone to bed, because he wanted to know.

Not the whole of it. The whole of it he knew. It had come on him over the dome without asking and stayed for hours, and he'd lain on cracked glass in the dawn with the city looking up at him, and he didn't want that again until he had to. He wanted the other thing. The thing Corbin had done on the bridge. A man's shape, with the dragon in it; wings folded, claws, but standing. Something a man could walk to a door in.

He went out to the yard behind the stables at midnight in his shirt, where the stable boys slept in the loft over the tack room and nobody else came, and stood in the middle of the cobbles, and reached for it.

He got the dragon.

All of it. At once, the way it had come over the dome: he reached for a hand's worth and the whole of it came, from the jump, because that was the door that was open and that one came easily. He was standing on the cobbles in his shirt and then he wasn't; he was lying across the whole of the stable yard, white and enormous, with his tail along the wall of the coach house and one wing half open against the loft, and the light coming up in him under the scales, gold, so that the whole yard lit; and the loft door opened, and four stable boys in their nightshirts stood in it and looked at him.

He couldn't go back.

That was the same too. Coming back was the hard part. He lay on the cobbles and reached for the man and the man wasn't where he could get at him. He lay there and breathed. The stable boys didn't go back to bed. They sat in the loft door in a row with their knees up and watched him until it got light, and nobody came, because nobody else was awake, and he couldn't call anybody without waking the castle.

At a little before five it let go of him. All at once, as it had over the dome; and he was a man on his back on the cobbles in the dawn with the rags of a shirt round him and his hands still black from the burns.

He got up. He looked at the four boys in the loft door. They looked at him.

"Bed," said Harrold.

They went.

He walked into the castle in what was left of his shirt, and up the back stair, and didn't try it again in front of anyone.



Amara went to the market on the Thursday morning, the eleventh, the day after the raid, and bought bread.

She did it on purpose. Harrold knew she did, because she told him at breakfast that she would, and he let her, and she put on the grey dress with the high collar and her gloves and her hair under its net, and took the basket, and walked down the Gate Avenue to the bakers' row in the market square at nine in the morning in full daylight, the way she'd gone to the market a hundred times since the winter.

The whole city had seen a woman go up over the dome on a tongue of fire. Some of it had seen her face, in the last of the white light, when the helm drew back on the dome.

Harrold had it from the home knight who walked twenty yards behind her each morning without being asked. She bought two loaves at the third stall, where she always bought them, and paid, and the baker's wife who served her didn't take her eyes off Amara's face the whole time and dropped the change. And when the woman behind her in the line, a big red-armed laundress with a child on her hip, said loudly to nobody that she'd seen that face over the dome on Tuesday night, plain as day, Amara turned round and looked at her pleasantly and said, "People tell me I look like her."

And walked home up the Gate Avenue with her basket.

Saban's rule. Never deny, never explain. Harrold had heard her say the line four times by the Saturday, by report, to four different people; she'd gone to the market every morning that week.

The city split down the middle on it. He heard both halves. On the Gate Avenue and in the Works and among the knights at the base they said it with their chins up, that the woman over the dome had brought down a Church hull that was trying to burn the tree, and whoever she was, she was theirs. In the lower ward and the old customs house streets they said it lower, and crossed themselves, and some of them stopped going to the market on the mornings she went.

The pulpits had it by the Sunday.

Not Doran's. The others: the two by the old customs house, and every pulpit in the kingdom that took its sermons from the cathedral at the capital. For two months they'd preached the king who'd left his people for the cursed sea. On Sunday the fourteenth they stopped, all of them, in the same week, as if a letter had gone round, and preached a new thing. That a dead queen had risen over Kaldo in the night and flown, and brought down a ship of the faith with fire. That the king's mother was long years in her grave and walked the market. That there was a word for raising the dead, and the Church had always had it, and the word was necromancy.

Saban brought him the gist of three of the sermons on the Monday, written out by people Saban paid to sit in the back.

"Do we answer it?" said Harrold.

"No," said Saban. "Never deny. Never explain." He put the papers on the table. "She buys bread. That's the answer. It's a good one."



They buried Declan on the Monday, the fifteenth, in the knights' ground below the base, at ten in the morning, in the rain.

Rylan and Osric carried him, at the head, with four of the home knights behind; and the whole of the home company stood on the wet grass in their blues with their rifles reversed, and the families behind them, and behind the families more of the city than Harrold had expected, standing at the ground's wall in the rain with their hats off. Declan's mother was at the front. She was a small upright woman in black who'd come up from the country on the Sunday's train with the relay slip of Emeric's letter in her hand, and she held it all the way through, folded, against her coat.

Harrold spoke. He hadn't wanted to and he did it anyway, because the regent spoke at a knight's grave and because nobody else should have to. He said that Declan had asked leave properly. That he'd been right about where they'd stand. That he'd held the thing that killed him long enough for his knights to finish the work, and that every one of his knights was standing on this grass. He said it in about as many words as that, and stopped.

Doran said the words. The old ones, the plain ones, in his old black cassock with the rain on his face, without hurrying.

And then Rylan read Ward's.

It had come by relay on the Saturday, held, with a note on it from the line room that it was for the grave and not before. Rylan read it off the slip standing at the head of the grave in the rain, in his ordinary voice, which shook once and then didn't.

Declan. You asked me if it was right and I said it was. It was. You talked to your horse the whole way south and I never told you to stop. I should have said that. Ward.

Rylan folded the slip and put it in his coat. They lowered Declan into the ground.

Before the spades, Osric stepped up to the edge and dropped something in on top of the coffin, and stepped back into his place with his face doing nothing. An old riding boot, cracked across the instep. Most of the people at the wall didn't know what it was. Rylan, who'd ridden south with them in the first week, did, and made a noise that was nearly a laugh and turned it into a cough.

Amara had come. She stood at the back by the ground's wall, among the people with their hats off, in her grey dress, and Harrold had seen two or three of them shift a step away from her when she arrived, and seen her let them. He went to her when it was done. The company marched off by fours. The families went slowly down toward the road with Declan's mother in the middle of them. And Rylan came back across the wet grass with Ward's slip in his coat and stopped in front of Amara.

He'd been a delver since the winter before the long sleep. He knew where she'd been all those years. He'd seen her.

"You were in a stone," he said.

"Yes."

"I was on the ridge. Ten feet off him." The rain ran off the brim of his hat. "If I'd known how. Could we have put him in one."

"No," said Amara.

She said it gently, and at once, so that he wouldn't have to stand there waiting for it.

"Augustine had me in a gully with the fight over and nobody coming," she said. "The field was his. And it still took him so long that they had to leave my body where it lay. A whole mind doesn't go in a breath, Rylan. You told us how long Declan had. It wasn't long enough for anybody."

Rylan looked at the grave. The men with the spades had started.

"And it isn't sleep, in there," she said. "I'd not have wished it on him."

"No." Rylan put his hand to his coat, where the slip was. "He'd have hated it anyway. Nobody to talk to. No horse."

Amara laughed, one short surprised breath of it, and put her gloved hand on his arm; and he went off down the hill after the others.

Declan's mother had stopped at the gate at the foot of the ground, with the slip still folded against her coat, and let the others go on without her. Harrold went down to her. He'd told her son he'd take her up the tower himself, in July. It was June, and he did it anyway. He took her up that evening, when the rain had cleared, and stood with her on the leads while the dome's held faces caught the lamps of the city below, half of it lit and half of it dark, and she didn't say anything about it, and he didn't either.



The juggernaut was in a drained cell at the base, under the old armoury, where Harrold had put it on the Wednesday with a man to sit with it.

It had been fed. Somebody had to sit with it, Harrold had said, because it was a man, or near enough, and men who were chained in the dark went mad; and a knight of the home company had drawn the first watch and asked for the second, and was there now when Harrold came down the steps on the Tuesday afternoon with Amara, on a stool outside the bars with a lamp and a book he wasn't reading, and stood up when the regent came down.

It sat against the far wall with its knees up. A huge thing. Taller than Harrold, wider through the shoulders than any knight he'd ever seen, with its hands in drained iron on its knees and its head down; and its face, when it lifted it at the lamp, was a young man's face, smooth, with nothing in it at all.

A steward of the base had come to him on the Monday after the burial and asked, respectfully, whether it should be put to death. It had killed a knight of the household. It was the Church's. It was dangerous and it ate as much as three men and there was no knowing what it was for. Harrold had said no, a second time. He'd said the same thing he'd said on the bridge: it had done what it was made to do, and he wanted who'd made it.

So Amara read it.

She didn't need it to agree. Harrold knew that, and so did she. Imani had opened the dead ones in the cold room in the winter and found what was in the middle of them, which was nothing: no wall, nothing that could push back. A thing built like a house with no doors, so that a handler could walk in and give orders. Any delver could walk in. It couldn't say no. Amara stood at the bars in her grey dress and took one glove off and looked at it, and Harrold knew she'd gone in only because she'd gone still. The young man's face against the wall did nothing at all.

She was in it until the lamp needed trimming.

When she came out she put her glove back on, slowly, finger by finger, and kept quiet until they were up the steps and outside in the yard with the rain stopped and the light going.

"There's nothing to take," she said. "They sent it blind. Every hold in it is shut, and nothing behind them; no route, no names, nobody's face but its handler's, and he's dead." She looked at the yard. "They knew it might be taken. They made sure it couldn't tell us anything."

"Then what's in it?"

"Its life." She was quiet a while. "It was made in a woman. Inside one. By the Church, on purpose, for this. And raised to orders. From the start. Orders and food and the yard where they trained it, and nothing in the middle." She put her gloved hands together. "No mother. No name. Nobody ever spoke to it except to tell it what to do. It doesn't know it's a person. It doesn't know there's a thing to be."

Harrold looked at the door to the cell steps.

"Keep it alive," he said.

"I know."

"The knight sits with it."

"He asked to," said Amara. "Did you know? He asked for the second watch and the third." She looked at the door too. "He reads to it. He doesn't think it understands."



Saban came to the long room on the Tuesday evening with the customs house's bonded-stores ledger under his arm and a sheet in his own hand, and put both on the table. It was what he'd told Harrold at noon on the Wednesday with the ledger open, written down now, and marked at the top with one word: guessed.

Harrold didn't know the word in that sense. Saban told him: Ferra's word, in Ferra's book, for what you knew from what you'd seen, set apart from what you'd seen. Saban had started using it. Everybody who read Ferra's slips had started using it.

"It's the nephew," said Saban. "The Velmoor clerk's. I'm calling it guessed, because I can't prove it, and I'll never be able to." He kept his finger on the column. "He was in the customs house every day from April, under escort, doing exactly what your exception said he could. He never left the counting room without a man at his elbow. He never touched a paper he wasn't given. He broke no condition. And this ledger sat on the desk in front of him every day, open, with every tank in the port yard in it, and what was in each one, by the week." He took his finger off. "He sailed home with the Velmoor ship on the thirtieth of May. A man with a good memory and a ledger in front of him for six weeks doesn't need to carry a paper out. He carries it in his head."

"And from Velmoor..."

"To the Church. Through somebody. I can't name who and I never will." Saban shut the ledger. "That was mine to see and I didn't see it."

Harrold was quiet a while.

Then he took a sheet of the long room's paper out of the drawer, and his pen, and wrote, at the table, in front of Saban, in his large plain hand. That the exception granted to the clerk of the Velmoor house in April for his nephew's presence in the customs house was revoked from that day. The date. And the reason, in one line: that the exception's conditions had governed the man and not the ledgers. He signed it and sealed it.

The contract stayed. He said so. The Velmoor cash ran the kingdom programme that summer, the schools and the roads in the west, and he wasn't going to stop the roads because a boy had a good memory.

He didn't apologise to Saban. Saban had objected to the exception in April, in writing, and the objection was in the minutes under it, and it stayed there. Harrold didn't say anything about it, and Saban didn't expect him to; he picked up the ledger and went out, and at the door he stopped.

"Minute that," he said.

"I will," said Harrold.



The regency sat at the capital on the Wednesday, the seventeenth, and he went up by the early train.

He'd have liked not to. His back still hurt where the bomb had gone in, and his hands were still healing pink under the black, and the capital's council room was a long cold hall with too many windows and lords in it who'd voted for him and lords who hadn't. But the regency sat when it sat, and the regent sat in it, and if he didn't go they'd say he couldn't.

Lord Cassel wasn't there. He'd gone back from Kaldo on the Sunday. His report had gone ahead of him; Harrold could tell it had from the way the lords looked at him when he came in, which was the way men look at a man they've been told something about and want to see for themselves.

They did the river rights. They did the paper mill. They did the tram again. And at the end of it, when the clerk had read the last item and was gathering his papers, a lord on the east side stood up, an old one of the north whose father had sat in that hall under Harrold's father, and asked it.

He asked it to Harrold's face, across the table, politely, in front of all of them. Lord Cassel had written that on the night of the raid on Kaldo a woman had gone up over the city on fire and brought down a ship of the Church. That half of Kaldo said she was the late queen. That the Church's pulpits were saying the same thing, with another word on the end of it. And the lords of the regency would like to know, from the regent, whether it was true.

Harrold looked at him.

He'd thought on the train what he'd say. He'd thought about it most of the way. He couldn't deny it; it was true, and Saban's rule said never deny. He couldn't explain it; it wasn't his to explain, and Saban's rule said never explain. It was his son's mother and his son's secret and his son's kingdom, and his son was a thousand miles away at the bottom of the sea.

"Ask the king," said Harrold, "when he's home."

The old lord stood a moment.

Then he sat down. And Harrold watched the whole room take it, and write it down in their heads, and keep it, every one of them. It wasn't an answer. It was a date.



Corbin found Cinderhale's coats on the Tuesday, the twenty-third, in a steward's report from the east.

It was a small item. A line under the harvest figures, in a steward's hand who'd started putting his worst number at the top of the form as asked and then gone on to put the second-worst at the bottom, as if to show willing: The mill lords at Cinderhale have taken on a watch. Forty men. Against riot.

He sent for the rest. It came on the Thursday, the twenty-fifth, with a sketch the steward had had a clerk make in the street: forty men in new coats, all alike, of a dark cloth the steward's clerk had written was Dunholt's, with iron at the belt, and drilling in the mill yard in a line on the Wednesday morning like soldiers, which the steward had underlined.

Corbin sat over the sketch until the light changed.

Then he went down to the counting house, where Ilsa sat in Ferra's chair with the books, and asked her who held Cinderhale's mortgages.

Ilsa didn't need to look. She looked anyway, because she was Ferra's, and brought the ledger and opened it at the page and turned it round for him, and put her finger on the line.

Tullio's book.

The House's crown book, that Tullio Ducatis had taken out of the House when he left it: the crown's old debt, and half the old lords' paper, and the money that had ridden with them. Corbin had seen its pages once before. Years ago, in the Lieutenant's rooms at the capital, with Tullio himself turning them over for him one at a time, naming every lord who'd ride for him when he went to take his brother's crown.

"That's how they paid for me," said Corbin.

Ilsa looked at him over the ledger and didn't ask what he meant.

He went back up to the long room and read the levy laws. All of them, the old ones, the ones nobody had needed in living memory. They were there. A lord might raise and keep a watch on his own land against riot, at his own cost, in his own coats, and the crown had no say in it unless the watch rode off his land or fired on the king's people. Cinderhale's mill lords had raised forty men at their own cost and put them in coats and drilled them in their own yard. Every word of it was lawful.

He couldn't touch them.

He sat at the long table with the levy law open in front of him and the sketch beside it and wrote it up. All of it. The forty men, the coats, the cloth, the iron, the drilling, the mortgages, the book; and under it, the law, in its own words, and what it let them do; and under that, in his own hand, that he couldn't touch them, and why, and what he'd want to do if he could. It was for the call Ferra's office would book when the fleet had a day, so that when it came he wouldn't have to say any of it from memory.

When he'd finished it was dark. He sat with it for a while.

Then he took a fresh sheet and wrote one line at the top of it, small, and put it on the pile for Harrold in the morning. His worst number, for the week.

Cinderhale. Forty.



The Kaldo News, Thursday 25 June, year 17

The lady at the bakers' row. Readers continue to write in about the lady in grey who buys two loaves at the third stall most mornings. Nine letters this week say she is the late queen. Four say she isn't. One says she is very polite and always has the right money. Asked, she says people tell her she looks like the late queen. Asked, the castle says to ask the king when he's home.

The pulpits. Two chapels by the old customs house preached on Sunday the fourteenth, and again on Sunday last, on the raising of the dead. The castle has not answered them.

River mud. The Works asks lower ward households to leave river mud at the kerb in sacks, not in the river. Carts come on Tuesdays. Sacks from the Works Office counter, free.

Oil. Lamp oil is up a copper at the port. Electric light is not.
