Book 2, Chapter 37: The Fetch Line

She'd swum out past the break with him at half past nine, when the fire on the beach was still red and his brother and his brother's wives were still on the sand, and now it was gone eleven and they were still out there, and Zale's stitches were pulling.

He didn't mind. He'd stopped minding about ten o'clock. He lay on his back in the flat dark water outside the reef with the swell lifting him and letting him down, and his gills working slowly under his ears in the cool of it, and the sea holding him up so that the wound in his side was the only part of him that weighed anything; and she was beside him in the water, on her tail, with her hair loose and floating, a hand's width off. Then less.

The stars were very bright. There was no moon. Along the top of the caldera wall the Rim's lamps went round in a long broken ring, and below them the black water, and under that, if he put his face in it and looked down, the faint far glow of the city on its terraces, five hundred feet down, like embers in a grate.

They'd talked about nothing. The surf. The boy with the two fish. Whether Armand's eyebrows would grow back. Whether Ward had understood a single word Taurre had said to him all night, and decided he hadn't and had nodded anyway and that it was the most diplomatic thing a man had ever done on that beach. The set, in its brass case, on the sand by her shoes; she'd had her guards take it to the gate, to be there at dawn, and he'd watched her watch it go.

And then they'd stopped talking. A while ago.

"Zale," she said.

In the water. Not in Kaldo. In her own tongue, wet, with her colour; and he could see her colour now, in the dark, with the dark sight the bell had given him. Blue.

He turned his head.

She asked him for the sharing.

He knew the word. Ilenne had taught it to him on the K Endeavor's tank deck in the spring, with the others, in the list of words a husband would need; and Ressar had said it again in the hard rooms in the second week, stiffly, in the forms. The binding at the door had made them married, in front of the city, for as long as one of them lived. The sharing was the other thing. The stone rite, the old one, where each of a bound pair gave the other a piece of themselves through a stone, both ways, so that neither lost anything by it. It wasn't a law. It was a thing you asked for.

She'd never asked. They'd never talked about it. He'd thought, if he'd thought at all, on the bad nights, that she wouldn't now; that after the bell and the custom and the walk up the hall and the throne, after everything she'd had to carry because of him, she'd want to be married to him the way the law said and no further.

She was asking. Here, in the water, wet. So it was said for ever.

He lay on his back in the sea and looked at the stars and felt the stitches pull.

He was going in the morning. He'd chosen it, on his own deck, on the Sunday, and he wasn't going to unchoose it; and the sharing wasn't a thing for a man with a hole in his side on his last night, with a fleet waiting at dawn. He couldn't give it to her tonight.

He could give her the word.

He hadn't said anything wet since the door. Not one word. He'd been careful. He'd been more careful about it than about anything in his life; he'd lain awake on the floor of the bell going over things he'd said in the water in his head to make sure none of them had been a promise. Ilenne had taught him that the first week out, in the K Endeavor's tank, with the knights: in the water you don't say I will. Not by mistake, not for a joke. A thing said in the water before witnesses was said for always.

There was one witness. She was enough.

He turned over in the water, onto his front, so that he was face to face with her, level, with the sea up to his chin and his gills under it, and said it. In her tongue, which he spoke badly, wet, every word going out of him into the sea round them both and staying there.

"When I come back."

And then, because a man who'd lain awake over every word for two months wasn't going to give one this big without its edges, the rest of it. Slower, in her tongue, every word wet. So she'd know he knew what he was saying.

"I come back. Here. To you. Alive, if the sea lets me. By the fleet's road. No other." He breathed the sea in and let it out. "When I come back, we share. If you still ask. It's yours to ask."

He knew as he said it what it bound him to. He'd never be able to not come back. Whatever else happened on that road south, whatever he found or wanted or became, he'd said it wet, to her, and the only way out of it was the sea taking him first; and if he ever stood on a shore somewhere and decided not to, his word would be water, in front of a whole city that knew what he'd carried out of a broken bell. He'd be a man who'd said it and not done it.

It was the first true thing he'd said in the water since the door. It was the first thing he'd ever said in his life that he knew, as he said it, was true for ever.

Ilenne was quiet.

Then she put her hand on his face, under the water, along his jaw, over the gills, and held it there. Her colour was blue all through her; he could feel it in the water like warmth.

"Come," she said, in Kaldo. "Down."



She took him down the stages to the queen's house, by the long way, so that he could walk it.

He walked. His side told him every step of it and he didn't care. Down the cut stair in the dark with her hand in his and the line's lamps going by and the cold coming up, past the landings, past the third bell, past the place on the stage wall where somebody had scratched a mark at the height of a child; into the city's glow, and along the shelf to her mother's house, which was her house now, and up its bell stair.

The bell had been mended. The glassmakers' yard had done it in a fortnight, at the crown's order, the broken crown and the shell over the pallets re-leaded in new panes so clear you could hardly see them against the old green ones round them; and the anchor chain was the same, and the water door, and the two pallets. He'd slept on the glass floor beside hers every night since the binding, by his own choice, until the night of the fire. He'd lain three yards off, on the far side of the chain, with a blanket and his arm under his head, and listened to her breathe.

Thalune was at the water door.

She was always at the water door. She'd been at it since the night of the binding, drawn by lot from the great houses to watch a bound heir's door for the heir's first season, and she'd watched it through everything; she'd been at it when the charges came, alone, with her colour going in great dark waves through the water. He'd felt it go, through the sea, the night he'd carried Ilenne up out of the broken crown. He'd never seen it move since.

Ilenne stopped in front of her. In the water, outside the door, on her tail, level.

She thanked her. Zale didn't follow all of it; he followed the shape of it, and Ilenne's hands, which said it in signs too, for him, without seeming to. That the watch was for an heir's first season. That she wasn't an heir. That the court's watcher had kept the door of the queen's daughter faithfully, through the worst of it, and said what she'd seen and only what she'd seen, and that the queen thanked her for it, and that she could go home.

Thalune hung in the water and heard it.

And her colour came up, warm, slowly, a soft gold at the throat that spread and held, that Zale had never seen on her and hadn't known she had; and she bowed, the flat hand lowered, and then the second small movement of the hand that was the courtesy between equals. And turned in the water, and went up, slowly, into the dark, and away.

Ilenne went in through the water door.

He went in after her. Through the sheet of water and up into the bell's air, onto the rock, dripping, with his gills closing slowly in the dry and his lungs taking over with a cough; and she came up out of the water after him onto the rock in her land shape, on her legs, with her hair down her back and the sea running off her, and stood in front of him.



He didn't sleep on the floor.

He'd wondered how it would go. He'd wondered for months, if he was honest, since before the wall; he'd wondered on the K Endeavor's rail and on his own deck and on the floor of this bell, more than a man his age had any business wondering, and he'd never once imagined it going like this: the two of them standing on the rock in the bell's lamplight, dripping, married for two months, a man and a woman past forty who'd been bound to each other at a city's door by accident of law and had wanted this since the cold, looking at each other, and neither of them knowing who was supposed to start.

"Well," said Zale.

Ilenne laughed. In the air, out loud, a short startled sound, and put her hand over her mouth.

"Well," she said, through her fingers, in Kaldo, and took her hand away, and came the rest of the way.

She kissed him. He'd been kissed before; he'd been a sailor his whole life. He hadn't been kissed like that. She put her hands on either side of his face and kissed him as if she'd been thinking about exactly how for a very long time and had decided, and her mouth was cool and tasted of the sea, and he stood there on the rock in his wet shirt with his arms at his sides like a fool for about the space of a breath and then put them round her.

Her hand found his side. The bad side. It went there flat, through the wet shirt, over the stitches, exactly where they were, as if she'd known where they were all along, which she had; she'd had her hand on them in the hall with the whole court watching. She pressed. Not hard.

"Here," she said, against his mouth. "I know."

"I don't care."

"Imani cares."

"Imani," said Zale, "is on a beach."

She laughed again, and pulled the shirt up over his head, carefully, round the strapping, and dropped it on the rock.

The pallets were the same pallets. Woven weed, on the glass floor, against the curve of the bell's wall, with a blanket on each. She pushed them together with her foot without taking her eyes off him, which made him laugh, and then he wasn't laughing; she'd undone the ties of her dress at the shoulder, and it came off her in the lamplight and lay on the rock.

He looked at her.

He'd seen her before. On the beach at South Sentinel, half dead in the shallows, when he'd thought she was some kind of seal. In the tank on the Cradle. In the sea, a hundred times. He'd never looked, because a man didn't, and because it would have meant something he hadn't been ready to have mean anything. He looked now. Pale grey-white, all of her, in the lamplight, with the faint sheen on her like the inside of a shell; long, lean, a swimmer's shoulders, and her breasts small and high, and the long slight curve of her belly to her hips; and, when she turned, the two old white marks low on her back where the drain-stone bident had caught her running, and Imani's stitches had closed them.

He put his hand on them. The scars. Flat, as she'd put hers on his.

"Here," he said. "I know."

She made a sound he'd never heard her make, and pulled him down.



It was clumsy. He'd been afraid it would be, and it was, and it didn't matter at all.

The pallets were narrower than they looked and the gap between them was exactly the wrong width, and he found it with his knee at once and swore, and she laughed so hard she had to stop and lie with her forehead on his chest. His side wouldn't let him do half of what he wanted to do, and he wanted to do a great deal; and she knew it, and kept her hand on the stitches, and every time he forgot them and moved wrong she pressed, once, so he'd remember, and he remembered, and kissed her harder to make up for it. She didn't know where to put her hands. He didn't know either. They found out.

She was cool all over. Cooler than any woman he'd known; cool as the sea at evening, her skin, her mouth, the long length of her against him on the weed. And he was hot. He'd always run hot, and the change had made him hotter, and she shivered at it, at the heat of him along her, and pressed closer, and said something in her own tongue that he didn't know and didn't need to.

He kissed her throat. Where they spoke from. He did it slowly, knowing exactly what it was; never touch anyone's throat, she'd taught the knights in the spring, the throat is where they speak from, a hand there is a hand over a mouth. He put his mouth there, at the hollow of it, and felt her go still under him, all of her, and then not still at all. She put her hand in his hair and held his head there.

And her colour came.

He'd seen it at the feast, at the Rim, on Monday, coming up her throat to her face in front of everyone, blue. He saw it now. All of her. It came up out of her in the lamplight from wherever it lived, from the throat first under his mouth and then outward, down her shoulders and her arms to the fingertips on his back, down across her breasts and her belly and the old white scar, down her long legs to her feet on the weed; the deep-water blue, and moving, coming and going in long slow pulses with her breath. Every inch of her, blue. For him. He could see it.

He'd thought it was the cold.

He said so, against her shoulder, and she laughed, and then she stopped laughing, because he'd moved, and she pulled him in.

It was slow, after that. It had to be, with the stitches; and then it was slow because neither of them wanted it to be anything else. The lamp on its hook. The sea's noise through the new clear glass and the old green, the long hush and draw of it on the bell's shell, and the anchor chain creaking. Her breath in his ear, short, and the words she said that weren't Kaldo and weren't the Rim's tongue either, not any words at all. Her knees at his hips. The blue going brighter in her, and brighter, until it was almost light, until in the lamplit bell under five hundred feet of sea he could have read by it; and her hand flat on his side over the stitches, pressing, and pressing, and then not pressing at all, gripping, and her back coming up off the weed.

He held her through it. He held her with his face in her hair and the sea in his ears and her colour going through her in great slow waves, and then it was him, and she held him.

Afterwards she lay against him on the pushed-together pallets with her head on his good shoulder and her hand on his chest, and the blue going down in her, slowly, to a faint deep glow at the throat that stayed; and he lay on his back on the weed and looked up through the bell's new crown at the dark water over it and found that he was grinning like an idiot, and couldn't stop.

"Your stitches," said Ilenne, after a while, into his shoulder.

He felt. They'd held. Mostly.

"Imani's going to know," he said.

"Yes."

"She's going to look at them in the morning and know."

"Yes," said Ilenne, and he felt her smile against his skin. "Good."



Neither of them said anything about it in the morning. He came up the stages behind her at five with his shirt on and his hair wet, and across the gate to his ship in the grey light, and up the gangway; and his crew were on deck already, getting her ready to sail, every one of them; and every one of them looked at him once and then looked hard at whatever was in their hands, and not one of them said a word.

That was how he knew they knew.

Linnet Shaw was coiling a line by the mast. She didn't look up at all.

"Morning, Captain," she said, to the line.



Emeric came across from the K Discovery's boat at half past five to see him off, which nobody had asked him to and Zale had known he would.

He came up the gangway in his coat with his locs tied back and his face doing nothing much, and stood by the rail, and looked at the gate, and the breakwater, and the grey light coming up behind the caldera wall; and Zale came and stood beside him, and they stood there together for a while at the rail.

"I said it wet," said Zale.

Emeric turned his head.

"What?"

Zale told him. The whole of it, the word and the edges, in Kaldo, plainly, with his hands on the rail. When I come back. That he'd come back, to her, alive if the sea let him, by the fleet's road. That they'd share then, if she still asked. That it was said, and was said for ever, and he'd known it while he said it.

He waited for the joke. There was always a joke. He'd been waiting for one his whole life.

Emeric didn't make one.

He stood at the rail and looked at Zale's face for a while. Then he nodded, once, slowly, and looked back at the gate. That was all.

Zale noticed. He noticed it more than he'd have noticed any joke.

And Emeric didn't ask where he'd slept. Zale stood there and waited for that too, and it didn't come, and that he noticed most of all.

"Imani's coming over at six," said Emeric, to the gate. "To look at your stitches."

"I know."

"She'll know."

"Everybody keeps telling me that," said Zale.



He tried to change on his own deck at a quarter to six, while they waited for Imani's boat. He hadn't meant to. He'd been standing at the rail and thinking about the water, the way it had felt round him at midnight, the dark and the colour and the cool, and he'd thought, without deciding to, that he'd like to feel his gills open; and reached for it.

Nothing.

He reached again. It was like reaching for a word you know, as it had been in the after cabin in the dry after the bell; he could feel the shape of it and not get his hand round it. He stood at his own rail in the dry air with the sun coming up and reached, and reached, and his hands on the rail stayed a sailor's hands, and his neck stayed his neck.

"What?" said Emeric.

"Watch," said Zale, sourly, and put his arm over the side.

He leaned out over the rail and put his hand in the sea. Just the hand, to the wrist, in the cold grey water along the ship's side. And it came. At once, the way it always did: the webbing between his fingers, dark, and the fine grey plates up the back of his wrist, and on, up his forearm out of the water, to the elbow, to the shoulder; and under his ears he felt the four lines open in the dry air, and gasp, and close again, because there was nothing in the air for them.

He took his hand out and held it up, webbed, plated, dripping, and looked at it.

"The sea," he said. "It's the sea. On the dry I can't."

Emeric was looking at the hand. Not at the water. At the hand, and at the drops running off it onto the deck, which were nearly nothing, a few spoonfuls of sea.

"In the after cabin," he said. "After the bell. With Linnet's bucket. You put two fingers in it, to the second knuckle, and it came all the way to your shoulder." He looked at the drops on the deck. "Two fingers. That's not the sea doing it. There's hardly any sea there."

Zale looked at him.

"I don't think it's the sea," said Emeric. "I think it's you. I think you've decided it only comes when you're in the water. Or something in you has." He didn't smile. "I don't think it's the water at all."

Zale held his arm up and watched the plates go back, slowly, from the shoulder down, and the webbing thin between his fingers, until it was a man's hand again on the end of a man's arm, wet.

"Then I'll live in the sea," he said.

Linnet, by the mast, snorted. Hesk at the wheel didn't look round, and Zale could see his shoulders shaking. Emeric looked at him with an expression that said, very clearly, that nobody on the ship believed a word of it, least of all Zale.

Imani's boat was coming across the gate.



The fleet lifted at seven, out of the ring, into a clear sky, and went west and south toward the fetch line low over the sea, the way Sorrel had planned it in the spring, because the racks were low and there'd be no Band climbs until they were somewhere a dragon wasn't watching.

Ferra watched it from the K Discovery's after window, with her book.

Four of them in the air from the ring: the K Discovery, the K Endeavor, hull four and escort one, in a loose string at a few hundred feet, slow, the morning sun behind them putting their long shadows out across the water ahead. And under them, on the sea, Zale's ship and the K Tam, under sail and engine, going out through the gate in the breakwater and across the caldera's outer water toward the markers and the pilots' lane, with the watch's skiff ahead of them and a pilot standing in its stern.

Ferra watched Zale's ship. She could see him on her deck through the glass, aft, by the wheel, in his coat, with Hesk beside him; looking back.

And on the breakwater, at the gate, at the end of the long black wall where the watch's lamps stood, a figure in a long grey coat with something pale at her throat, standing alone, very straight, with her hands at her sides.

She didn't wave. Ferra had known she wouldn't. Queens didn't. She stood at the end of her breakwater and watched her husband's ship go out through her gate, and didn't lift a hand.

And went blue.

Ferra saw it through the glass, even from there. A colour at the end of the breakwater, deep, the only colour on the whole long black wall, coming up and staying; and she saw Zale see it, on his deck, and saw him put his hand up on the rail and leave it there.



The set rang before the breakwater was out of sight.

Ferra heard it, because she was in the K Discovery's radio cabin by then, where she'd gone to log the fleet's first position of the leg south with the operator, a young man of Sorrel's with spots and a careful hand. The board lit. A call lamp she'd never seen lit before, on the panel at the end, that the line room had wired in at home for the twenty gift sets and labelled in Tess's hand; this one, the first, with a card in its holder that said 1.

The operator looked at it. Then at her.

"That's the queen's," he said.

"I know whose it is."

"It's calling the fleet." He had his hand on the switch. "It's calling Zale's ship. It'll go through us to them."

"Put it through," said Ferra.

He put it through. And because the radio room heard what it took, which was what Ferra's own card in the lid of that brass case said, and Ferra had written it and meant it, the voice came out of the speaker on the operator's bench into the radio cabin, a little thin, a little hiss of distance under it, from somewhere under a glass float bobbing on the water off the breakwater's end, where the figure in grey had gone down into the sea with the brass case in her arms: Ilenne's, in Kaldo, careful, short, saying Zale's name.

Through the window Ferra could see Zale's ship below. She saw Linnet come up out of the hatch at a run with the handset on its cord and hold it out to him at the wheel. She saw him take it.

She saw the whole of his crew on deck, every one of them, suddenly find something to do at the other end of the ship.

And on the operator's bench the speaker went on, Ilenne's voice, and then Zale's, answering, both of them a little thin; and Ferra reached past the operator and turned the speaker's knob all the way down, until it was nothing, and took her hand away.

The operator looked at her.

"Log it," said Ferra. "The time. The set. That it called. Not what it said."

He logged it. 07.24. Gift set 1 (Aumere) to Z's ship. Through.



The K Amara and escort three came up to meet them outside the fetch line, at the Grey Mark's pillar, at half past nine.

Ferra saw them from the after window, coming in low out of the west where they'd lain for a fortnight beyond Aumere's water by the court's order, the K Amara's long black hull with the new port rack bank Bram's crews had put into her after the first dragon; and escort three behind her. They swung in at the head of the string and took their places, and the fleet was eight hulls again, and Ferra felt the K Discovery's deck shift a little under her as Sorrel brought her round onto the course for the south.

She sat down at the cabin table with her book, and closed Aumere.

It took her an hour. She did it as she'd done every stop's closing since the wall: the page at the back of the stop, ruled, in columns. What had come in, and from whom. What had gone out, and to whom. What had been spent, and on what, and what was owed. What had been promised.

The trade first. The cane, the pearl meal in its tins, the sea silk, the ash cement, the holdfast in its barrel. Bought by weight, in Kaldo iron, and every bar of it in the column. The iron contract with the crown; the quay; the runway, leased, on the queen's terms, building while the fleet was away, and Kaldo's iron and coin to pay for it going out on the boats as it was laid. The bench's figures, published. Three black stones in a tin in hull four's hold, by the queen's leave, for the assay.

The gift ledger. The tools to the reef apprentices; the pressure studies; the Mark II's pattern shown to the Keepers' divers; Ressar's rubbing, at no value; the first gift set, sealed, to the queen. And above all of them, the host's entry, in Taurre's officer's careful Kaldo, every adverse word of it, without Kaldo's certifying it.

The costs. Restitution, paid in full, to Nimo's family and Esseth's, the court's figure and the difference counted out in the water before the court rose. The condemned rounds. The rifle back in service. The rounds aboard: five hundred and eighty-eight, unchanged. Relays, ten in the hold, none laid since the Grey Mark. Rifles, six. Stores: about a hundred and twenty tonnes left of the two hundred and thirty they'd loaded, which would carry them to the first week of October before the fish bought at the Rim was counted, and the relief pair sealed at home to load and stand ready, and the next stops to buy at.

The wounded. Zale's side, closed again, and Imani's face at six that morning when she'd looked at the stitches, which Ferra had seen through the glass. The two badly burned from the first dragon, still on Imani's deck, booked home on the relief run.

And the promises.

The breach. The K Amara's crossing of the markers on the night of the fire, entered in the court's record as a rescue and a breach, and the breach entered under Emeric's name, by his own wet word, for ever. The K Amara barred from Aumere's water; the stone barred without the bench's inspection; any land hull back only by permit and with the watch. The Challenger's berth refused. The grove's seed refused this season. Ask me when you come back. And the runway's price, not yet named. What I want for it, ask me when you come back.

And Zale's. She didn't know the words. She knew there were some; she'd seen his face at the rail at half past five through the K Discovery's glass while Emeric stood beside him, and she'd seen Emeric come back across the gate in the boat afterwards and sit in the stern without speaking all the way. She put it down as she knew it. Z. said something wet. Binds him to something. E. knows the words.

And Davrel's family. Not found. The line open.

She drew a rule under the page, and closed the book on her pencil.



Emeric came in from the bridge at noon and sat down across the table from her, and she told him the totals, all of them, in order, because he'd ask if she didn't.

He listened. At the end he was quiet a while.

"The charge," he said.

"The Leaving Terms." Ferra opened the book again, to the dry hall's page in May. "The Rim's house laid it. The house is struck, so it lapsed. It isn't answered. Ressar said so in the hall: nobody in Aumere can answer it without the text, and the text's at the elves' ledger-house." She turned a page. "And one of the winged ones carried word of it south in May. Sorrel saw two go off the cliffs at dusk the day it was laid, and neither came back. One went east by north. One went south."

He looked out of the window, south, where the sea went on.

"So it's ahead of us."

"It's been ahead of us since May," said Ferra. "Whoever's at the other end of it already knows what the Rim's house said we are. They've had six weeks to decide whether they believe it." She shut the book. "Nobody aboard can answer it. We'll have to be answered."



Far off and a long way up, on a ledge of warm black stone over a sea that nobody from the land had ever seen, Sohra told her elders what she'd taken home.

She told it in her woman's shape, standing. They were in theirs. The elders on the ledge in the evening light, in their great shapes, their great heads low on their forelegs and their eyes half shut and their scale dull with age and dust, the oldest of them grey at the edges of every plate; and the warm wind coming up off the stone round them, and the sea a long way down.

She told it plainly. She'd been sent to see what had come out past the wall in the spring, on the Band, in eight hulls, drawing on the sky as if the sky were theirs. She'd seen it. A fleet. Thieves of the Band, every one of them, drinking it into their racks without leave, without asking anyone, as the old ones' fathers had said the land had always done. She'd given them the Terms. She'd burned one of their ships to show she meant it.

And one of them had come up off his deck at her.

A man. A Band-thief. With the old face. She said it plainly, because they'd want to know, and because she'd seen it close enough to be sure: the old face, the face from before the war, gold-eyed. And he'd fought her in the cloud, on fire, in a body that wasn't sure what it was, a man's body with a dragon in it trying to get out, and he'd beaten her. She said that too. She'd come home with his marks on her throat to say it.

And then he'd stopped.

That was the thing she'd flown home to say. He'd had her, at the end, in the cloud, with her fire gone and his hand where he could have finished it, and he'd stopped. He'd let her go. He'd stood on his deck afterwards bleeding with his people round him and let her stand there and look at all of them, and nobody had touched her.

She told them about the other thing last, because it was the one she didn't understand.

A gun. On one of their ships. Long, a man's height and more. It had shot at her, and she'd never felt it. Not the gun, not the shot, not the hand on it. Nothing in it had any of the world's breath in it at all; it was cold all through, iron and fire-powder and something harder than iron, and it couldn't be felt, the way a stone at the bottom of the sea can't be felt. She'd only known it was there when it flashed.

The elders sat on it.

That was what they did. Sohra had known they would. They didn't answer her. They didn't ask her anything. They lay on the warm stone with their eyes half shut and the wind going over them, and thought, in the slow way the old ones thought, which could take a season; and the light went red and then grey over the sea, and nobody on the ledge moved.

Except one.

He'd been at the back. Older than her by a long way and younger than the elders, in his own shape, a big one, dark, with the gold eyes of her kind. She knew whose kin he was. Everybody on the ledge did: kin of one the war had killed. He'd grown up on the stories of it, as they all had, and listened to them harder than anyone.

He'd listened to Sohra the whole way through without moving. Every word of it. The face. The fight. The stopping. The gun that couldn't be felt.

When she'd finished, he got up.

He didn't say anything. He didn't wait for the elders, who hadn't moved, and wouldn't for days. He stood up on the ledge and opened his wings, and dropped off the edge of the stone into the evening air over the sea, and caught it, and went.

Sohra watched him go until he was a speck against the grey, and then nothing.

The elders lay on the stone and went on deciding.
