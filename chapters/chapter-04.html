Chapter 4 - The Vouch

He already knew better than to walk into the town square and explain why.

Bacteria. Invisible creatures living in the water, some of them helpful, some of them lethal, killing people from the inside without anyone lifting a weapon. That story got a foreign engineer burned as a heretic or locked up as a madman, prince or not.

He didn't need them to believe in germs. He needed the water moved and the outcome to speak for itself. Nothing he said out loud was going to convince anyone here of anything. A prediction that came true might. So would a set of numbers pulled out of records they already trusted, which meant their own.

That was the plan in theory. In practice he was missing two things: someone in this town who would talk to him honestly, and a way into records nobody was going to hand a prince just because he asked nicely.

"Who keeps the death records here?" he asked Rylan on the walk back. "Baptisms, burials, whatever the parish logs."

"Priest keeps the parish book. Has for years." Rylan said it like it was obvious, then caught up to why he'd been asked. "You're not thinking of asking him for it."

"Not yet. I'm thinking about who'd vouch for me first, so that asking him doesn't go the way everything else here has gone since I arrived."

He already knew what that meant in practice. He needed a name this town trusted more than it would ever trust his title, someone whose word carried because they'd earned it across years instead of inheriting it.

"Bram," Rylan said, before Emeric had finished getting the thought out. "Blacksmith. People bring him more than horseshoes, if you understand me. He's fair, and he's been here longer than the war took anybody. If he says you're worth listening to, some of them will listen." A pause. "If he says you're not, it won't matter what you actually do."

That was either the best lead he was going to get or the fastest way to lose this before it started.

Emeric filed it next to the well positions and the burial ground and a plan that still didn't have a shape, and decided the forge was where his feet needed to go next.

The walk cut past the edge of town, where the buildings thinned out into what should have been farmland and mostly wasn't. Fields sat fallow in long stretches, the soil gone hard and pale where nothing had been turned in a while, weeds doing better than any crop would have. Further on, a mill wheel sat dead still over water with more than enough current left in it to turn the thing.

He didn't stop to look closer at either. He added them to the same mental column as the wells and the burial ground. Unsorted, unexplained, waiting on more data before they meant anything.

The forge took up a decent-sized corner of town, and the air around it was thick with iron and smoke and sweat. At the main hearth stood a man whose stocky build did nothing to soften the strength coming off him, the body of someone who'd picked up a hammer the second he was tall enough to swing one.

Bram never looked up from his work, and Emeric didn't want to interrupt him. He knew what real focus in a craft felt like, and he knew exactly how much it stung to be dragged out of it. So he waited, and watched the man work through the end of the day.

The metallurgy was good. Emeric could still see a dozen places he'd improve it, given the tools and the go-ahead. Bram had a mind built to lift this whole town, if anyone ever let him.

"Do you fancy strong old men, boy?"

He said it without expression, which somehow made it worse. Emeric nearly lost it.

"No, sir. I like women."

Bram's mouth twitched, the smirk of a man who took it as a personal challenge to be amused by anything. "Guessing you're a noble of some kind. I don't have all day, so what do you want? You've been watching me since morning. I worked extra hard hoping you'd leave. Damn near burned myself stalling. If it's all the same to you, I don't have anything to sell and I'm not buying. Something needs fixing, bring it here and I'll sort it."

"Straight to the point. I like that. I think I'm going to like you."

"Rylan. Is he sure he doesn't like men?"

"No, sir. From what I know, His Highness likes women. If he didn't, there's a good chance we wouldn't be here." Rylan delivered it with the same straight-faced respect he'd have given actual royalty, which made it considerably funnier.

"If there's a god, he's on my side today," Emeric said.

"Watch your mouth, boy. The priest's a stickler for that kind of talk."

"Fair." Emeric let the smile drop, just enough to signal that the next part was real. "Let's get to it, then. I've got a request, and I'm hoping you'll hear me out before you decide what to think of me. I could tell you I'm not like other nobles and that you should trust me, but you and I both know talk's cheap. I'm not here for your trust today. I'm here because I need something, and you're the only person in this town who can get it for me.

"The priest keeps the parish records. Births and deaths both. I want to see them, going back as far as they go. I'm trying to find the shape of this plague. When it started, who it's taken, whether there's a pattern nobody's had reason to look for. I can't walk up to a priest I've never met and ask for his death ledger. He's got no reason to hand it over and every reason not to. You do. Rylan tells me that when you vouch for a man in this town, people listen. I'm hoping that includes the priest.

"I won't pretend I've earned that from you. I haven't. I just got here, and I know exactly what I look like walking around with a title and no track record. I'm not going to offer you money. I don't think you'd take it. All I've got is this. The kingdom doesn't look twice at towns like this one, and either nobody's reporting what's happening here or somebody's making sure they don't. I want to fix that, and it starts with knowing what actually killed who, and when. I can't do that without those records, and I can't get those records without you."

Bram set the hammer down slowly and studied him the way he'd study a piece of metal he wasn't sure had a flaw in it yet.

Most nobles who wanted something out of a smith didn't ask. They told, and they didn't explain themselves to a commoner either way. This one had just spent two minutes doing both.

"I'm not vouching for you," Bram said finally. "Not yet. I'll walk you to the priest myself and tell him you're worth hearing out. What he does after that is his business, and what this town thinks of you after that is yours to earn, not mine to hand you." He picked the hammer back up, already half-turned to the forge. "Don't make me regret the walk."

Behind Bram's back, Rylan caught Emeric's eye and gave the smallest possible nod. That went about as well as it could have gone.

Neither of them said much walking out of the forge district. Rylan seemed to sense that Emeric had spent most of what he had left for the day and didn't try to fill the space with his own.

There was one more house before the light went.

"The healer," Emeric said. "You said this street."

"Imara." Rylan slowed without being told to. "She'll be in. She's always in."

The house had a low door and a smell coming out from behind it that wasn't the town's smell. Vinegar, something bitter underneath it, herbs hung to dry in a room with no spare space to hang anything. The woman who opened it was somewhere in her fifties and holding a strip of boiled linen, and she looked at him the way this town had been looking at him for two days.

"Whatever it is, I'm out of it," she said.

"I'm not sick."

"Then you're worse." She didn't move out of the doorway. "Sick I can do something about."

"I want to know what killed the ones who died."

That got him a second look, longer than the first, and no invitation.

"The priest keeps a book," she said.

"I'm getting the book. The book only has the ones somebody paid to bury."

Imara stood there a moment with the linen in her hand. Then she stepped back from the door, which was nearer to a welcome than anything he'd been offered since he arrived.

Her front room was the only orderly space he'd seen in Kaldo. Everything hung where it could be reached one-handed without turning around. That was the tell. The room had been arranged by somebody who once needed to work faster than she could think.

"How many?" he asked.

"Past nine hundred. I stopped counting the winter it got past what I could carry." She sat down, which she clearly did not do often. "So the number's wrong, and it's wrong low. Start there and you'll be closer than the book will get you."

"What kills them?"

"The ground." Flat, the way a person says a sentence they've had twenty years to test. "It comes up off the ground where the water sits. You'll see it in which houses go first, if you stand here long enough. Low ones. Wet ones. The ones out by the burial rows."

She was right about every house and wrong about every reason, and something in Emeric's chest went loose that he'd have called relief if he'd been honest enough to name it. She had the map. She'd had it for twenty years. She just had a bad name for the thing drawn on it.

"Did anyone ever come?" he asked. "From the capital."

Her hands stopped for the first time.

"I sent word three times the first year. Through the castle, because that's the road it goes down." She picked the linen back up. "I don't know that anyone read it. I know nobody came. The Circle keeps healers, Your Highness. I've never met one. I've met the idea of one."

"And if one came now?"

"Then I'd have somebody to hand the ones I can't keep."

He let that sit where it landed, then said the thing that had been bothering him for a day and a half. "There's a guard of mine carrying a knife wound eight days old. I told him to come to you."

"Big one. Won't sit down." Imara made a sound that wasn't quite a laugh. "He walked past my door twice today and didn't knock. Send him again if it makes you feel better. He'll come when it goes red and not an hour sooner, same as every one of them."

At the door, she looked at him properly for the first time.

"You're going to tell me it's the water," she said.

He hadn't said a word about the water.

"I'm going to show you," Emeric said. "Not tell you. There's a difference, and I'd rather you held me to it."

"Everyone's going to show me something." The door was already half shut. "Bring numbers or don't come back."

The light had gone further than he'd meant to let it. The town was settling into itself the way it did before dark, less a place with people in it than a held breath waiting to be let out.

Emeric let the quiet sit. It was, for the first time since he'd arrived, something he didn't have to solve.

The walk back up to the castle happened at proper dusk, the kind of light that made every doorway one shade darker than it should be. The pull found him one last time on the road up from town, stronger than any of the earlier passes, close enough that he actually turned and looked at the wall running along the road's edge.

Nothing. A gap in the stonework, an old drainage cut, shadow doing what shadow does at that hour.

Then Ward stepped out of a side alley thirty feet ahead of them, waiting at the gatehouse like he'd been standing there the whole afternoon. Forearm still wrapped. Expression giving away nothing at all.

Emeric stopped walking again. He looked at Ward, then back at the wall he'd been staring at, then at Ward again, and something in his engineer's brain filed the three data points into the same column for the first time.

The pull hadn't been random. It had a direction, and every single time, the direction pointed somewhere Ward could plausibly have been standing.

"You were on the roofline back there," Emeric said. Not a question.

Ward didn't confirm it. He didn't deny it either. "You're back at the castle before full dark. That's the part that matters."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the answer you're getting tonight." Something crossed his face, not quite a smile, closer to the specific satisfaction of a job done clean. "Rest, Your Highness. Tomorrow's going to want you sharper than today did."

Emeric let it go, for now, and kept turning it over the whole last stretch of road anyway. He had a mind that wouldn't leave a real repeatable pattern alone once it noticed one, and this was shaping into exactly that. Not imagination. Not exhaustion. Something with a source and a direction, tracking a man he now had every reason to believe could move through a town without being seen or heard.

Ward's build didn't explain it either. Six and a half feet of muscle should announce itself on a rooftop. Whatever that bracer's rune did for him, it was doing more than the fight at the campfire had shown, and Emeric added it to the growing list of things he intended to ask about once Ward ran out of ways to dodge the question.

He still had no name for what he'd felt. He filed it next to the well positions and the burial ground and a plan that was finally starting to take a shape, and decided it could wait until it had more data behind it.

Everything else tonight had earned his attention first. It was the dead of night, and Emeric was ecstatic about the coming days.
