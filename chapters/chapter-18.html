Chapter 18 - Held

He threw the deadzone into the seam of the two mages, and the world around them went quiet in the way only a mage could hear.

The cheap water-stone hit the ground between them and vented all at once, all its little charge dumped out crooked and wrong, and the field for a stone's throw in every direction went slack and dead. Both grey-robes reached for the braided cast they'd been building and found nothing to hold. Their charged crystals, the fat ready ammunition on their belts, went inert in the same breath, so many dead rocks. Emeric had killed magic in a circle, on purpose, without casting a thing bigger than a thrown rock, and for one held second the whole enemy center had no mages in it at all.

Then the deadzone did the thing he had gotten wrong.

He had tested the stones near nothing, out past the empty quarters where there was no one to hurt, and he had read their reach off that empty ground and trusted the number the way he trusted numbers. But a field with men and charged steel standing in it is not empty ground, and the null spread further and dirtier than his clean little test had promised. It rolled out past the mages and washed over the near end of Kaldo's own sortie line, and Rylan, who had pushed up too close reading the same moment Emeric had, felt every crystal in his Enhanced harness die at once. One heartbeat he was the most dangerous thing on the field. The next he was a very good soldier in a suddenly ordinary suit, in the front of a fight, on dead gear, because his lord had misjudged a falloff by twenty paces.

Emeric saw it happen and had no time to feel it, which was its own small mercy and its own accusation. He had built a weapon to kill the enemy's magic, and it had cost him one of his four, and he had done it with a clear head and a wrong number, the way he had almost cut a loyal man off a clean page, the way he had angled an air-line for iron that wasn't this iron. The mistake was always the same mistake wearing new clothes. He filed it, because filing it was the only thing that let his hands keep working, and his hands still had one thing left to do.

The enemy war-leader, Renner, sat his horse at the center of the mageless circle, rallying the charge he thought was still coming, the one man whose falling would take the coalition's nerve with it. He was across open ground and inside the dead zone, which meant no spell in the world would reach him, which was exactly the case Emeric had built the slug for. He braced the tube on the parapet, sighted down a hundred paces of dead air, and fired.

The air-crystal shoved the rifled cone off the muzzle with a violent crack, spinning it into the world. After that, it was not magic. The deadzone had nothing to say to it. It crossed the null as a plain, fast, heavy thing, obeying nothing but the arithmetic of its own flight.

Renner never saw it coming. One moment he was sitting his horse, his banner rising behind him, men watching him from the road. The next, something punched through his chest. There was no flash, no spell, no warning—just impact. His body jerked backward in the saddle as if an invisible giant had slammed a fist through him. A dark hole appeared beneath his breastbone, and blood erupted across his tunic a heartbeat later.

For a moment, Renner didn't understand what had happened. He looked down at himself, almost confused, waiting for the pain to tell him what he was looking at. It did. The pain came all at once. His breath vanished, his chest seized around a wound that had no business existing, and when he tried to draw air he managed only a wet, broken gasp. His fingers clawed at the ruined cloth, pressing against warmth already pouring between them.

His eyes found the blood, then the hole. He stared at it. What did that? There had been no spell. No glow. No rune. No shimmer of warding magic. The deadzone was still dead. And yet something had crossed it. Something had just passed through him.

The realization came slowly, horribly, as his strength began draining from his limbs. He looked toward Emeric, and for the first time, Renner understood the sensation rising inside him.

Not pain.

Not fear.

Death.

The pull of it.

His body knew what his mind could not yet accept.

He was dying.

His horse screamed as Renner slipped from the saddle. The banner behind him kept moving. His men did not. For one long second, nobody on the road understood what they had just witnessed. Then Renner hit the ground.

The center broke first, as centers do when the man they're watching goes down, and their mages have gone silent and the air itself has turned against them, and the break ran outward.

The coalition knights, magicless, their fireballs dead and their leader down, did the only thing a hundred committed men can do, which is come on anyway, out of the deadzone and up the open approach at the gate, still churning forward on the momentum of the ride and orders given by a man who was no longer mounted and no longer relevant. And Kaldo answered with the last thing the stale map had never accounted for.

The gate opened.

They came out in four columns, not a wall. Ward on the left flank, Declan to the right, Osric center-left, Rylan center-right. Each of them at the head of a tight file of five, the whole sortie no wider than four men abreast, moving fast off ground Ward had walked every morning for a season and the coalition had seen for the first time an hour ago. Rylan was on dead crystals, every charged piece in his harness gone inert when the deadzone rolled over him, and the field did not know it and he did not intend to show it.

Ward moved first, and the way he moved was the thing that cracked the coalition's shape before the swords did. One moment he was at the edge of their sight, a black figure coming out of the gate. The next he was somewhere else, somewhere a man could not have reached in the time it took the eye to follow, and then he was there again, inside the front rank, past the second, the gravity hammer already falling. The weightless upswing was nothing, a tap. The landing was a man going down through his own armor under a weight he had no frame for, and the one beside him had a half-second to understand he was now the front rank before Ward arrived in front of him too.

Declan cut the right flank before it could wrap, moving in the same grammar, that wrong-distance blur that did not resolve into steps a watching man could count. Osric drove the center like a post through ice, not swinging wide, always straight, leaving the men beside his line for the five who followed him. They were not elite. They had never been elite. They were men who had been drilled for a season into knowing exactly what to do in the space the man ahead had just opened, and they did it without gap, because gap was the one thing Ward had refused to tolerate since the first week. Not heroes. A system. Four systems moving at different speeds through the same crowd.

The cast-out men were in the front of that crowd. They had come up the road telling the coalition what to expect, and the men who guide you to a place are the men you put first when you get there. Three of them caught the column in the opening seconds. One crossed with Osric's file and had time to recognize the man directly behind Osric, a garrison regular he had ignored for two years running because the man had been quiet and paid on time and done nothing worth noticing. The recognition went both ways. It was a clean engagement and a brief one, and when it ended the file had already moved past. A second made it twenty paces in before Declan's angle found him. He had been one of Wexler's senior men, the kind who borrowed tools from the lower garrison and did not return them, and had not thought twice about any face in that lower ward, because those faces had not seemed worth the thought. He had time to think that, and then he did not.

The rest of the cast-out men peeled away before the columns reached them, because they had spent the ride up the road knowing this would go one of two ways, and they could read, by the time the gate opened, which way it was going. They had a map. They knew where the grain was.

The coalition did not break cleanly. It shredded, front to back, each piece dissolving the way ice dissolves from the edge nearest the fire, not all at once but in pieces, each one changing the calculation for the men behind it. The ones at the back had not yet reached the killing ground. They saw the front going wrong and they slowed, and then the ones behind them slowed, and by the time any of them arrived at a decision it was the same decision, which was away. What was left of the coalition ran, and Kaldo let them run, because chasing a routed enemy off your own killing ground with two dozen men is how you turn a victory into a field of your own dead.

The two grey-robes went with the rout. Stripped of every charged stone they carried, worth nothing in a fight now and knowing it, they had turned with the break and run with the rest, and Kaldo, holding its ground the way Emeric had told it to, let them go with everyone else. He marked it, because two mages who had stood inside a dead circle and felt a thrown rock reach into their hands and take the magic out of them were two men who would remember exactly how it had been done, and carry the memory back down the road to whoever had sent them. He had shown the trick to survive the day. He could not now un-show it.

They had won. Emeric knew it the way he knew a furnace had lit, by the numbers going the right way, and he had exactly long enough to know it before Ward's hand closed on his arm and turned him toward the town, toward the smoke that was not the foundry's.

The granary was burning.

The rest of the cast-out men had done the one thing their stale map was still good for. They did not know the new wall's weak points or the twenty's drill or the depth of the trenches, because none of that had existed when they left. But they knew where Kaldo kept its grain, because the granary sat where granaries had always sat, and it had not moved, and while the field was being won a handful of men who no longer had any stake in winning it had slipped to the one thing they still knew the location of and put it to the torch. The seed for next spring. The reserve against the winter. The whole cushion Emeric had built the barony toward, going up in a heat that had nothing to do with any furnace, and the men who'd lit it dying in the alleys around it because there was nowhere left for them to run to and they had known that when they lit it.

The unarmored dead were laid out that evening, and there were more of them than there should have been and exactly as many as Emeric had known there would be when he decided who got steel and who did not. Archers, mostly. Townsfolk who had stood behind stone in cloth and caught the arrows the good armor would have turned. He walked the row of them himself, because the version of him that had walked past Tam without asking his name was a man he had promised not to be again, and he made himself learn every name in the row before they were covered, and none of it brought a single one of them back, and he had known that too.

They held the vigil at the closed gate, the whole town that was left, eighteen hundred less the ones under the cloth. Nobody cheered the victory. They counted it, the way Bram had said they counted everything: the field won and the grain burned and the neighbors' banners gone back down the road they'd come up, and they set the win against the cost and did not pretend the two canceled. What Emeric said to them was short, because Kaldo had no patience for a speech and he had less than usual for himself that night. He said it would not happen again. He said he had built a thing worth taking and had not, until today, built the thing that would keep it, and that he had learned the difference at a price other people had paid, and that he would spend the rest of his time here making sure the ones who had paid it were the last. It was not a speech that flattered him. He had stopped writing those.

And the town, which had counted him all season and found him wanting in the one way Bram had named, watched the man who had beaten a hundred with two dozen stand over the row of the dead and take the weight of them onto himself in front of everyone, and closed ranks behind him. Not because he had won. Because he had not lied to them about what winning cost.

Out on the road, Baron Paxton of Milford took his unspent strength home without ever committing it, and thought, the whole way, about what he had watched. He had come up the road with a hundred men to break a plague town and had watched two dozen and one clever exile break the hundred instead, on ground that had been a ruin a season ago. He had seen the wall and the graded approach and the trenches that ran on nothing but the fall of the land, the mundane bones of the thing, and he had seen the mad prince meet fire with a handful of water and kill the enemy's magic with a thrown stone and drop a war-leader with something that flew like an arrow and was not one.

"He has a mage's blood, and he builds like a man who doesn't," Paxton said to no one who would repeat it. "He's got the magic. Why does he make himself lean on the mundane for everything it will bear?" And answered his own question a mile later, cold, the way a man answers a thing he intends to remember: because the mundane does not run out, and does not go inert when a stone dies, and cannot be turned off by an enemy, and a barony built on it holds when the magic fails. It was the smartest thing he had seen a lord do in thirty years, and it had been done by a boy his allies had ridden up the road calling mad, and Paxton went home resolved to be on the right side of it before someone made him choose.

He did not know yet whose vendetta he had just spent his neighbors' men on. Neither did Emeric, quite, though he had felt it at the edge of the day, the way three nervous barons do not muster and march in a single week without a colder hand somewhere behind them, lighting the fuse and standing well back. He filed that with the burned grain and the row of the dead and Rylan's dead crystals and Tam's name, the growing account of things he understood the shape of and could not yet reach the bottom of.

That was the thing the fire had truly cost, in the end, and it did not show up in the row of the dead. The granary had been the cushion, the margin, the whole reason the coming harvest had only ever needed to be adequate. Now the harvest did not need to be adequate. It needed to be enough to feed a barony through the winter with nothing behind it, and refill from ash everything the fire had eaten, and it needed to do it for a place that had just spent its last coin on the foundry and the wall and the steel that had saved it. Emeric had won the field and lost the one thing that had let him afford to wait, and Ferra had the ledgers open before the vigil candles were cold, and the number at the bottom of them was the same number it had been the night the foundry lit, only worse now, and running out of time in a way it had not been that morning.

He already knew what came next. He had known since he stood at the tap channel and heard the word muster. There was one road left that put coin in the barony's hand fast enough to matter, and it was the one road the whole design had been built never to take, and he was going to take it, because the alternative was to let the town's bet collapse to keep his principles clean, and he had learned that day, expensively, what it was worth to keep a thing pure while the people who trusted you paid for the purity.

"Find out," he told Saban, "where one finds House Ducatis."
