Chapter 3 - Downwind

After the attempted assassination, time flew like a runaway train. Emeric and what was left of his guard made it to town. He'd never wanted a parade of arrival in his life, but that raised a question the baron of this place had no way of answering correctly. Most nobles knew the etiquette when someone higher up the ladder came through. So either the man had chosen not to come out and greet him, or nobody had bothered to tell him a prince was coming. Emeric couldn't decide which was worse.

Four of them now, and Petram besides, bound and silent in the back of the carriage where nobody had to look at him directly to know he was there. Rylan drove, quiet in a way that hadn't been in him before the fire. Declan and Osric rode close flank instead of their usual loose formation, eyes on the treeline more than the road. Ward rode nearest the carriage door with his forearm wrapped tight under his sleeve and his jaw set the way it got when he wasn't going to mention the pain and wasn't going to be talked into resting it either.

Nobody had said much of anything since the fire burned down that night. Emeric hadn't slept right since. He kept catching his mind running the same diagnostic loop, the one his engineer's brain ran on problems it hadn't solved. Input, failure point, root cause. Every pass came back to the same unsatisfying answer. He'd frozen. Not for long and not completely, and the freeze itself wasn't really the point. The point was that something in him still worked exactly the way it had at seven years old, and he hadn't known that until it nearly cost a guard his life. The same guard now riding one-armed a few feet from the carriage door, because Emeric hadn't moved fast enough.

Kaldo announced itself before he saw it. The smell arrived first, and there was nothing faint about it. Woodsmoke on top, and under the woodsmoke something slickly sweet and worse, rot that got into the back of the throat and stayed. A burial ground too close to a well. Latrines that had never been dug anywhere sensible. He'd read about this exact failure once, medieval sanitation, disease vectors, always in the past tense, always something solved a hundred years before he was born. Standing downwind of it as a lived condition rather than a paragraph was a different experience entirely.

Then the walls. Low and old, patched here and there with stone that didn't match, repair work done by people who couldn't afford to do it properly and had stopped waiting for anyone else to. The gate guard who waved them through looked less like a soldier and more like a man who'd been handed a spear and told where to stand. Ward confirmed who they were and who they were there to see, and they were guided up to the castle.

Small was generous. It had been built for a barony twice this size, back when Kaldo still had a working port, and men who weren't dead or conscripted, and the gap between what it was built to hold and what it held now sat in every corner. The drawbridge chain had been replaced with rope at some point, thick enough to do the job and ugly enough that nobody had planned on it lasting. A section of the outer wall sagged where water had gotten in behind the stone and never dried out. Same patched-not-fixed logic as the town wall, just higher up and better hidden from anyone who wasn't looking.

Inside wasn't better. The entry hall had been sized for a household staff that no longer existed, half the torch brackets empty, footsteps carrying in a way that told him more about the payroll than any account book would have. A steward met them, thin and tired, doing the work of what should have been three people. He didn't apologize for the state of the place. It was written into the walls already. A corridor that had clearly held ornate art on both sides at some point led to the office of the current baron.

"Baron Kaldo, I presume," Emeric said, with the doubt already sitting in it.

Kal had been a knight. Generations down the line, that discipline should still have shown somewhere in the posture. Knights raised the way Kal was raised took it home with them, and it usually stuck for a few generations at minimum. This wasn't one of his.

The man behind the desk didn't stand, and didn't correct him quickly enough for it to read as anything but deliberate. Whatever he'd meant to say, he let the pause do the work first, leaving a prince standing there being wrong a beat longer than manners allowed. This moment gave the baron clear confirmation of the type of prince he was dealing with. It seems this one didnt have any type of information network. Likely everything said about him being a weak child with no true backing is true.

"Wexler," he said finally. He still didn't rise. He didn't offer a hand. "Baron Wexler. Kaldo's the land, Your Highness. Not my name." Another pause, just long enough to land. "The line hasn't run blood-to-blood since old Kal himself. No children of his own, so he handed it to the man he trusted most instead of a son he never had. My family kept the name we came with. Kaldo kept ours."

He'd corrected other people the same way. More than once, by the sound of it, and he'd never once minded doing it. The disdain wasn't in his face. It was in how little effort he spent hiding it, the specific laziness of a man who'd priced this visitor and decided he wasn't worth the performance of respect, only the minimum required not to be called insolent to his face.

Emeric, being a man of two worlds, didn't take offense. Wexler was senior, and for most of a life Emeric only half remembered, he'd been known as a waste of a prince. It was fair enough that people treated him like less. Making an issue of it would have brought him friction and nothing else, and if he ever did decide this baron had to die, he'd want the town on his side when it happened. He'd been here an hour. Blood had already been spilled on the road. He had no read yet on how the town would take any of it.

They exchanged the pleasantries the situation required and wasted a quarter hour doing it, and then the steward showed him up to his room. Nothing special. It would do. By the end of the day everything from the carriage had been carried up, and Emeric went to sleep with plans for the morning.

Morning came through a window that didn't quite fit its frame anymore.

"Damn. We're really not in the castle anymore," he said, and didn't waste any more of the day on it than that. He ate standing, whatever the steward's kitchen had managed on short notice, and went looking for Rylan before he'd finished the last of it.

"You said you hadn't seen your family in four months," Emeric said. "Take me with you."

Rylan blinked at him. "Your Highness, I can go alone, there's no need-"

"There's every need. I haven't seen the town. You have a reason to walk through it that isn't me inspecting anything."

It wasn't entirely honest, and Rylan's face said he knew it wasn't, but he didn't push further. Ward didn't like it. He said so with a look first, then with actual words when the look didn't land.

"You're not walking through an unmapped town with one guard and a title nobody here respects."

"Rylan's enough."

"Rylan's a father going home to his family. He's not watching the street behind you while he does it, and he shouldn't have to be." Ward's jaw was doing the thing it did when he'd already decided and was explaining as a courtesy. "I'm not asking permission. I'm telling you how it's going to work."

"And how is it going to work?"

"You won't see me."

"Fine. Then before you vanish, Rylan says there's a healer in the town. Imani. Have her look at the arm."

"The arm's fine."

"The arm's eight days old and you've been sleeping on it."

"You won't see me," Ward said again, which was the whole of his answer to that too.

That got a raised eyebrow. Ward didn't elaborate, and Emeric had learned by now that pushing on it wouldn't speed anything up. The forearm was still wrapped and still bad. He came anyway, just not at Emeric's side. By the gatehouse he'd already dropped back and gone quiet in a way that had nothing to do with the wound, and the second time Emeric looked for him, he wasn't anywhere a guard was supposed to be. The walk did more work than any report could have.

The smell that had announced Kaldo from a mile out only got worse inside it, and Emeric started tracing it backward from the symptom, the same as he'd trace any bad system. Even from a distance, the burial ground sat too close to the water. That much needed no local's help. The latrines sat too close to the wells. He couldn't say yet exactly what was wrong, not off a single walk through streets he didn't know, but he could already feel the scale of it under the smell, a shape he recognized from a textbook instead of a street.

The people he passed had eyes caught somewhere between hope and resignation. Somewhere past the second street corner, he felt it for the first time and had nothing to call it. Not a sound. Not a sensation he could point to anywhere on his body. A pull, low and without direction, a weight settling behind his eyes that hadn't been there a breath earlier. He stopped walking without deciding to.

"Your Highness?"

"Nothing." It wasn't nothing. It nagged at him the same way a machine running one gear tooth off true nagged at him, a wrongness too small to name and too specific to be imagination. He looked back down the street they'd come up. A woman airing bedding out a window. A dog asleep in a doorway. He kept walking, and the weight faded off into the background without disappearing, the way a smell fades once you stop attending to it.

He had no framework for this yet, and he knew better than to force one on that little data. So he filed it the way he filed anything he couldn't explain. Input observed, no working theory, revisit if it repeats. It came back once more before they reached Rylan's street, in an alley narrow enough that he had to turn sideways past a stacked cart. Sharper this time. Sharp enough that he looked up at the rooftops and found empty tile and a gap of grey sky.

Nothing there. It had a direction, though, one he couldn't have described to anyone, sitting closer to his spine than his ears and thinning out the second he tried to look straight at it, the way a faint star does when you stop watching it sideways.

He was a mage now. He still hadn't gotten used to the word applying to him, even inside his own head. He knew, distantly, that mages were supposed to have some sense for this kind of thing, a pull toward nearby mana, heat off a stove felt without touching it. Nobody had ever told him what that was supposed to feel like from the inside, and he had nothing to calibrate against. A decent-sized personal mana pool handed him capacity, not experience. Provisionally, he decided it was probably nothing. Stress. A bad night translating itself into phantom sensation. He'd revisit if the data kept showing up.

Rylan's house was small, and clean in the specific way poor houses stayed clean, cleanliness being one of the few costs left that didn't require money. Nessa answered the door and went still half a second too long before she remembered to bow. Finn hid behind her leg. Wren didn't, and stared at Emeric with the flat, unimpressed curiosity of a child who hadn't yet learned that princes were supposed to be interesting.

Emeric stepped back and let the reunion happen without hovering over it, let Rylan be a husband and a father for a few minutes instead of a guard. It cost him nothing and told him plenty. Neighbors came out for a guard's homecoming in a way they'd never have come out for a prince's arrival, and even then there was a wariness underneath it, the particular caution people reserve for nobility that shows up expecting gratitude. He had a lot of walking left before any of this turned into a plan. When the lovebirds had had their time, he finally got to ask what he'd come for.

"What was the town like, before?" he asked. "Before the war."

Nessa considered it longer than the question seemed to need.

"Busier. My parents talked about it more than I remember it myself. I was young when the men left, ten, maybe. One week the harbor had hands on every dock, the next it didn't, and nobody explained where they'd gone past 'the crown needs them.'"

"Nobody said which war."

"Would it have mattered? We weren't the ones deciding to fight it." No heat in it, just fact. "Then the plague came a couple of years after, once some of the men who came back were already trying to put things right. Took the ones the war didn't. Men, women, children, animals. It didn't ask who'd already paid."

"The census had this place at over four thousand once."

"It did. Ask anyone over fifty what it used to be and they'll tell you like it happened last spring." She glanced toward the window, toward the street. "Half that now. Less, maybe. Feels cursed some days, if you let yourself think that way. The old folks talk about it like the town is still that place underneath, just resting. The rest of us don't have anywhere else to go, so I suppose we agree with them whether we believe it or not."

Emeric took note of that. Four thousand once, half of it now. A war that was already over couldn't be helped. A plague, going by everything he carried, had a decent chance of being curable. Plagues normally started with bacteria. Which raised the question of how you explained bacteria to commoners who didn't trust nobles and had been cheated with less than nothing to begin with. So you're telling me there are creatures too small to see, living in our water, and some of them help us and some of them kill us. If he weren't a prince, that conversation put him at genuine risk of the stake, or a healthy dose of a lobotomy to cure his mania. He decided on field research instead, and time to collect his thoughts. Once Rylan was finished with the pleasantries, he came along, and the two of them walked the whole town.

By the end of it, Emeric was sure. The plague was almost certainly waterborne. The same pattern kept repeating wherever he looked. Latrines dug downhill and too close to the bank. Burial grounds sitting just upriver of the wells the town drank from. And no sign anywhere that a single person had ever connected those two facts to each other. It wasn't ignorance in the way he'd have mocked back on Earth. Nobody here had access to a framework that made the connection obvious. It was bad geometry, decades old, never questioned because nobody had ever been handed a reason to question it.

The pull found him once on that circuit, sharp enough that he slowed his stride to listen for footsteps that never came. Not enough data. Keep walking. He didn't mention it to Rylan. He wasn't sure yet it was worth mentioning to anyone. Near the well closest to the burial ground, a woman filling a bucket saw Rylan first and started to nod a greeting before she registered who was walking beside him. The nod died halfway through. She finished filling and left faster than a full bucket should have allowed, water sloshing over the rim with every hurried step.

"Was it me?" Emeric asked once she was out of earshot.

"Wasn't you specifically." Rylan didn't slow down. "Her boy didn't come back from conscription. Crown took him, Crown never said what happened to him, Crown's the only reason she's hauling her own water instead of having someone else do it. You're the Crown today, whatever else you are on other days."

"That's not something I can fix by being right about the water."

"No," Rylan agreed. "That one you don't fix. You just don't make it worse."
