Book 2, Chapter 14: The Long Table

10 May.

The Velmoor ship came up the river on the morning tide on the Sunday, with her flag at the peak and her hatches already off, as if she'd been told Kaldo didn't keep a Sabbath and wanted to be first at the table; and Saban watched her come in from the customs house door, where he'd stood since half past five, with the list in his pocket.

He didn't need the list. He'd had it by heart since Thursday. Three factors, two clerks, a master, a mate and the ship's own people, who would stay on the ship. Every one of the five who'd come ashore named in the contract by name and house, with where they'd sleep and who'd vouched for them, and every one of them seen by his own people at Velmoor a fortnight since, in an office over a dye-works, where a man of Saban's who sold rope had sat in a corner for an afternoon and watched them come and go. He knew their faces. He knew the clerks' hands. He knew which of the factors drank.

The customs house was ready. It had been ready a week. The new desk for the Velmoor factors stood at the river end of the long room under the window where the light was good, with its own scales and its own ledger and its own lamp, and a door at its back that went through to the bonded store and nowhere else, and a man of Saban's at that door who'd been told that the factors could go through it and nobody else could, and that he could knock down anyone who tried, Saban included. The other door, the one that went out to the quay and the town, had two of Declan's knights on it.

It was a room with a lock on it. Harrold had made it that. Saban had wanted the factors not to come at all, and said so, at the long table in the castle, with Vantic's four papers in front of them and the regent at the head of the table where his son should have been, and Harrold had heard him out, and then made the room instead. One year. A break clause on a day's notice. All sales on the quay, inside the customs house. No factor past the customs house door. Every man named. Payment before loading. And no substitute for a named man, ever, except by the regent's written exception, for cause.

The ship came alongside at ten past eight. Her people threw her lines. And at twenty past, the five came down her gangway onto Kaldo's quay, in their good coats, and Saban counted them as they came, and then counted them again, and then stood quite still in the customs house door.

Four of them were on his list.

The fifth was a young man, thin, fair, in a coat a size too big for him, carrying a clerk's case; and he wasn't on any list Saban had ever made. He came down the gangway last, behind the others, looking about him at the quay and the cranes and the big shed on the yard's slips with his mouth a little open, as men did the first time; and when he came to the bottom he took a letter out of his coat and held it up, before anybody had asked him for anything.

The letter was genuine. Saban knew that within a minute and made sure of it within an hour.

The named clerk had fallen ill at Velmoor the week the ship sailed: a fever, the kind that went through the dye-works every spring off the vats. His house had sent his nephew in his place, because the nephew had kept the books with him for two years and knew them, and the house had written to Kaldo to ask leave for a substitute, with reasons, and the house's seal, and the named clerk's own mark at the bottom in a shaking hand. All of which was exactly right. Saban had the seal checked against the seal on the contract and the mark against the mark on the letters of February. They matched. His rope man at Velmoor would confirm the fever by the next relay, and Saban already knew that he would.

The letter had come on the same ship as the nephew. That was the trouble.

The contract said in advance. It said every man named in advance, and seen in advance. A substitute could come only on the regent's written exception, and an exception asked for on the quay, with the man already standing on it, wasn't asked in advance; it was asked too late. So under the rule the nephew couldn't land. Saban's people told him so, at the gangway's foot, politely. He was to go back aboard. He'd be fed. He could see the quay from the ship's rail as long as he liked. He couldn't set foot on it.

The nephew went white and said that the cargo couldn't be weighed without him. That the books were in his hand. That the factors couldn't buy without the books and his uncle's house would lose its first cargo and its first year and its name in Velmoor, which was a small town and talked. He said it all very fast, and it was all true, and Saban stood in the customs house door and watched him say it and didn't move.

The factors were looking at each other. The master had come to the rail. The cranes had stopped.

And then, at nine, Harrold came down.

Saban had sent for him, because it wasn't Saban's to decide. He'd have liked it to be. He'd have decided it in a second.

The regent came down the castle hill on foot, alone, in his grey coat, with his hands behind him, and walked the length of the quay without hurrying and without looking at anyone, as he walked everywhere; and the quay got out of his way. He'd been a soldier before he was a king, and a king before his son made him a regent, and Saban had never in his life seen a man who hated a job more and did it better. He came into the customs house, and Saban gave him the letter, and the contract open at the clause, and stood back.

Harrold read both. He read the letter twice. Then he went out onto the quay to the foot of the gangway, where the nephew stood with his case clutched to his chest like a man holding a cat, and took his time over him.

"Your uncle," said Harrold.

"Sir."

"How long's he kept the house's books?"

"Twenty years, sir. Twenty-two."

"And you?"

"Two years. With him. Sir. I know them. I know every..."

"I believe you," said Harrold, and turned round and went back into the customs house, and sat down at the end of the long room on the clerks' bench, and held out his hand without looking up; and Saban, knowing what he wanted, put a pen in it, and a sheet.

"He's not on the list," said Saban.

"No."

"The letter came with him. Not before. The clause says before."

"The clause says a substitute by the regent's written exception for cause." Harrold wrote. He wrote slowly, in his square soldier's hand. "Here's the cause. A first cargo held on the quay over one clerk is a first cargo Velmoor goes home without. That's the contract broken on our side the first morning. That's a reason for a house in a small town to walk and say why." He didn't stop writing. "And a reason for every other house that's thinking about us to think again."

"That's what they'd want you to think."

"It's what I'd think if I were them, and I'm not, and I still think it." Harrold looked up. "He stays inside the customs house. He doesn't go past that door into the town, not for a drink, not for a walk, not to see the big shed. Every hour he's ashore he's got one of yours at his elbow. When the ship sails, he sails with her." He signed it. "That's the exception. Written."

Saban looked at it.

"The escort's the lock," said Harrold. "The room was the lock before. Now it's the room and a man. That's still a lock."

"It's a lock with a key that walks about."

"Yes," Harrold agreed. "It is."

Saban took the sheet. He read it once more, all of it, every line, and found nothing wrong with any of it, which was what he'd expected. Harrold had done a sensible thing for a good reason with every safeguard he could think of round it. And it was a gap. Not a hole; a gap. A man who wasn't on the list was standing on the quay, and would be in the customs house all week among the ledgers, and every safeguard round him was a person, and Saban had spent his whole life finding out what people could be got to do.

"I'd like my objection minuted," he said. "Under the first one."

"Minute it," said Harrold.

So Saban did. He sat down at the clerks' bench beside the regent and took the minute book out of his own pocket, where it lived, and turned to the page where in April he'd written Saban objects to the contract; reasons given; the regent proceeds, and wrote under it, in the same small hand: 10 May. Saban objects to the exception; the man was not seen in advance and the safeguards round him are people. The regent admits him under escort. Both noted. He turned the book round and Harrold read it and nodded once.

"Both," said Harrold. "Good. That's fair."

The cranes started again. The nephew came ashore between two of Saban's people with his case and went into the customs house and sat down at the Velmoor desk under the window, and opened his books, and his hands were shaking so much he couldn't find the page.

Saban picked the man for his elbow himself. He picked the dullest he had: a big slow patient fellow who'd been a carter, who never in his life had been interested in anything anybody said to him, and who could sit beside a man for twelve hours without either of them noticing. Then he went up to the castle to put the day in his book, and found he was angry, and didn't know at whom.



The fleet's news came in pieces, as it always did, on paper, in Linnet Shaw's decoded sheets off the relay, carried up the hill from the line room by a girl who ran.

The wedding had come on the Thursday before, the seventh, a little after noon. Saban had read it standing in the Board room doorway with his coat still on, and had taken a sheet of the line room's flimsy and written his reply on the doorframe and sent it down by the same girl for the four o'clock relay: nine words, to the ship, not to Zale. Harrold had asked what he'd said and he'd told him, and Harrold had gone back to his soup with an expression Saban had seen on him perhaps three times.

The rest came after. A sheet on the Saturday: the steps, the call, a man named Ushar refusing to speak in the water to a man who couldn't breathe it, and the sign returned. A sheet on the Tuesday night: the court in the dry hall, the binding found made, five to one, two houses holding their colour; a motion to set it aside put off to the season. And on the Wednesday, the twelfth's close, a long one in Ferra's hand: an old promise, charged, that nobody on Kaldo's side could answer, because nobody had ever read it.

Saban read that one twice.

Harrold read them all at the long table, where he read everything now, at the head where he hated to sit, with Amara at his left hand and Corbin at his right and the stewards' reports in a pile. He read the Saturday's sheet with his face doing nothing at all. He read the part about the man coming up from under, at the door, to Zale; and the hand moved sideways; and a man whose word is water, said to his son, at his son's own wedding.

He put the sheet down.

He said something then that Saban had heard soldiers say, once or twice, in the castle yard, when they thought nobody above the rank of sergeant was listening. It was short. It was about the merfolk court. It concerned what the merfolk court could do, and with what, and where.

Amara didn't look up from her own sheet.

"Say that again," she said.

Harrold looked at her.

"Go on," said Amara. "I didn't hear all of it."

Harrold said it again. He said it, if anything, more clearly the second time, every word, looking at his wife; and Corbin, at his right hand, put his face down into the stewards' reports and his shoulders shook for some time, and Saban in the doorway found that he had to look out of the window.

"Thank you," said Amara. She turned a page. "I wanted to be sure."



Corbin was good at the stewards' reports. That was the thing nobody had expected, and Saban had come to expect it now, and still didn't entirely like it.

He was better at them than Harrold. Harrold read a report as a soldier reads a dispatch: what's happened, who's hurt, what do I need to send. Corbin read it like a ledger. He read Paxton's from Milford and Bryda's from the yard and Aster's from Grayrock with a pencil, and he found the thing on page four that didn't match the thing on page one, and he wrote a note in the margin in a small, correct hand, and sent it back, and the steward wrote back, and the thing got fixed before anybody had to ride anywhere. He'd been in the chair since the fleet went. It was his chair. Emeric had given it him at the long table the week before they sailed, in front of everybody, without a word of explanation, and Corbin had taken it the same way.

And at night he learned the word book. Saban knew because Saban knew where the lamps were lit in the castle after midnight, and one of them was Corbin's, in the little room off the Board room, with Ferra's copy of Ilenne's words open on the table in front of him and a sheet beside it where he wrote them out. Over and over. Columns. He'd got as far as the colours.

Saban had been in the Board room doorway on the Tuesday morning, the twelfth, when Corbin asked.

It was after the reports. Amara had gone down to the line room. The stewards' sheets had gone back out with their margins. And Corbin had put his pencil down, and squared the pile, as he squared everything, and said to his father without looking at him:

"Why do you watch me?"

Harrold didn't answer at once.

"When you think I'm not looking," said Corbin. He said it quite evenly, to the pile of papers. "You watch me. At the table. In the yard. Last week at the dam, when I was talking to Tess. I'm not complaining. I'd like to know."

Harrold sat back in the chair at the head of the table. Saban watched him from the door and saw nothing in his face at all. That was Harrold's whole skill. He'd kept things behind his face all his life, and he kept this one.

"It's what fathers do," said Harrold.

Corbin looked up at him then. He looked for a while. Then he nodded, and picked up his pencil again, and let it go.

Saban let it go too. He knew what Harrold was watching for, because he was one of the very few people alive who did, and he knew it was nothing Corbin had done, or would; and he knew that the king had made Harrold a promise about it that Harrold would keep to the letter, which was that Corbin would be told to his face, with his father there, when the king came home, and not a day before. So Harrold watched. And Corbin had noticed. And Saban, who watched people for a living, stood in the doorway and thought that the boy had got very good at noticing, and that it was going to make the telling worse, not better.



Declan came for the stopwatch that night.

Saban had the only good one in the castle, a heavy silver thing he used for timing the relays' beats when he wanted to know whether a beat was late or only felt late, and he'd never lent it to anybody. Declan asked for it at his office door with his cap under his arm and said what it was for, which was the home company, some time after midnight; and Saban gave it to him, and then, because he wanted to know what a man like Declan wanted with a stopwatch at one in the morning, got his coat and went too.

The home company slept in the long barrack at the base, under the hill, twenty to a room, and nobody had told them. Declan sat on the bench by the yard's bell post in the dark with the watch in his hand and Osric beside him with his arms folded, and at nineteen minutes to two he nodded, and Osric pulled the bell.

Saban had thought he knew what knights looked like coming out of bed. They came out of the barrack door in their shirts with their suits in their arms, and the suits went on in the yard, on the run, by feel, every buckle in the dark; and then they went up, one and three and ten together, into the black over the yard, and made their ring at two hundred feet, and held it.

"Two minutes and ten," said Declan, and showed Osric the watch. "In April it was four."

"In April it was the middle of the afternoon."

"It'll be the middle of the night."

Osric didn't argue with that. He went to the bell post, where something hung off the rope a foot above the handle on a loop of twine, and Saban saw that it was a boot: an old riding boot, cracked across the instep.

"Last man up fetches it down," said Osric, "and wears it to breakfast." He squinted at the ring. "That's the Wayford lad. Again."

"Whose boot is it?" said Saban.

"Mine," said Declan. "I threw it at him on the road south, the first week. He's never given it back."

"You missed," said Osric.

The ring came down. Declan wrote the time in a small book in a small square hand, and a man's name under it and the word knee, and nothing else, and gave Saban back his watch, and said he'd want it again on Friday at an hour he wasn't going to say.

On the walk back up to the castle Saban asked him how he'd come to think of it. Declan said that in April he'd watched the Church's hull sit over the market for a full minute before the ring was round it, and a minute was a long time. Then, because Saban didn't say anything, he said he'd spent his evenings since March out along the gorge with Tess's level and staff, running the reservoir's flood line on the ridges above the river for the dam, and that walking every yard of a place made a man think about who'd want to stand where. He said it to the road in front of him, as if it were the weather.

Saban put the drill into the night's held traffic for the K Amara, under Ward's name, because Declan's own slip would give the time and say nothing about whose idea it had been. He didn't hear what Ward made of it.



Call day was the Wednesday, the thirteenth, and Saban went down to the line room at six in the evening because the harbour set's slot was the first of the night and he wanted it over.

The line room was in the old wing under the counting house, a long low room that had been a wine cellar, with Ambrose's switchboard down one wall in a great frame of brass and black wood and hundreds of little doors and plugs and lamps, and the two girls who ran it on the night shift in their chairs in front of it with their headsets on, and at the back a table with the fleet's call list pinned over it. Live calls to the fleet were rationed. They had to be. Every one went out through the relays, a voice going from drum to drum across four hundred miles of sky and more, and the chain could carry only so many before it choked; so there was a call day, and a list, and an order, and a girl with a clock who cut you off at the minute whoever you were. The list had been made by Ferra before she left. Nobody had dared change it.

Saban's was first. He talked to the K Discovery's set for three minutes about the lighthouse's lamp and the new harbour watch, which was all he had to say, and the fleet's operator answered in Sorrel's flat voice, and that was that; and he took the headset off and gave it back, and then he didn't leave.

He told himself it was because he wanted to see the second listener's log, if Orla's report had come. It hadn't. He stayed anyway. He'd heard about call days from Ambrose and he wanted to watch one.

The second slot on the list was Harrold's, at a quarter past. Harrold was on the stair already; Saban could hear him. And before Harrold had reached the door, a lamp came up on the board that wasn't on the list at all, and the girl nearest it sighed.

It was a long sigh. It was the sigh, Saban thought afterwards, of a young woman who'd been running a switchboard since the fleet went and had already learned that there were some lamps she was going to see a great deal of.

"Works," she said, to the other girl. "Marked urgent."

"Again?"

"Technical and urgent." The girl put a plug in. "It's her. It's always her."

She put Tess through to the fleet ahead of the regent of Randillia, because the rule was that technical and urgent went first, and Tess had read the rule.

Saban heard Tess's half. That was all anybody in the line room heard; the fleet's side came only through the headset. He stood by the table at the back with his hands in his pockets and listened to her flat voice coming down the Works line onto the board and out, and he didn't understand one word in three, and he didn't need to.

It was glass. She'd found something in the Forge books, the ones she had the key to, about a way of cooling a thing so it didn't hold a strain in it; and she'd been stuck on it for a week; and she'd solved it. That was the point of the call. Saban could hear it in her voice. She wasn't asking him anything. She was telling him how she'd done it, step by step, with numbers, as fast as she could talk, because the girl with the clock was watching her minute.

"...and then I turned it every four hours instead of every two, and it came out clean. Clean. No stars in it at all. Bram's going to be furious." A pause. Something from the other end. "No. I didn't need you. I wanted you to know I didn't need you." Another pause. "Yes. Yes, I know. I'll sleep."

The girl with the clock held up one finger.

"What?" said Tess. Then, slower, listening: "Lift it how? All of it?" A long pause. "How heavy is it?" Another. "I'll need its weight, then. And its plate. What its plate is, how thick. And where its ribs are. I can't put anything on it if I don't know where the ribs are." Saban heard her pencil going. "Twelve, then," said Tess. "And they'll want charging. No, I don't know yet. I'll know when I've drawn them. Send me the ribs."

The girl with the clock pulled the plug.

Saban didn't ask what was twelve. He didn't ask what wanted charging, or whose ribs. He wrote Tess: twelve, charged; the king wants something lifted in his book, because he wrote everything, and then he stood by the table and waited for the regent, and the regent came in and sat down in the chair in front of the board without a word and put the headset on as a man puts on a helmet he dislikes.

Harrold's report was flat and official and took exactly the three minutes on the list, and Saban, hearing only his half of it, thought it the most interesting three minutes he'd spent all week.

He gave it as a regent gives a report to a king. The holdings: Milford's spring sowing done, Grayrock's yards up a tenth on the quarter, Wayford quiet. The quay: the first Velmoor cargo landed on the Sunday, weighed, paid for before loading, the ship loading still. One exception made, in writing, for cause. Saban's objection minuted. Harrold said it in that order and that voice, as if it were a line in a list like the others.

Then Harrold said: "And Corbin settled a quarrel at Milford. Between two of the villages, over the water on the upper meadows. It had been going since your grandfather's day. He wrote to the reeves and they came to the hall and he sat them at a table. Nobody had to ride out."

That was all he said about it. He didn't say it was well done. He didn't say anything about it at all. He said it as he'd said the yards' figures, and that was how Saban knew it was true, and knew that Harrold was prouder of it than of anything else in the report, and knew that the king, at the other end, four hundred miles and more off under the sea's rim, knew it too.

Then there was a question. Saban didn't hear it. He heard the silence where it was, in the headset, and he heard Harrold not answer it.

It was a pause of perhaps two seconds. Harrold sat in the chair in front of the board with the headset on and his hands on his knees and his face doing nothing, and didn't answer, for two seconds, which for Harrold was a very long time. Saban watched the back of his head and knew, as surely as if he'd heard it, what he'd been asked. He'd been asked it before, every time, in every report that had come back from the fleet by paper since April, at the bottom, in the king's own hand. Sideways. Never the thing itself, because the thing itself couldn't go over a line.

"Holding up," said Harrold at last. "Well. He's holding up well."

He said it in exactly the voice of the yards' figures. And it cut both ways, and Harrold meant it to, and Saban thought that the king at the other end would lie awake on it tonight under the sea, and that Harrold knew that too, and had said it anyway, because it was true.

The girl with the clock held up her finger. Harrold took the headset off and gave it back to her and stood up.

"Thank you," he said to the girl.

He went out. He didn't look at Saban on the way.



The rest of the week went into Saban's book in a single page, the way most weeks did.

On the Thursday morning he walked up to the dam, because the first stage was closing its gates and he wanted to see a thing that size do something. He went up the river road in the dark with his coat buttoned and came out on the hill above the gorge at first light, and there it was across the valley, grey and new and enormous, the river shut behind it for the first time since the world was made; and the water beginning, very slowly, to stand up against it. Tess was on the crest. She walked the whole length of it on her own feet, from the north end to the south and back, with her stick on its hook at the Works and her hands in her coat pockets and Osric a yard behind her not touching her, and she stopped at every gate and looked down at it and walked on. When she came off the south end she saw Saban on the hill and waved her pencil at him, once, and went down to the plant house at the dam's foot, where they were taking in the liquid mana store that afternoon in its sealed casks; and Saban wrote down the time she'd taken, because he liked to know such things, and didn't tell her.

On the Friday Ambrose's report from the line room said the first run of the fibre had reached the Answer Towers and come back clean. Amara had sat at the end of it in the line room with her hand flat on the junction box, which was how she listened to anything now that had a voice in it, and called it good. On the Saturday Rowena came on the field set from Emberfell for one minute, to say that if the Velmoor men were the ones she thought they were, they'd count the spoons, and Kaldo had better count its own first; and rang off before anyone could ask her how she knew. And Declan had asked, and Harrold had let him, to have the home knights drill the wall line by day, every day, the whole circuit, in working dress, so that anyone on the river road could see them do it. Saban wrote that one down with a small mark beside it. A city with its king away was a city somebody would try. Declan knew it. It was the only thing he'd ever said to Saban that Saban hadn't thought of first.



Orla's report came up from the Well on the Wednesday after, the twentieth, as it came every week: two sheets in her small hard print, the relays' high end, the drum checks, the radiosondes' heights, and the home net's traffic counted by the hour. Saban read it at the long table because he read everything.

He read it twice. Then he went down to the line room and asked Ambrose for the log sheet she'd copied with it, and Ambrose found it for him, and he read that three times.

It was a column of times. Night times: from midnight to four, three nights running, the fifteenth, the sixteenth and the seventeenth. Beside each time, in Orla's print, the same two words, over and over, down the whole column.

Carrier. East.

And beside each of those, the time of the nearest heartbeat on the chain.

Saban sat down on the line room's back table with the sheet in his hand.

A heartbeat went out of each drum every quarter of an hour or so, at a time that wandered, so that nobody listening could set a clock by it. Emeric had built them that way on purpose. Saban had said what he thought of it in September: that a heartbeat was a lighthouse, and anybody with a set could see it. The king had built the net anyway with his eyes open, because a net that didn't speak couldn't be heard by its friends.

And somebody in the east had heard it. Somebody with a set, a faint one, far off, low on the band, had sat there three nights running and keyed a carrier, not a message, just a carrier, a blank tone, every time a heartbeat went out. Every time. A breath after it. However the time wandered. Every one, for three nights, from midnight to four.

And then, on the fourth night, nothing.

It wasn't a message. There was nothing in it. It was somebody setting a clock: listening to the beat, and answering it, again and again, until they were sure they had it, and then stopping, because they had it.

Saban went back up the hill and wrote it in his book. Then he went and found Harrold, in the Board room, alone, at the head of the long table with the lamp lit, and gave him the sheet.

Harrold read it.

"Is it a breach?"

"No." Saban had thought about that all the way up the hill. "It isn't anything. The heartbeats go out to anyone. That's what they're for. Anybody can listen. Listening doesn't break anything and it doesn't prove anything. Whoever it is hasn't read a word. They can't. It's in Ferra's cipher." He paused. "They've learned the beat. That's all."

"That's all," Harrold repeated.

"It changes nothing," said Saban. "Until it does."

Harrold looked at the sheet for a long time in the lamplight. He didn't ask who, in the east. He didn't need to. Neither of them said it.

"What do you want done?"

"Nothing," said Saban. "I want Orla to keep logging. Every night, every carrier, every time. And I want Ambrose to change nothing. Not the interval, not the power, not the cipher. Nothing at all." He took the sheet back. "If we change anything, they'll know they were heard."

Harrold nodded slowly.

"Then don't," he said.

Saban went to the door. He stopped in it, because that was where he always stopped.

"They'll be listening tonight," he said. "Same as us."

"I know," said Harrold, and turned the lamp down.
