Book 2, Batch 5: The Door

Chapters 36–43 | Friday 3 July – Friday 17 July, Year 17

Part Two of Book 2 opens as the fleet leaves Aumere. Ilenne is queen, Zale is her consort sailing with his brother, and Kaldo has paid its bill there in full: the K Amara barred from Aumere's water, the breach entered under Emeric's name, Ferra and Imani judged for their shots. The charge that humans broke the old promise, the Leaving Terms, has already flown south ahead of them on the winged couriers. At Ilenne's advice the fleet is heading for the orcs' treaty-house at the mouth of the Sun River: "Go to the orcs' door before anyone else's. They'll count you coldly. Everyone listens to who the orcs count."

At Aumere, Kaldo was a guest without jurisdiction. Here it is a guest under a law built to keep strangers apart, a law older, better and stricter than anything Kaldo carries. The orc coast treaty-house is neutral ground. Nobody takes sides, nobody casts, nobody carries arms past the boundary stones, and everyone, even a gold-eyed king, is counted the same at the door. Each chapter tests whether Kaldo will keep a law that costs it something when breaking it would be faster.

Two kinds of copy run through the stop. A bridge between two towns is governed by two treaty originals, both truly sealed, so no amount of checking the paper can pick a winner. Into that comes a forged letter that turns the families of the orcs' old door-wardens against the fleet, and that one really is a forgery. The lawful way also has a price: Ward chooses the door's law over a fast rescue, and a werewolf boy dies past the boundary stones a few hours before the writ that would have let him go is sealed. The stop ends with the Guest Fire, a cooking contest the night before the bridge's sitting, which gives two feuding towns their first shared measure in twenty-two years.

Chapter 36: The Door

From Friday 3 to Thursday 9 July the fleet makes the leg south to the orc coast, roughly 420 miles in low hops with long set-downs, charging from the thick air. On Sunday they make one short climb to lay a relay, with Sorrel and a dragon watch at the rails. Nothing comes, though Sorrel reports a bird too big, far off, flying south ahead of them. Ten relays are left in the hold.

On the leg Ferra writes down the reasons for this stop, because the Assembly at home will ask. First, interpreters: Ilenne has stayed behind, and past Aumere nobody aboard speaks anything but merfolk speech and a word book, while the orcs' treaty-houses train and hire out interpreters in every tongue past the wall. Second, the charge: orc law made the Leaving Terms binding, so every orc door keeps a register line for them in its roll, the first place Kaldo can read in writing what it is accused of. Third, a door to be counted at: a treaty-house is neutral ground where any people may ask to be received, and the world listens to whom the orcs count, whereas landing uninvited on someone's beach is exactly what the charge says Kaldo does. Fourth, food and a fallback: the valleys sell grain at the river mouth, and the relief pair coming from home needs a fallback berth. Fifth, the road on: the house's letter is how a stranger reaches the dwarves' gate. Ferra also closes a line in her ledger that was opened before the voyage: the hope that the fleet might slip in unnoticed, taken for the humans who long ago left with the old peoples. At Aumere it never applied, since they were known from the first hour and Ilenne vouched for them.

On the K Amara's deck that Friday afternoon, Emeric and Ferra write the First Voyage Charter out of what Aumere taught them. Rescue unconditionally. No strategic transfer by field decision. Anything permanent needs the host's consent, a local maintainer and a way out, on the model of the Aumere runway's terms. Costs are published and receivers may enter their own records. No side is taken in a host's domestic dispute for gain. Things are named honestly. Imani's limits on disease, animals and plants hold. The host can refuse or end any project. The charter also sets what follows a breach, who can stop a project (Imani for health, Ward for safety, the host always, and a guest for their own craft), who audits Emeric (not Emeric himself, but Ferra's dissent column, which he cannot delete, and the Assembly on return), safe refusal through Imani's office or a ship's master, and an emergency exception that ends when the danger does. Its first entry is the breach at Aumere, under his own name. Ward reads it, signs as witness, and says it should have been written in April. Emeric agrees. It does not settle Ward's older objection to Emeric's risks, and neither of them pretends it does. The relief rendezvous with hulls five and six is confirmed by relay: 1 to 10 October off the Sabir coast, with the orc river mouth on 20 October as the fallback, and the two badly burned from the dragon fight booked home on it.

Imani reads the charter she has just signed against her own log. No guest casts on door ground, and the charter wants a host's leave for anything done on a host's ground, so Emeric's dragon-form sessions stop at the sea mark. She writes the reason under "session two" and closes the page. He argues for one more at sea before they arrive, and loses. Meanwhile, Emeric does not want to describe buildings to Saban and his father over a hissing line; he wants them to see them. He asks Dagny for a way to send a drawing by relay, and over four days in hull four's workshop she builds what he sketches in five minutes: a facsimile, which the crews call the picture wire. A drum turns the drawing beneath a pinhole of light, a light meter behind the pinhole turns brightness into a tone, and the tone travels down the relays like a voice. At home Tess will build the receiver from Dagny's figures: a matching drum with a stylus on damp treated paper that darkens where current flows, both drums kept in step by tuning-fork clocks. Ferra logs the relay hours it will cost.

One night on a flat sea, with the hulls set down, everyone who can get across gathers on Zale's deck. Zale lies on a coil of rope with the squeezebox because Imani is watching. Arthur plays his guitar unplugged, Mira the bass, Bren the cask, Finn the concertina. Since the set list is Ferra's, so is the name: after an hour's argument they take what the Rim's children called the concert, put into Kaldo words by Neris: Dry Thunder, thunder you hear in the air and not under the water. Mira votes for it because it sounds like a weapon. Ferra writes it at the top of the list and underlines it once.

On Tuesday the fleet sets down at the house's sea mark, a lantern buoy, and goes no farther. A door is asked, not entered. Ferra sends the request by the house's own boat, and they wait a night. On Wednesday a warden comes out, cold and correct. The winged couriers came in May with the Rim's charge, and again in June with garbled news from Aumere: the land king's brother took a merfolk lord's head in the queen's hall. The house hosts them because that is what doors are for, and it says so. The terms of the door are read out: neutral ground, no casting by guests, every guest's arms declared and tallied, firearms kept in the hulls and the camp's locked armoury, and nothing carried past the boundary stones but a working knife. A pilot leads them in along the causeway, past the door statues facing outward, to the land gate. The wardstone there takes the signature of everyone who passes, and Ferra watches it take the king's and wants one so badly she has to look elsewhere. She enters Kaldo in the roll under two seals, the voyage's and the Crown's, and tells nobody why.

The first warden is old and broad, planted in her own doorway. Emeric says the hard sentence once, standing level with her: "You know what my people did here. So do we. I'm not here to tell you it was someone else." She answers that a door does not ask what a guest's grandfathers did; it counts the guest. Then she counts him. Kettrin, the house's clerk, reads Kaldo the couriers' letter as the house received it, because a guest has the right to hear what has been said about it at the door. Ferra asks to see the register line for the Leaving Terms. The years do not match. She writes down both and says nothing yet. That evening Kettrin lays out the house's business, the bridge, in an accounts voice Ferra recognises from her own mouth: a legal failure and an accounting failure at once.

On Thursday, from Ward's side, Kettrin takes the Crown's party up the river road about eight miles to the bridge, the first crossing of the Sun River, stone piers carrying an iron deck. Hasker, on the south bank, owes stone and will not wait for the sitting, so its stone wagon and six draught oxen are crossing on the emergency repair under toll. The third pier settles under the load. The deck tilts, the oxen stampede both ways along the iron, and a drover's son goes down under the wagon's rear wheel. Ward is there first, down the bank faster than anyone has seen a man run. The knights lock their magnetic boots to the iron deck and hold the wagon's traces while the boy is pulled out, until the traces part and the wagon and its stone go into the river. The boy's leg is broken, and Imani sets it on the bank with the drover's leave, explaining each step.

When the traces part, one ox is left loose on the tilted deck between the knights and the boy, bellowing and swinging. Emeric puts a hand on her shoulder the way he would at home, to calm and read her, because there is a boy under a wheel and no time to think about whose animal she is. Her mind shuts him out like a door slammed on his fingers, and she swings her head at him. Hasker's drover knocks his hand away. On this coast you ask a beast's drover first, then the beast, and you do not go into one that has not let you in. Then the drover talks the ox off the deck with her voice and a hand on the dewlap, having raised the team from calves. Ward sees the king step back with his hands open and say nothing. Hasker's crew rounds up the rest of the team from the shallows themselves and will not take Kaldo's help with the animals that week.

The bridge is shut to everything. Mek's caravan is stranded on the north bank with a medicine shipment spoiling in the heat. Varrow, on the north bank, says the pier is Hasker's to mend. Hasker says the river took Varrow's bank this spring. Both are quoting their own originals, and both are right.

Chapter 37: Two Originals

On Friday 10 July, in the record room with Kettrin and both towns' clerks, Emeric asks to compare the two treaty copies the way he would compare any disputed document: ink, hand, witness lists. Kettrin shows him why that is wrong here. The paper is only a copy. The treaty itself is the seal-clay impression and the signatures taken at the wardstone door. Hasker's copy is the older. Varrow's was written out after a fire destroyed Varrow's archive seventy years ago, and the surviving signatories came back to this door and sealed it themselves. In the copying, a marginal note on seasonal maintenance was lost by accident: whichever bank the river eats that season, that town repairs that season, and the other pays half at the next toll. Both clays are true, so provenance cannot pick a winner. There really are two originals. Emeric says plainly that he was wrong and asks what would settle it. Kettrin answers: agreement. Engineering cannot settle law.

The house hires Kaldo as a contractor to build a temporary crossing, a timber trestle on screw piles beside the broken pier, for people, pack beasts and handcarts but not wagons, explicitly without prejudice to either town's claim and paid from the house's bridge fund. The orcs' bridge engineer will not let anyone near a pier without a riverbed survey, so Bram starts one, and he and Dagny go upriver with a working party. On orc roads, orc measures: everything Kaldo builds there is made to the standard bridgeiron gauge and the toll houses' certified weights, and Ferra writes a conversion table that goes into the gift ledger. Ferra also reads the house's road law and finds the mercy line, which lets medicine and the sick cross by any means. At noon flight knights carry Mek and his crates over the river. Half the medicine is spoiled. He thanks nobody and says so, because he had hoped for a bridge. Kettrin agrees to let Kaldo trial a press under the house's seal, and Emeric, privately pleased with himself, thinks the word lithograph while Dagny and Neris do the work.

The market flats sell the valleys' goods: smokeroot, bellfruit, grain, hides and river fish. Varn, Hollis's seed hand from Milford and the fleet's only plant man, buys paddy rice seed from a valley farmer for Milford's wet bottoms, under Imani's quarantine. Across a stall, a child recites the gold-eyes warning at Emeric, the way children recite things they do not understand to strangers they have been warned about. Its sense is: when the eyes in the sky are gold, shut the door and hold it. He listens to the end. Armand writes the words down.

By afternoon the market has a story from the hills: the land king burns the dead of the door-wardens for oil. The first warden asks Emeric to his face whether Kaldo's works dig on the Isle. He tells her the truth. Kaldo has run oil and shard works over the old city there, openly, since last year, and he does not know where the doors were. It is the honest answer, it feeds the grievance, and he knows both as he says it.

In the guest rows is Grau, a big, quiet werewolf with a physician's hands and a bag he never puts down. He has come down the pass with Coldspring's charter in an oilskin, to enter it at the house so that Gorm cannot claim the pass for the Pelt-Sworn. Inside the boundary stones he is safe. Outside, four Pelt-Sworn wait in the hills, and he has known their smell since Coldspring. To complete the charter he must fetch its living witness, an old Coldspring survivor in a hill hamlet a day's walk up. Ward offers his knights as escort, and the wardens refuse arms off neutral ground and explain their own way instead: the guest-writ. A guest who must cross ground where someone hunts them goes under the house's writ with the house's wardens, sealed by the first warden only after notice is given to the hunting party at the boundary. Ward helps set it up without running it: the notice, the list, the route. Notice is set for dawn on Monday. Emeric meets Grau's eyes once across the row and takes the measure of a man who has buried people he could not save.

Privately, Emeric has felt something since Wednesday: in the hills above the camp, a ring of wolf signatures with one small humanoid signature inside it, a child by its size. He tells nobody and leaves it alone while the stop's crisis runs.

The guest room on the rock is narrow stone, with one bed built for an orc, the first bed since the K Discovery's bunks big enough for three. Imani declares it a hospital cot and claims it on medical grounds. Ferra claims it on contractual ones. The night turns playful and loud until someone remembers that the walls are stone and the first warden sleeps two doors down, and then there is shushing, and then not, and the frame, built for weight rather than this, complains. He sleeps in the middle and is tired in the morning. They are not.

On Saturday evening the council call comes through the camp's field set, Corbin and Harrold at home, Emeric, Ferra and Ward on the fleet's end. Corbin says Cinderhale's armed coats are an army. Ferra defends honest numbers: some new watches really are catching bandits, and two alarming reports were false. Ward says the Crestless would be the right people to watch them, and the Crestless are on the voyage. Emeric orders every private watch registered with Keller within thirty days, an audit of the three biggest, and the Private Arms Act on an accelerated schedule. He refuses to suspend the levy right by decree and refuses to recall the Crestless. It is a defensible decision, and the wrong one. Harrold adds one line at the end about the pulpits' new word, and Saban's rule holds. Ferra watches Corbin be right and be overruled, then watches Emeric ask Harrold "How's he holding up?" after Corbin has rung off, and knows why, and writes nothing.

Chapter 38: The Lanterns Go Out

On Sunday 12 July at dusk, the lanterns on the boundary stones go out one by one, from the hills end toward the river. The camp sees it and so does the house. Nobody knows what it means: an evacuation, an attack, or a trap to make Kaldo fire on the wrong orcs. A warden runner brings the house's call. Under siege, the first warden may call any lodged party's escort to the door's defence for the night, under a warden's command, on the house's ground only. Ward will not commit blind. Torben's men and the wardens are both orcs, both in road coats, both carrying wards. From the rock, Emeric gives Ward bodies and positions over the field set and says plainly that he cannot read loyalties. The first warden has asked him, as the Crown's guest, not to cast and not to leave the house that night, because if the gold-eyed king walks out of the door into the hardliners, the war they came to prevent begins. He gives his word.

Mira goes out with a knife and no rifle, in the stealth suit with the night glass down and the heat layer on. The dark is not the problem. The flats come through grey and the bodies on them warm. The problem is that every warm body is an orc in a road coat with a ward, and the glass cannot read loyalty any more than Emeric's sense can. Putting out the lanterns is a custom, not a tactic against Kaldo's eyes: a door with dark stones is a shut door. Grau insists on coming. He knows the wardens of this door by smell after a week in the guest rows, and he knows the four in the hills from Coldspring. Ward lets him, because he is the only one who can tell orc from orc in the dark. They meet two of the house's outer scouts who disagree, one blaming Varrow's toll men and the other werewolves from the pass. Grau says neither, and Mira trusts the nose.

At the third stone, the heat layer finds a bright smear on the grey: the lantern keepers. One is bound and gagged. The other, who fought for his lantern, is cut deep across the shoulder with a broken collarbone. He heard Torben read a letter aloud to his men before they put the lights out, a letter about the burning. Mira learns the shape of it. The attackers are not all orcs, and not all the door families: they are about sixty hardliners from the hill hamlets under Torben, holding the causeway head with boats across the water stair, while the wardens outside the house are few, scattered and loyal. The friendly-fire disaster that would have followed if Ward had sent knights at "orcs in road coats" never happens. On the hill wind Grau says the four Pelt-Sworn are close, higher up, watching the dark flats. He says nothing about the wolf pack he smells farther up, or what is with it, and Mira does not ask.

Mira's way back to the camp is open and fast. The cut keeper cannot walk it, and he is the only witness to the letter. She gives up the clean exit, carries him the long way through the storm culvert under the guest rows, and comes in with him across her shoulders, an hour later than Ward wanted and with everything he needed. Ward commits the Crestless and about thirty knights under the warden who meets them at the causeway's foot, their arms drawn from the camp armoury on that warden's tally.

Chapter 39: A Door Held From Both Sides

Near midnight the Crestless go at the causeway at a run in their suits, Arthur first. Torben's men wear shard charms, since the door families have made door wards for two thousand years, and they have set a ward screen across the causeway, a shard field that nets any fast body by its wake. Arthur is caught in it mid-stride, held like a fly in amber. Ball rounds from behind him stop in the air at Torben's front rank and drop. A warden walks out to Arthur at a stroll, takes him by the collar and walks him back. Then she says it once: a ward lets a stranger in at a walk. So they walk. With knives, hands, the knights' grappling and stun rounds, whose current passes through a charm where a ball is caught, they retake the causeway at a walking pace. It is the strangest fight any of them has been in.

Inside, about eighty civilians cannot leave: the caravan, both towns' delegations, market folk caught at dusk, and Grau's party. Torben's boatmen have planted themselves on the water stair's landing, and a planted orc in a doorway cannot be moved; it is the stance of every door statue. A frontal push would bring the house's own defences down on the people behind them. Arthur and Bren go through the storm culvert under the warehouses to come up behind the landing. It is too tight for the suit's pack, so Arthur sheds it and goes on in his undersuit, and one knight's forearm breaks against the culvert's mouth. Inside the house an old warden relights one lantern on the water-stair wall. Its light shows the civilians sheltering behind the shutters: the caravan's children, both towns' delegations, Tahl the caravan cook holding a cleaver. Some of Torben's boatmen see the children and stop pushing. One hardliner on the landing, trapped between Arthur and the stair, lays down his long knife and starts handing children into Zale's boats. Ward has to decide whether to trust him, and does.

Ward runs the withdrawal in the dark along the stair, the civilians going down into Zale's boats and the K Endeavor's launch. One hardliner will not stop. Ennick, Torben's son, goes at a Varrow carter in the line with a long knife, and Ward kills him with his hands. It is fast. Ward does not look away from it, and he is not right for the rest of the night. Inside the house Emeric does nothing but read bodies and say where they are, the hardest night's work he has done in months. Imani and Bettany tend the wounded in the court, and Ferra stays with Kettrin and the roll.

Out on the flats, during the evacuation from the guest rows to the causeway in the dark, Grau's sixteen-year-old apprentice Lisk carries his master's bag a few yards past a boundary stone nobody can see, and the four Pelt-Sworn take him. Emeric feels five signatures go north fast and tells Ward. Grau smells it. Ward knows the direction. He is the fastest thing on the flats and could go unarmed, but he is holding the civilians' line at the water stair, and the first warden asks him not to take the house's escort past the stones before the writ. He stays and sends for the writ.

At dawn the first warden demands that Torben show his grievance at the door, as door law requires. Under truce on the causeway, Kettrin reads Torben's letter. It carries a road guild wagon's tally mark, not the winged couriers' hand-off mark. The house's own copy of the May letter is read from the wall, and it says nothing about burning. The rest of the forged letter is the true charge word for word, and the part about the oil is true, which is why the door families believed it. Torben sees the difference. He stands his men down, but keeps the true half: "Answer the rest."

The writ is sealed at first light, the day's notice waived because the hunters have taken a guest, which is an act against the door. Wardens and knights go up the valley under it. They find Lisk in a stream bed below the pass. He has been dead for hours.

The door families carry their own stunned and bruised to their own convoy healer. Imani offers once and is refused. She says, "Then show me how you'd do it. I'll hold the lamp," and does. The house rules Ward's killing of Ennick lawful defence on its ground and enters it in its roll, and Ferra enters the finding in her ledger as the second host entry. She also enters the night under the charter's first rule, rescue unconditionally, as a question rather than a breach, with Ward's reasons in his own words: no known position until the hunters moved, the civilians' line, and the house's law and its waiver at dawn. Ward signs under it. Emeric kept his word to the first warden and never left the house. Neither man defends the night at length, and neither says he would do it differently.

Chapter 40: The Bout

On Tuesday 14 July the wagon mark leads to Isbrand, the river lenders' guild's factor at Varrow, who holds the bridge's toll farm. A neutral bridge authority with open accounts, measured by Kaldo's surveyors and printed by Kaldo's press, would have ended his toll farm and put the guild's interest on the table. He forged the letter so the door families would demand the house shut its door to the humans and the sitting be put off. He did not want a siege. The house's market court strikes him from the guild, forfeits his toll farm to the bridge fund, the same fund that pays for the temporary crossing, and orders restitution to Ennick's family and the wounded lantern keeper. Kaldo stands at the back and says nothing. Isbrand leaves the river.

Then comes the challenge. By door custom, a guest whose people have shed blood on the house's ground answers to the house's own hands, bare, so the door can say the guest's strength was measured and the guest submitted to its law. It is not vengeance, but the warden who steps forward, Petrok, is Ennick's cousin and grieving anyway. In the house's yard, inside a chalk square, with the wardens and both towns watching and Kaldo along one wall, there is no magic and no kit. Ward has the orcs' own build, tall, strong and heavy, plus a seed eater's body, which the custom does not bar. Petrok plants, and Ward cannot move him and stops trying. It goes long. Ward wins, narrowly and hurt, with two cracked ribs and a split lip, while Petrok's hand breaks on Ward's head. Ward helps him up, and the yard notes that he does it at a walk.

Afterward the first warden says the door owes Kaldo nothing for the siege night and Kaldo owes the door nothing for Ennick, and the roll records both. The house now treats Kaldo as a complicated ally, not its saviour. Imani mends Ward's ribs. He keeps the lip and tells Imani it is theirs to see for a few days. That evening Petrok comes to Imani on his own, holding his hand. She explains every step before she touches it, and he watches every step.

The eldest of the door families, Brega, mother to Torben and grandmother to Petrok, Ledra and Ennick, sends for Ward, not the king. At her fire on the flats, with Torben's place empty, she asks about the doors. The orcs died holding the Isle's doors, and Kaldo now mines over the place. Ward tells her what he saw, once. "I stood beside six of yours at the doors we came in by. Nobody'd moved them. I didn't either." The city was swept, with no bodies. The doors her people held were the outer land doors, under the sand, and Kaldo never reached them. He cannot tell her they are gone, and he cannot tell her they are there. Brega says bitterly that the elven royals left by sea while her people held the doors for them. Ward does not answer, because it is not his to answer. She asks to be taken to see. Ward says he will carry it to his king tonight, that the king will say yes, and that he will be there when they go if he is allowed.

Chapter 41: Item One

On Wednesday 15 July, at Brega's fire with Ward beside him, Emeric makes a narrow offer. Kaldo will carry whoever the door families send to the Isle on a Kaldo hull, at their choosing; the first ride it can offer is the relief pair's homeward leg in October, which can touch the river mouth on the fallback date. Who enters, and what happens to the dead, is for those with standing among the families to decide, not Kaldo. Kaldo will not promise what they will find. First, though, comes the present harm: a protective zone along the buried city's landward edge, as far as Kaldo's survey can trace where the outer land wall ran, with a margin, and every shard face and bore inside it stopped until a joint survey with the families' delegates finds the doors. Home works belong to the regent, so Emeric asks Harrold by relay that afternoon. Harrold signs that night, with the date and the reason, and sends one line back: it stops the newest bore, about a tenth of the Isle's oil, in the summer he asked the works for more. The relief run sails anyway. Emeric tells Brega the figure. She takes the offer and the zone. She does not thank him, and she does not say she believes Kaldo. She believes Ward. Torben refuses to be in the room. Kaldo also refuses something: it will not be made judge of the door families' quarrel with the wardens, or of the bridge.

That night on the K Discovery's deck Ferra says quietly, "Item one." He does not answer.

At dusk Grau's party carries Lisk up to the stone ring by their own custom, past the stones under the house's writ, wardens and Ward walking behind. Kaldo is asked to stand at the ring's edge, and does.

On Thursday morning the first proof sheet comes off the lithograph press. With no type for orc letters and months needed to cut a font, Dagny built a stone press instead: a flat slab of fine limestone from the house's own quarry, a scraper bar on a lever and an inking roller. An orc clerk writes the text in greasy ink on transfer paper, which is pressed onto the stone so it carries the clerk's own hand, and Neris makes the etch and the ink, which is chemistry and her strength. Ferra laid out the proof as any clerk would, with Hasker's older copy as the base text and Varrow's differences in small marginal notes. Ledra, Petrok's sister and a clerk trained in the house's interpreters' row, reads the sheet at the press table and says what it is: on a printed page the base text looks like the law and the margins look like Varrow's mistakes, and a toll house a hundred miles upriver would read it as Hasker's treaty, corrected. Printing an error makes it look permanent. Ferra sees it before Ledra finishes and tells Kettrin her tool took a side in a governance problem and made it worse. At the table, Ledra, Ferra and both towns' clerks build the fix while Emeric watches others do it: two columns of equal size, each headed with its own seal; the lost seasonal note printed in both margins as held by one original and absent from the other; and a joint appendix of disputed readings witnessed by both towns' clerks and the house. It fixes the print, not the dispute. The press sale will wait until the house has seen a joint edition survive a sitting.

In the record room the first warden asks the Crown's party whether Kaldo claims the Leaving Terms allow its coming. The register line's abstract contains one verb in the old law-hand that can be read two ways: leave be, meaning do no harm, or leave alone, meaning do not come back. The first reading would let the house find that Kaldo's coming is no breach and stop the charge at this door. Armand reads the old grammar better than anyone in the fleet, and he says he does not know. The house enters Kaldo as guests and enters no finding on the charge, so the charge stands here and runs ahead to the next door. Emeric does not argue with him. Ferra writes down what it cost. She also writes down the mismatch she noticed on arrival: the couriers' letter dates the human promise as two thousand and eleven years old this season by the merfolk stone count, but the roll's register line, converted by Kettrin's table, puts its sealing thirty-eight years later. She sets down three possible readings, a different calendar, two thousand years of copying errors, or a revision sealed again later the way Varrow's copy was, and refuses to choose among them.

Ledra signs as the fleet's interpreter, her terms read aloud by Ferra on the K Endeavor's deck: wages, freedom to refuse any mission, freedom to report home to the house, her own people's law kept, and service only as far as the next port, the dwarves' Sea Hold. Her reason is the house's autumn reckoning, when the dwarves' grain debt to the orcs falls due, which is her family's business to witness. Kettrin agrees a fallback berth at the river mouth for the relief pair on 20 October, and agrees that the door families' delegates may board there if they have chosen by then. A Varrow carter hired for the crossing privately asks Imani's office to be let off after the siege. He is, with his wages, and nobody tells the king who asked. Ferra buys about forty tonnes of wheat and barley at the door's certified weights with Kaldo iron, about a month of the fleet's bread.

On Wednesday Tess's urgent call lands in the middle of a kiss. She has marked it technical and urgent: the Great Answer Tower's turning cap. Emeric gives a wrong answer and she corrects him, while Ferra laughs into his shoulder through the whole call. Then Tess asks, not urgently, whether Neris got her letter, and says her receiving drum is built. On Thursday night the picture wire sends its first pictures home. The first is Neris's bead pattern for Tess, the colours coming through as shades with Neris's numbers in the margin. The second is Emeric's: the first sheet of the Grand Museum, the central stadium with its rising stage and the five wings in outline, sent under Saban's seal to Saban and Harrold, who knows what it is for. To everyone else in Kaldo it is a hall for the fleet's homecoming. Harrold signs the works and has ground broken by the campus in late July, the stadium and stage first.

At dusk on the river beach below the camp, Maeve and Mira, still matching under their sleeves from the surf day's stripes, have an ordinary evening. Maeve is furious she was kept aboard for the siege, since no guest may cast on door ground. Mira shows her the walk rule, and they turn it into a game, walking at each other with sticks while each tries to judge whether to strike. Maeve names the slow walk Sunday, as in a Sunday stroll, and from then on it is their word. Emeric watches from the deck with his brother's glare ready, and Imani talks him out of using it. That night the small signature on the hill comes lower, to the edge of the ferns above the camp's lights. He does not go. Not yet.

Chapter 42: Finn's Rule

On Friday 17 July, the night before the bridge's sitting, the treaty-house lights the Guest Fire. Every party with business at the sitting cooks one pot for the house, and lodged parties may enter too; a dispute eaten together settles faster, and sittings that open with a Guest Fire close in about a third of the time, which Ferra wants as a column. The rules each exist because something went wrong centuries ago. One pot per party, by its seal on the roll. The First Spoon: the cook eats the first full spoonful from her own pot in front of the house, then a warden draws a second at random, a thousand-year-old answer to poison that Imani recognises at once after Aumere. Market only: every ingredient bought at the door's market that day. Open pots: everything open to the house's eye, and any tools from outside shown at the door. And the blind pot: pots numbered and carried to the judges by wardens, judged by three wardens and one judge from each party, nobody scoring their own, cards counted in the open. The prize is honour only: the winning pot's name cut into the hearth lintel for the season, and the first toast.

The chapter is seen through Dorrit Hake, head of the galley, sixty, short and broad with forearms like a stevedore's, carrying Maren's green ledger. At first light the lantern-prawn boat lands, and every team wants its best basket. Lantern prawns glow blue-green while they live and go dark as they cook, so cooks say that when the light goes out, it is done. House rule forbids running on the quay, so the knights do an absurd speed-walk. Finn swims out to the boat before it docks and buys the basket in the water. Ferra says it is against the spirit. The wardens rule it legal, then add a line to their book: no swimming to the boats. On the house's roll it becomes Finn's Rule, and every Kaldo trick of the day becomes a new line of orc law. Ferra counts three by nightfall.

Dorrit will not have Emeric at her fire. "Kings peel." He wants to cook, and the rules allow one pot per party. Ferra reads the roll: Kaldo is lodged under two seals, the voyage's and the Crown's, the latter covering the king's own business at the house, the old promise and the bones. Kettrin grants it, sourly, and writes the day's second line: a party is its seal, not its ship. Varrow's ox-roaster asks whether the king will eat his own spoon. Emeric says yes.

The market is read twice. Dorrit buys by smell and by the vendors' faces. Emeric buys with Imani's dose register in hand, choosing only light-dose food, without saying why. Beside the bellfruit on a valley farmer's stall he finds true pumpkin seed and buys a sack for home under the seed hand's care and the quarantine stages; Kaldo learns the orcs' name for it, and Emeric calls it pumpkin. Dorrit passed her Band card, but local food is a dose, so Imani limits her to four tastes all day. She cooks by smell and through two tasters, Ferra in numbers ("salt's a seven, sour's a three") and Imani in a clinician's terms ("it coats the tongue; the finish is short"). Both keep wandering over to taste Emeric's pot, and Dorrit keeps calling them back: "You're my tasters."

Then the galley's pressure cooker breaks the open-pot rule. Imani calls it her autoclave with a handle. The wardens convene a five-minute court in the middle of the yard, and Ferra argues it under the orcs' own law: a sealed pot opened under a warden's hand is open to the house's eye at the moment that matters. Mid-argument the relief valve screams. The wardens' hands go to their batons, Mira half-draws her declared knife, and everyone ducks. Ruling: allowed, if a warden opens it. It is the day's third new law, and the wardens want to buy two, which goes into Ferra's ledger as a trade.

Five pots go on the house roll. Varrow's ox-roaster makes drover's ox over smokeroot coals with bellfruit mash, smoke on smoke. Hasker's old clay-pot cook braises ox and deepcap, the dwarves' dried cave mushroom. Tahl, the stranded caravan's Sabir cook, makes a chilled dish of glasspod jelly with numbing coldseed and fizzy storm honey, with crackle-salt flatbread. Dorrit cooks for the voyage's seal, and Emeric for the Crown's. The fire is lit, five pots are on the roll, and Grau's party sits at the edge of the yard and does not cook, grieving Lisk, killed four days earlier.

Chapter 43: The Blind Pot

With an hour to go, Hasker's clay pot cracks on the hot stones. Dorrit lends the old cook Maren's spare iron pot, the one Maren packed herself. "Maren will know." "Maren lends pots." Varrow's cooks see it.

Then Dorrit faces the salt. The market has no plain fish that day, only shellfish, and Maren's ledger says not with the shellfish, and salt at the end. Dorrit has to break Maren's page either way, so she closes the ledger and cooks her own mother's stew instead: salt at the start, her mother's way, and crackle salt at the end, which only crackles if it goes on last, so both mothers were right. No cream. Zale asks for cream, and Dorrit says Zale is wrong, as Maren's margin says. To anyone who asks about the salt, her one word is: "Both." At the First Spoon she eats a full spoonful of her own dense stew in front of the house. Imani has ruled one spoon within her mark. Dorrit's nose bleeds into her cloth. "It's the onions." Ferra: "There are no onions in it."

Emeric has cooked since his other life, alone in a small kitchen, and his sauce once lost to Maren's at South Sentinel because Harrold said it tasted like something made very carefully for somebody who was not there. Dorrit was on the island that day and heard the verdict. Now he makes a noodle broth from light-dose ingredients only: noodles cut by hand from valley wheat, deepcap soaking water for stock, roasted bellfruit, smokeroot shaved thin, soft-cooked eggs from the market's hens, and crackle salt at the end. He privately thinks ramen and never says it. A king kneading dough in a treaty-house yard draws a crowd, and Varrow's roaster heckles: soup in July. In the heat his noodles go soft and clump. Tahl shows him the desert cook's trick of plunging them into the cold spring trough so they stay firm, and in return wants the egg trick. Emeric eats his own First Spoon without fuss, and the orcs take note of a king who eats his own spoon. He never says the point of it: he has cooked the one pot in the yard that Dorrit can eat a whole bowl of.

The cards are counted in the open. Tahl's cold dish wins, because it is July, the yard is a furnace, and nobody else made anything cold. Dorrit's pot is second on both towns' cards. It was the only pot neither town could identify, so both scored it honestly, and it is the first measure Varrow and Hasker have agreed on in twenty-two years, since their common ledger failed. Emeric's pot comes fourth of five. The wardens find it fine, and Varrow's card says soup in July. Ward, judging blind and split-lipped, gives the king's broth a seven and the cook's stew a six, too much salt. Told afterward which was which, he says, "It was still too salty." Kettrin says nothing and writes a line.

Late, with the yard emptying, Emeric fills a bowl from his pot and sets it in front of Dorrit without a word. She eats all of it, her first full meal of the day. Then she says it was made for somebody who is here. He does not answer. Ferra puts it to Kettrin plainly: the blind pot is the common ledger. Surveyors drawn by lot from both towns' lists and the house's, with measurements entered before anyone knows whose they are. Kettrin replies that engineering cannot settle law. But a blind measure is not engineering, and she takes the idea to the sitting. The dispute stays open, but the method is now on the table, and it came from a cook. Ferra and Imani each claim his fourth place was the other's fault for tasting it too often.

Mira, who carried the lantern keeper in on the siege night, carries bowls to Grau's grieving party at the yard's edge, and the wardens let her without anyone asking whose she is. The moment is left to sit.

Where the Story Stands After Chapter 43

Kaldo has been counted at the orcs' door and kept its law at a price. The forged letter was read in the open, Isbrand was struck from his guild and stripped of his toll farm, and Torben stood his men down while keeping the true half of the grievance: answer the rest. Ward killed Ennick to save a carter, won the house's bout narrowly, and has a death on his hands, a boy dead under his choice, and a family that sent for him rather than the king. Lisk died in a stream bed because the writ was sealed at first light, and the charter records that night as a question for the Assembly. Emeric kept his word and never left the house, and he will remember it.

The door families have asked to see their dead on the Isle, and Kaldo has offered transport on their terms and a protective zone that Harrold signed at the cost of a tenth of the Isle's oil. The bridge's sitting opens on Saturday 18 July with a joint printed edition of both originals, a temporary crossing standing without prejudice, Bram's riverbed survey, and Ferra's blind-lot method taken from the Guest Fire. Grau's charter for the pass will be entered at the same sitting. The four Pelt-Sworn who killed Lisk are still in the hills, and Emeric has felt a small signature among the wolves above the camp since the day he landed. He has left it alone, but it is coming closer.

On the larger questions, the old promise charge has no finding at this door and runs on ahead, Armand could not say which way its key verb reads, and Ferra holds three explanations for its mismatched dates without choosing one. Ledra has joined as interpreter to the next port, the dwarves' Sea Hold. At home, Corbin was right about the armed coats and overruled, the Isle's oil is down a tenth in the summer it was most needed, and ground has been broken for a homecoming hall that Harrold and Saban know is the first piece of the Grand Museum. The fleet has its band name, Dry Thunder, and Maeve and Mira have a word of their own: Sunday.

This batch shows Kaldo keeping a stricter, older law than its own, being wrong about how it works, and letting other people supply the answers: a clerk who reads a misprint, a warden who teaches the walk, and a cook whose blind pot gives two towns their first shared measure.
