Chapter 5 - Root Causes

The room was cold again by the time he sat down. It had been cold every night since he arrived. Whatever the steward's kitchen had sent up sat at his elbow, going cold faster than he was eating it, and he didn't notice. He had a quill, ink, a blank page, and a day's worth of unsorted input finally ready to become something with a shape. The ink was terrible, and the paper was worse. He used them anyway. He started a list and worked top to bottom, the way he'd have worked a punch list on a job site or a code review pass. Root causes first, symptoms sorted underneath them instead of promoted into problems of their own.

The water went at the top. It mattered most, and it was the one he already had a plan for. Waterborne, almost certainly bacterial, and it didn't take a villain to explain it. A burial ground sitting upriver of the wells. Latrines dug too close to the bank by people who had never had a reason to map those two facts against each other. Active, ongoing, killing people right now. Everything else on the page could wait longer than this one could.

Under it, not a problem so much as a permanent condition he'd be building around: the town had lost more than half its people, first to war and then to plague. Two separate failures stacked on each other, not one. Whatever he did from here, he'd be doing it with fewer hands than the job needed, and no amount of clever engineering put that number back up.

Wexler went on the page too, and he was careful with the wording, even writing it only for himself. Disengaged, at minimum. The man hadn't ridden out to greet him. He'd corrected him out of spite rather than courtesy. He'd shown no flicker of interest in his own barony's condition through the one real conversation they'd had. All of that was consistent with plain apathy. It was also consistent with something worse, and he had no evidence for worse, so he wrote down apathy and made himself leave it there. Acting on a theory he hadn't earned was how competent people ended up as the next cautionary tale.

The fallow fields went down as an open question, not a conclusion. Could be the labor shortage explaining itself. Could be something else. The idle mill went beside it for the same reason, noticed and not understood, and he was careful not to hand the two of them a shared cause just because they happened to be broken in the same town.

Last was the castle. Rope where a chain should be, wall damage hidden instead of fixed, a steward doing the work of three men. He almost left it off. It wasn't a cause of anything; it was what every item above it looked like from the outside, and fixing those would fix this without him lifting a finger toward it. The ledgers would tell him faster. He doubted he'd get those out of the baron without a fuss.

He read back over what he had. Six problems, and exactly one of them had a next step he could take tomorrow. That was fine. That was how it always started.

The day came, and Emeric finally felt like he could do something worth doing. He'd sent the maids past the barony's edge for the water, up beyond the burial ground and the latrine trenches, to a source that ran clean of everything the settlement dumped downhill. The extra walk annoyed them. Hauling water that far when there was a river at the bottom of the slope struck them as backward in a way that had nothing to do with the distance. He was still a prince, though, and telling them it would make sense later got them moving. He wasn't going to walk into a stranger's house and ask them to boil water he hadn't been drinking himself. Everything that came up to his rooms went over a fire first, same as he'd be asking of them, and if it turned out he was wrong about any of it, he'd find out before anyone else did.

The boiling was a double measure, not a lack of faith in the source. Clean water was clean water, but he didn't know what else the ground up there might carry, and he'd rather burn extra wood than gamble on it. Overall safe was the point. He wasn't trying to prove the source was bad, he was trying to leave nothing to chance.

He was already missing plumbing. Showering in this barony was inefficient enough without turning the water supply into an experiment. He went to Bram with Rylan beside him and Ward somewhere behind, and found the smith already waiting at the cart with his arms crossed, looking like a man who'd rather be at his forge.

Rylan fell in beside the cart on foot, one hand loose near his belt out of habit rather than expectation. He didn't ask questions. He knew these roads without looking at them, and he took the shorter route past the mill without being told. The parish house smelled like tallow and old paper. The priest who met them, whose name Emeric didn't bother learning, was polite in the specific way people are polite to nobility they don't trust yet, and reluctant to hand over anything at all until Bram said something low and local that Emeric didn't catch. Whatever it was, it worked. They carried four books out between them. Rylan took the heaviest without being asked.

Back at the smithy, Emeric cleared a table and started stacking pages by year, then by month, then by something the other two couldn't see yet. Names, ages, cause where a cause had been written down, and beside every entry a location. Which well. Which street. Which row of the burial ground.

"You're not reading it," Bram said, watching him move faster than reading allowed. "You're sorting it."

"Same thing, further out." Emeric didn't look up. "One death near the north well doesn't mean anything. Six in the same month, all on the same side of it, means something."

Rylan leaned over the table and stayed quiet a while. Then he put a finger on a line near the bottom of a page from three years back.

"That's my aunt's row." Flat, no weight on it, but his hand stayed on the page a second longer than it needed to. "Same well."

Emeric marked it and kept going. He said nothing about the pause. The pattern would say it for him soon enough, and it would say it to a hundred people who'd lost someone the same way, not just to a table in a council room. By the time the candle burned low, he had the beginning of a map on cheap paper. Dots for deaths, clustered thick around the north well, thinning toward the east side of town where the water ran higher and cleaner. Not proof. A shape.

"That's it?" Bram said, looking at the scatter of ink. "That's your evidence?"

"That's the start of it." Emeric sat back. "Now I go find the households near the thick part of that map and ask them to try something different for a season. Not everyone. Enough."

He took the map to Imani before he took it anywhere else, because she was the only person in Kaldo who could tell him what wasn't on it. She looked at it a long while without touching it.

"Your dots are thin on Weir Row." She tapped the paper, three houses east of the north well. "Four there the summer before last. None of them are in the priest's book. None of them had anybody left to pay the burying."

"Names?"

She gave him eleven, across five years, from memory, in the order they'd died. Emeric wrote them all down and marked them and watched his own map get worse, which meant it was getting truer.

"It's still the ground," she said.

"It's the water sitting in the ground."

"That's the same sentence with a word moved."

"That's the whole difference." He set the quill down. "You can't move the ground. You can boil the water."

Imani didn't answer for a while.

"Five whole years I've been telling this town it's the ground." She pushed the map back across to him. "If you're wrong, you've cost them a winter of firewood. If you're right, I've cost them five years." She got the door for him. "So be sure. Then come and get me, and I'll say it standing next to you."

They went house to house with it, and doors opened that wouldn't have opened for a prince, because Imani was the one knocking. He kept it plain, no lecture. The water near the burial ground wasn't safe to drink and hadn't been for a long time. The sickness that kept coming back wasn't a curse on the town; it was that ugly little problem going unnoticed for decades. He showed them where the clean water was, said very little else, and left it there. He couldn't force anyone to change how they lived. That was how riots started, and it wasn't a road he had any interest in walking. It took two weeks to know anything for certain, and what he learned wasn't clean.

The Hallards, three doors down from the north well, switched without complaint and boiled every cup before it touched a mouth, and nobody in that house so much as coughed wrong the whole fortnight. The widow Corwin tried it four days, decided the extra walk to the clean well wasn't worth the ache in her knees, and went back to the water she'd always used. Her youngest ran a fever by the end of the week, mild and survivable, but a fever all the same. Old Pell refused outright, told Rylan to his face he'd drunk from that well sixty years and wasn't about to let a boy prince tell him different, and nothing happened to him at all, which did more damage to the case on that street than any failure could have. The Talbrooks split it down the middle, boiled for the children and didn't bother for themselves, and it was the parents who got sick.

No clean line anywhere in it. Nothing a determined skeptic couldn't put a hole through. But laid next to the dots on the ledger map, there was enough there for anyone looking carefully to see it. Boil the water, and the odds moved. They didn't move for everyone, and some who ignored every word of it walked away fine, and the odds still moved. Emeric logged each result the way he'd have logged test data that refused to behave. Not proof the method had failed. Proof it was real. Imani brought him most of it herself, house by house, in the order she'd walked them. She read the Talbrooks off her list last, the parents sick and the children not, and then she stood in Bram's back room saying nothing for a length of time Emeric had the sense not to interrupt.

"Boiled for the little ones and not themselves," she said eventually. "I've watched that house for five years. I'd have told you the parents were the strong ones."

"You'd have been right. That's what makes it evidence."

"I know what makes it evidence." She folded the list and put it away. "I'm deciding what to do about being wrong for those five years, and I'd rather not do it in front of you."

She was at the north well the next morning telling people to boil it, which was the only answer she ever gave him.

By the time Bram invited him back for a drink, weeks further on, the argument had mostly made itself. The holdouts didn't come around all at once. They came around one bad week at a time, one neighbor's fever weighed against another neighbor's dry summer, until boiling water before it touched a mouth was just what people did on that end of town. Old Pell was still drinking straight from the well the last anyone checked, daring the rot to try him again. Nobody was betting on him changing his mind. He was the exception people talked about now, though, not the rule.

The smithy was closed for the evening, so Bram brought him home instead. From the outside, the house looked like every other house on the row. The upkeep showed the moment he was through the door. Living with a blacksmith clearly had its perks. Emeric sat down in Bram's front room with a cup of something herbal going cold in his hand. Bram's daughter had poured it for him without a word and disappeared back into the house before he could thank her. He thought about the list from that first night. Six items, one of them finally showing results.

"I plan on solving this town's problems while I'm here, Bram." The look on his face did most of the work before the words caught up. "I'm going to need a lot of help. I can't do this alone. If we're getting the town out of the state it's in, we need the town on board, not watching from a distance."

"That's easier said than done." Bram sipped without looking up. "Hope's costly in this town. Nobody's willing to pay for it twice."

"The water I can handle. I think I already have. The fields are going to be a bigger problem." Emeric turned the cup a quarter turn on his knee. "I can grow that field myself. I've got enough in me to do it. But what happens the day I'm not here to do it again? Growth magic needs a steady hand behind it, and if that hand's mine, it's mine instead of everything else this barony needs me for.

"It makes no sense that the capital hasn't sent anyone down here already. Imani sent word three times the first year of the plague, through the castle, because that's the road it goes down. Nobody came. Nobody wrote back to tell her why." He let that sit and moved past it. "It's like there's no report of what's actually happening in this town. As somebody decided there wouldn't be one."

"Fixing the fields properly isn't the hard part. I know ways to get more out of a harvest than anyone here's used to seeing. Faster growth, bigger yield, ways to collect and store it that don't rot half of it before winter. All of that needs metal. And it needs a smith."

Bram's cup stopped halfway to his mouth. "Are you trying to hire me, boy?"

"Yes. You and your workers. Exclusive, long-term. The whole barony's smithing, if you'll take it."

"Tell me more of these ideas of yours, and I'll think about it."

"All right. So. This is how farming actually works."

He spent the rest of the evening explaining how a plant grew, root to leaf, in terms he hoped would land without sounding like something out of a heretic's mouth. He skipped everything he couldn't prove to a man with no way to check it. Nitrogen. Photosynthesis. The invisible machinery running underneath every green thing Bram had ever grown or sold metal to someone else for growing.

What he kept was what he could show. Rotate what you plant so the soil doesn't get bled dry of the same thing every season. Feed it what it's missing instead of guessing. Water it on a schedule instead of whenever somebody remembers. None of it needed magic to work. The magic was only going to make it work faster, and bigger, and less dependent on however many hands the war and the plague had left standing. Bram listened without interrupting, which Emeric had already learned was as close to enthusiasm as the man came. When it was done, he set his cup down and studied him the way he had at the forge, like a piece of metal he still hadn't found the flaw in.

"You talk like you've already built this in your head."

"I have. I just don't have the hands or the metal to build it yet. That's what I'm asking you for."

Bram was quiet a long moment. Somewhere in the back of the house his daughter was doing something that involved the occasional clatter of a pot, close enough to be listening, far enough to pretend she wasn't.

"I'll think on it," Bram said again, which, from him, Emeric was starting to understand was not a no.

"By the way, you haven't introduced me to your family," Emeric said, to break the silence.

"It's just me and my daughter, Ferra. Wife died of the plague a couple of years back. Been the two of us since."

Emeric felt awful for asking. He'd lost someone who mattered too.

"Condolences." Which soured the mood he'd been trying to escape in the first place.

Bram noticed and hauled it back. "I know my daughter's cute. But no, you cannot have her."

Emeric nearly sent tea out through his nose. "I would never ask you to do that for me. Not only would she have to accept my advances, but I'd also take it upon myself to ask your consent before anything, of course."

He could sense Ferra around the corner in the kitchen. He couldn't read her state, but this was the scene in every movie he'd ever watched where the woman starts blushing at being discussed in that light.

He decided he'd seen nothing. That put the mood back where it had been. At the end of the night he walked back to the castle to sleep, and Ward was still sneaking along behind him. At this point it was ritual. Tonight had a surprise waiting. Wexler was standing in the entry hall. For what, Emeric didn't know. He'd find out.

"Hello, Emeric." Flat, hiding every emotion that might have been under it.

There it was again. The silent, sporadic disdain he'd been getting since the day he arrived. Anyone below a prince on the royal ladder used the title before the name. Emeric didn't care about the pleasantry itself, being half a man from a world that had never had one, but the baron knew the custom and was actively choosing not to follow it. He couldn't call it out either. Why make bad blood in a place where most people would happily cast him as the villain of the story?

"Hello, Wexler. What can I help you with?" Same silent disrespect, returned in kind.

"I heard you've been making trips around my barony." Snide, barely bothering to hide it. "Is everything all right?"

Emeric saw no reason to lie. The baron knew where he'd been and when. No ruler lets a man with any measure of power roam his land untethered, and while Emeric doubted there was a spy on them, since Ward would have handled that quietly, the baron could simply trace their steps afterward. Information was never hard to buy. Most people caved to pleasure, payment, or pain.
After that thought, he gestured for the baron to follow him into a room rather than answer in the hall.

The move irked Wexler, though nothing showed. Who was this boy, running the conversation like the house was his? As far as the baron was concerned, moving a conversation from one room to another was his privilege alone. Emeric laid out the surveys he'd been running through the town and what he was trying to learn about why the plague kept collecting bodies. Wexler's face did nothing at all through it. He listened.

"Surveys," he repeated, turning the word over for a hidden edge. "You spent a month in my barony doing surveys."

"Water sources, burial sites, dates of first symptom onset, cross-referenced against household proximity to the river," Emeric said it the way he'd have said it to Bram, or to himself. Not performing an answer, just giving one. "The wells nearest the latrine outflow correlate with the earliest and worst cases. That's not proof yet; it's a pattern. I'd want more days of data before I acted on it."

Something moved behind Wexler's eyes, small, and gone almost before it registered.

"And what would you do with those days of data, Your Highness?"

"Put the latrines and the burial ground downstream of the wells, or move the wells upstream of both. Whichever's cheaper to dig. Boil the water in the meantime, and tell people why instead of just telling them to." Emeric shrugged, because to him it was obvious. "It won't take, though. Not without buy-in from somebody people already trust. I can hand a stranger a theory and get nothing out of it. Bram can hand them the same theory and get compliance by dinner."

He hadn't meant it as a display of anything. It was true in the way water finding low ground is true.

Wexler stayed quiet a moment too long.

"Bram," he said finally, "doesn't take to strangers."

"He didn't take to me either, at first." Emeric didn't dress it up. "I gave him something worth taking to."

The disdain was gone from the baron's face. That lazy performance-of-the-minimum he'd worn since the road wasn't there anymore, and what replaced it wasn't respect. It was the stillness of a man recalculating a threat he'd priced too cheap. A wasted prince asks where the sickness comes from and gets told a story. This one had built a testable model in under a day, out of nothing, and had the town's most trusted man speaking for him before the castle knew he'd left it.

"Get some rest, Your Highness." Wexler stood this time, which he hadn't bothered to do before. "You've had a long day."

It wasn't a dismissal. It was a man buying time to think.
