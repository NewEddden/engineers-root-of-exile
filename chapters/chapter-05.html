Chapters 19–57 | Batches 3–6 | Years 18–19. This is the complete prose synopsis of the four batches, following the planned events, character developments, political conflicts, battles, and consequences chapter by chapter.

Batch 3: Struck

Chapters 19–28 | November, Year 18 – February, Year 19

Chapter 19 — The Law of the Note

November brings a fundamental change to Kaldo's economy. The Assembly debates and passes the Issue Act, making the crown note the realm's single legal paper currency. Beginning January 10, the note will be accepted for every tax and public fee. Existing contracts written in coin retain their original terms for 120 days, after which old debts become payable in notes at par, provided the government's redemption guarantee remains enforceable.

Vantic watches the vote with particular interest. Her House will initially print the notes under its fixed-term charter, but that responsibility will eventually transfer to the independent Issue Board. She has deliberately helped construct a system that will ultimately remove her own monopoly over its most important function.

The opposition is substantial. Representatives of the March and Fairhythe fear losing their control over exchange and grain settlements. Several mill interests oppose the legislation as well. Most troubling to Vantic and Ferra is Tullio, sitting silently in the public gallery. He takes no notes and makes no objections. Ferra finds his silence more concerning than an open challenge.

Elsewhere, Tess continues work on the secret Bulwark. The pilot Wren manages to curl a single mechanical finger without moving any of the others, a significant improvement in the machine's sensory mapping. But the pilot's delayed reactions remain, Imani's strict testing limits still apply, and the machine is nowhere near certification.

Winter also changes the Drifter problem. The surviving young have little access to mana-rich stone, with shard fields fenced and storage buildings secured behind iron. Their growth slows. On Duvrenne's side of the Isle, another young Drifter is killed, its parts eventually entering the foreign trade.

The chapter closes with the Saltreach chandlers' guild approaching the Inspector of Sealing. The guild wants permission to operate a private sealing house for Drifter remains. For the first time, the crown faces an applicant that satisfies the law but that Emeric would rather not entrust with dangerous material.

Chapter 20 — Whatever's on the Forms

On December 5, Fulk Garside visits his sister Mabel for his birthday. He works as a foreman at Wystan's industrial works and now earns considerably more than he once did. He brings Mabel a cast-iron weighing scale with a brass pan, something she has wanted for years but never felt able to afford.

Their visit is affectionate, ordinary, and uncomfortable beneath the surface. When the mill whistle sounds, Fulk instinctively glances toward the door. Mabel notices and asks what Wystan's works is actually making. He answers too quickly and jokes about foremen becoming expendable when they ask questions. Pressed further, he says they make whatever is written on the forms.

Mabel doesn't pursue the matter. After he leaves, she studies her accounts and calculates how much coin she needs to reserve before the new currency takes effect. The expensive scale remains on her counter, raising questions she isn't prepared to write down.

Meanwhile, Merryn Pellow kills one of the two Drifter pups that escaped observation in September. It is found in the salt marsh almost exactly where it had last been seen.

Later in December, Kesrath grants Tess permission to examine the captured Tideshell's core, provided Duvrenne retains ownership and a witness observes the examination. Inside the Claim's iron shed, Tess reads the preserved core. She finds the familiar markings identifying its origin beneath the Isle, but another marking reveals something more significant: the Drifter's mature body wasn't predetermined when it was born. Its environment shaped what it became.

The core also contains instincts and movement patterns for swimming. This settles the question of Ward's planned Bastion mech: the Tideshell's core belongs to the kind of machine intended for Zale's Undertow, not Ward's land-combat Bastion. Emeric tells Ward personally. Ward accepts the result; he will wait for a Rampart's core. The discovery means these monsters grow into forms determined by the places in which they survive.

Chapter 21 — The Long Table

December 25 brings the first Long Table celebration held at home since the voyage. For one evening, Kaldo's leaders and their families try to put aside political struggles, the Isle Works Review, the currency transition, and the creatures wandering beyond the fences.

The castle's hall fills with familiar faces. Zale and Ilenne travel from Aumere. Waylon and Alina return from the Isle, now several months into their marriage. Tess, Osric, Wren, Maeve, Harrold, Corbin, Amara, the Crestless, and the people closest to Emeric gather together. Zale returns to the table where he was the first guest years earlier.

Runa experiences her first Long Table celebration at home. She attended the previous year's gathering on the polar ice beneath the aurora, but that occasion had no presents. Here, she doesn't understand why people keep giving things away. When she receives gifts, she becomes overwhelmed. For a child who lost her parents, survived alone, and learned to expect little from anyone, inclusion carries more weight than the others may realize.

The gifts reveal the people exchanging them. Emeric gives Tess precision files engraved with her name. Ferra gives Emeric a blank ledger for something other than work. Imani gives Ferra the mint she secretly chews while concentrating. Waylon gives Alina a badly made sand-glass he constructed himself, and she treasures it because he made it.

Amara, who doesn't need food, sits with a plate and performs the rituals of eating. Emeric attempts to lure Imani and Ferra away before dessert, but Ferra insists on pudding and Imani adds the toast. Emeric complains that they're making him wait for two things. Ferra reminds him that she can count.

Later, Wren sings a Duvrenne cradle song whose words Alina brought with her. Alina stays in the room to hear it. Harrold raises a glass to whoever will be sitting in the review room next month. Nobody asks him to explain. They simply drink together.

Chapter 22 — Day One

Early January begins with a medical milestone. Imani introduces clinic-monitoring bands to the four workers whose blood was affected at the Isle works. The bands measure pulse, blood oxygen, skin temperature, and falls. The workers consent, and Imani distinguishes what the measurements actually establish from what remains uncertain.

On January 10, the Issue Act takes effect, and the crown note becomes acceptable for taxes and public fees throughout Kaldo. In Cinderhale, Maudie oversees the League's chest as it accepts notes for legitimate dues. A millhand pays an actual assessment at face value. Another man attempts to pay a year's dues in advance using notes bought cheaply, but Maudie refuses. A stranger asks to exchange notes for coin and is also turned away.

The League is not a bank, speculative fund, or currency exchange; its rules are posted where everyone can read them. Elsewhere a family buys bread at the posted price with paper. Mabel carefully divides her accounts between coin obligations and local note transactions.

The rest of the realm responds less smoothly. At Pryor's Gate, exchangers accept notes at seven-eighths of face value. In Fairhythe, Sayer's weigh-house suspends grain settlements while waiting to see whether the paper holds. Halverne's harbour interests accept notes at face value immediately.

Tullio brings a legal challenge before Keller's court, arguing compulsory conversion violates creditors' contracted rights. His objection is genuine, not merely political, and the argument is published for the realm to examine.

The following morning Tullio orders payment on 1,900 harvest notes, representing about 7,400 Gold Rand, all payable in coin on February 10. Every demand is lawful inside the transition period. He signs them in geographical order, one farm after another across the western plains.

Emeric sees Cinderhale's bread purchase as evidence that the currency might work. Ferra warns him against turning one town's success into a national triumph. Tullio's demands are already moving into the countryside.

Chapter 23 — Part One

On January 28, the Isle Works Review delivers its first formal finding. Emeric and Harrold attend as the men whose signatures made it binding on the crown. Ferra sits in the public gallery because she is recused. Dacey sits with the families of those who died.

Keller reads the established facts in sequence: the emergency exception, the order issued under it, the explosives fired, the altered measurements, the destroyed seal, and the awakening beneath the Isle. The review identifies the emergency exception as the authority that permitted the decisive charges.

It delivers two remedies. First, it recommends removing the crown's unilateral emergency power over public works and armed forces. Future emergency operations would require the Assembly's authorisation. Second, the crown's share of the Isle works is placed in trust for ten years for the families of the fourteen dead and the Islanders. Compensation must also come separately from the crown's own funds.

The findings take away two things Emeric values: the ability to act quickly without legislative permission and direct access to the Isle's strategically important resources. He states his objections. The four armed houses are becoming more dangerous, and the mysterious warning concerning the hollow suggests that something larger may be approaching. A government forced to debate each emergency could lose valuable time.

He acknowledges that the review has accurately established what happened and promises to obey its remedies. Dacey feels no victory. Hearing Bryn's name formally read aloud leaves her exhausted rather than satisfied. She accepts appointment as one of the families' trustees and walks past the First Room afterward without entering.

Emeric and Harrold sign the trust deed that afternoon. That night, Emeric tells Imani and Ferra that the Quiet's clock will not stop for a committee. Imani answers that arriving a day late can be better than acting a day too early and being wrong, as they were when fourteen people died.

On February 1, Emeric reads the review's recommendation word for word to the Assembly. He neither advocates for it nor argues against it. A vote is scheduled for February 28. The older oil bores resume operations, but their proceeds now belong to the trust. Ferra must purchase Kaldo's former share at Duvrenne's market price. Emeric has lost an asset he once controlled, and he accepts that loss.

Chapter 24 — The Rolls

In Cinderhale, a dispute over dues has worsened since January. Ezra Pelham's collection agents continue demanding coin from tenants who have already paid their assessments. Maudie gathers marked payment slips, receipts, and entries from the official assessment register. She submits a written complaint on January 17, acknowledged by the mill lords' clerk. The demands nevertheless continue, and by early February thirty-one tenants have joined the petition.

On February 3, the Hearth acts. Kezia supervises a night operation in the courthouse, where members remove Pelham's working collection rolls and burn them in the hearth. They preserve the official assessment register and tenure records. The destruction is targeted at the collection process, not the underlying lawful dues.

Even a carefully limited operation has consequences. The following morning, Maudie is furious. The rolls were the most convenient central record of disputed collections. She must now travel from home to home recovering individual documents once gathered in one place. One tenant is relieved that Pelham's agents have lost their working rolls; another sees the fire as an obstacle to legal redress. Both have reasons for their reactions.

Within the Hearth, the argument grows sharper. Gethin asks who selected the pages for destruction. Corrick answers that they did. Gethin asks who "they" are. This time Corrick cannot truthfully claim that the town made the decision.

On February 4, Keller's court decides Tullio's currency lawsuit. It narrowly upholds conversion at par but rules that timely redemption in coin is essential to protecting creditors. If the government fails its guarantee, creditors may seek legal relief. Tullio loses the immediate case but preserves a dangerous legal opening.

Vantic reads the redemption provision twice. She understands that the note's success now depends on more than legislation: the institutions backing it must actually perform.

Chapter 25 — Friday

February brings the first major financial crisis of the new currency. Tullio's 1,900 coin demands become payable February 10, pressuring a western economy already short of coin. Government redemption offices operate on published days, and their schedules cannot immediately meet the demand. Fairhythe's suspended grain settlements and the March's discount on notes make matters worse.

Assembly representatives propose a standstill bill preventing new coin demands on harvest notes during the rest of the transition. But the bill requires nine days to pass and cannot undo Tullio's already-served demands.

For individual traders, the problem is immediate. Tom Ashlock delivers a load of cheese under a coin invoice. His shipper cannot provide coin and offers notes instead. Tom cannot accept them as full settlement under the original terms: his wholesaler and two workers must be paid in coin before the next redemption opportunity. They need their wages Friday.

Vantic opens a private financial window through her House. It buys verified unpaid commercial claims, checks each to prevent duplicate assignments, and pays suppliers and workers directly in coin. It operates separately from depositors' funds, under independent audit, for thirty days. Tom's claim is accepted on day two. His workers and supplier receive coin; Tom gets the remainder after a disclosed one-percent fee.

Not everyone qualifies. A Halverne factor trying to exchange bearer notes is refused because he has no assignable commercial claim. The operation exposes Vantic's House to substantial risk, including a pledge against her own glass tower.

Emeric opposed the plan because he feared allowing a private House to become the financial authority the Issue Board was meant to be. On February 16 he visits Vantic and admits that he was wrong about the immediate need, while maintaining his concern about concentrated private power. She agrees: the fixed end date and independent auditor exist for exactly that reason.

The Assembly passes the standstill bill February 18. That Friday, Tom pays his workers in coin. He does not yet understand the political and financial struggle that made their wages possible.

Chapter 26 — Four Chairs

In the Cocan March, reformers challenge the hereditary system from within. A committee of reeves, toll workers, junior magistrates, and local reformers, including Agatha Vell, petitions the Assembly for representation and a referendum on the March's civil franchises.

The governing coalition offers four elected seats and a formal rights hearing before the broader state map has been completed. That timing matters: Cinderhale and other communities have been waiting for representation through the ordinary process. The March, however, commands important roads and possesses armed forces capable of blocking access toward Ostrel. Its leverage produces concessions peaceful petitions have not.

Pryor refuses to let the March vote on the proposal. His argument is historical: the hereditary charter has kept roads open, provided protection, and supported widows for three hundred years. Elected representatives could be purchased, and a distant Kaldo majority could eventually repeal guarantees on which the March depends. He will not trade those protections for four chairs.

Agatha responds that those three hundred years were never his to trade. Pryor remembers her late husband's name as she leaves, a gesture personal enough to hurt but insufficient to change her mind.

In Cinderhale, Corrick pins two documents on the League's wall: the earlier minute deferring Maudie's petition for representation and the new offer made to the March. He writes nothing underneath. A carter reads both twice before observing quietly that this, apparently, is what it takes.

Chapter 27 — The Hour

On February 25, the circuit court holds its first sitting in Cinderhale. Maudie appears for thirty-one tenants whose dues were improperly collected. With the surviving register, receipts, and marked slips, she wins an interim order suspending disputed demands and binding Pelham's agents. Legitimate dues remain payable; the ruling does not simply erase obligations because a collector behaved unlawfully.

The next morning, Emeric visits the League's hall with Ferra. There is no royal platform, dais, or ceremonial separation. Gethin refuses to enter. Outside, Emeric notices the reconstructed channels in Gethin's regrown hand and asks who repaired it. Gethin's answer points to Imani without naming her. When Emeric later asks, Imani refuses to discuss a patient's treatment. Gethin was her patient, and that is all she will disclose.

Inside, Corrick lays out the League's documents. He confronts Emeric over decisions made far away, particularly his choice of Wayford while aboard a ship. Emeric admits that he misjudged the cost to ordinary people.

Corrick requests a seat for Cinderhale by royal order. Emeric refuses: representation cannot depend on the king granting personal favours to those who confront him. Corrick asks instead for representation through general law. Emeric acknowledges that this is a legitimate demand and that the state map has already been delayed twice.

The conversation moves from individual mistakes to constitutional power. Corrick asks what prevents a future king from reversing the reforms. Emeric answers that the existing safeguards aren't enough. Not yet. This is an admission, not a solution.

Outside, Ferra presses him to explain what he intends to do with the monarchy. He refuses to give a firm answer. She says that without a concrete commitment, Corrick has nothing by which to hold him accountable. Emeric knows she is right. The hour ends without reconciliation, promise, or forgiveness.

Chapter 28 — Struck

On February 28, the Assembly votes on the first major recommendation of the Isle Works Review: whether the crown should retain unilateral emergency powers over public works and armed forces.

A member representing the Tarrow argues against removal. He has watched Sayer's armed porters grow increasingly threatening and fears that requiring parliamentary permission will make the government slow to respond when violence begins. His concern is sincere, and the Assembly treats it seriously.

The crews' representative answers with the constitutional argument: keeping the power to deal with dangerous private houses also hands it to every future king. The Assembly cannot guarantee that Emeric's successor will exercise it responsibly.

Even the mill lord, usually hostile to broader Crown reform, supports the recommendation because he signed the review's finding and intends to honour his own judgment. Corbin confirms that Emeric has submitted the recommendation exactly as promised.

The Assembly votes. The emergency exception is struck from the crown's prerogative. A new procedure places emergency authorisations under the Assembly, including their terms, renewals, and oversight of spending.

Ferra's earlier censure has expired, restoring her personal power over crown emergency spending. But the new law removes the category of unilateral crown emergencies to which that power applied. Constitutional reform has made the restored authority largely irrelevant.

Emeric signs the formal notice without protest and leaves by the members' door rather than the ceremonial royal entrance. At the foot of the steps, Ferra is waiting. She says, "That's one." He understands that she is counting safeguards being established against future monarchs. He answers yes. They walk home together.

Batch 4: Ninety Days

Chapters 29–37 | February – April, Year 19

Chapter 29 — Served

The morning after the Assembly removes the crown's emergency powers, Ferra sits at breakfast with Emeric and Imani, reading the new procedures. Her year under censure ended only days earlier. She has regained the authority she lost, but the Assembly has transferred oversight of emergency spending to its own committee. She laughs at being the first Chancellor to recover her powers only to find the government has eliminated the kind of spending she could control. Emeric observes that the institution is working as intended. Ferra answers that this is exactly what frightens her.

The political consequences arrive almost immediately. At Fairhythe, the Inspectorate obtains a witness willing to testify about illegal firearms concealed in the porters' lodge and weigh-house arms room. A dismissed porter gives sworn evidence that weapons subject to the Private Arms Act remain hidden, together with equipment for making ammunition. The circuit court grants an order of entry.

On March 2, a commissioner serves that order on Baroness Sayer across her own weighing table. She signs for it and declares that commissioners are welcome when they come by the road and ask permission. Her first private instruction is deliberately ambiguous: no crown man may land at her tables without her leave. Her captains interpret it differently.

The next day a crown barge passes the chain because the captain is unsure whether the restriction applies to a boat. The clerks are turned away from the upper stair, but nobody is injured. Sayer responds with an unmistakable second order. The commissioners are not to pass the chain, and the watch must stop them by all necessary force, including gunfire. Fairhythe's notary records it on March 4. The courier makes a copy for the captain and keeps the signed original, anticipating a need to prove what Sayer ordered.

In Cinderhale, one of Pelham's collectors brings another demand to widow Tench despite her receipts and the court's stay. He is served for contempt and fined. Tench signs Maudie's next petition but refuses to praise the Hearth. She rejects the mill lords' abuse and the group's unilateral decision to take the law into its own hands. Sayer's signed order travels upriver, waiting to become evidence of a crime not yet committed.

Chapter 30 — Lantern Night

March 6 brings Lantern Night, the annual celebration of Kaldo's First Light. Families gather along the river wall, writing names on lanterns before releasing them into the night. The celebration combines public achievement with private grief.

Dacey attends her first Lantern Night in Kaldo and writes her brother Bryn's name. Emeric asks whether he may write Bryn's name on his lantern too. Dacey tells him each person has one lantern and that he should write his own. She isn't trying to punish him, and he understands. Emeric releases his lantern blank; Imani notices but says nothing.

Nearby, Runa writes Veya and Orren in the school letters she has learned. They are her dead parents' names and among the first words she wanted to learn to write. She holds the lantern longer than anyone before she lets it go.

The celebration also shows how Kaldo has changed. The fleet's 1,200 crew members wear their new dress coats from Abloh. Ferra announces educational graduates by field, rather than one grand total. The crowd cheers unexpectedly, even for surveying. Sorrel sends a tethered balloon over the square with two children in the basket while loudly complaining about its burner.

Fennick's fireworks illuminate the city. His last rocket falls harmlessly into the river, exactly where Ferra's safety rules require it to land. He is disappointed by the predictable end to his best display yet.

While the city celebrates, Fairhythe's crisis worsens. Emeric reads Sayer's polite written response to the Inspectorate and still believes the matter may be resolved through ordinary legal procedures. He doesn't know she has already ordered gunfire. Far away on the Tarrow, the commissioners' barge waits at the lower quay for morning.

Chapter 31 — Four

On March 7, the Inspectorate's barge moves upriver toward Fairhythe's chain. Four commissioners and a magistrate's clerk carry the lawful entry order. A young bargehand from the Penhale family helps guide them. They are still eighty yards short of the chain when the captain of Sayer's battery orders fire.

The swivel gun fires first, and the other weapons follow. Four commissioners are killed. The clerk survives with a wounded arm. The bargehand throws himself into the water and swims to safety. The barge never reaches the chain.

News reaches Kaldo that afternoon. Ferra receives the report and writes the four dead commissioners' names herself. The next morning Keller convenes a preliminary hearing. There is enough evidence to issue warrants against Sayer, the chain captain, and identified members of the gun crew.

Keller orders preservation of Fairhythe's notary register and the battery's powder book. He gives protection to the surviving clerk and bargehand, but refuses a blanket warrant against every member of Fairhythe's armed watch. The evidence establishes potential liability for identifiable people, not proof that every porter knew of the order or participated in the attack.

He also insists that issuing warrants isn't convicting anyone. The kingdom now faces armed resistance, but courts must still treat individuals as individuals. The chapter ends with Keller signing named warrants and rejecting collective guilt.

Chapter 32 — Ninety Days

The killings at Fairhythe produce the first serious test of the emergency system created only days before. Emeric can no longer order a military operation and seek approval afterward. On March 8 he stands before the Assembly and formally requests authorisation to end armed resistance in Fairhythe and the Cocan March.

For two days, members debate duration, scope, surrender, and civilian protections. The Tarrow representative who had opposed striking the royal exception admits that his fear of Sayer was justified but his constitutional conclusion was wrong. He now prefers the Assembly to write the limits rather than a king in a hurry. A March representative argues the operation amounts to war against his own people; Agatha Vell watches from the gallery.

Corbin presents a surrender instrument setting out limited conditional non-prosecution, the crimes never covered, and the protections offered to lower-ranking fighters. Emeric listens without trying to dominate proceedings. Privately he complains that the process has cost a day. Ferra corrects him: three. Nobody else died during those days, but Fairhythe's upper town used them to strengthen its defenses.

On March 10, the Assembly passes the March Emergency Authorisation. It runs for ninety days, from March 11 through June 8, with renewal only by another Assembly vote. Ward commands the field company. Magistrate Ada Quarrie is appointed surrender officer, with carefully limited powers and reviewable findings.

The authorisation offers pardon and pay to ordinary armed men who surrender but excludes murder, torture, hostage-taking, and command after the proclamation. Force against a held position requires the proclamation to have been read there. Emeric's personal force is bound by the same terms. Quarrie asks Keller whether her decisions can be reviewed, and accepts only after he says yes.

Elsewhere, Tullio sends another payment representing a quarter's wages to Pryor's forces and preserves the duplicate receipt. He thinks of it as financing a client whose collateral is the only important road to Ostrel. The chapter ends with emergency law in effect and his money moving toward the rebellion.

Chapter 33 — The Window Shuts

The financial crisis and armed conflict develop together. Saban's agents observe Church-associated carts passing Pryor's Gate at night, carrying materials toward the keep and old Holt fort. March exchangers begin taking stones in place of ordinary money. Saban suspects something powered by stones is being built but cannot establish what or why.

In Kaldo, three hundred pager bands are issued to duty staff under the authorisation. Field officers, surgeons, Quarrie's clerks, circuit bailiffs, and other emergency personnel can receive short radio messages. The devices are receivers only; staff must answer from nearby radio sets. Imani approves them for medical duty staff but refuses to put active alert devices on vulnerable patients. Clinic-monitoring bands remain passive.

On March 14, Vantic closes her private financial window exactly on schedule. Roughly 112,000 Gold Rand in claims were presented, about 81,000 accepted, and around 80,190 paid out. Credit secured against her glass tower has financed part of it. An independent auditor seals the books. She still doesn't know her final loss and walks home without looking up at the tower.

A message from the Sun Shore reaches Kaldo. The orc families have chosen Petrok, a warden and Brega's grandson, to occupy their long-empty chair on the Isle Works Review. Brega herself will accompany him to see the dead behind the buried city's doors; their ship is expected in April.

On March 20, the Issue Board publishes the seasonal coin shortfall across the western plains: about 95,000 Gold Rand. Vantic's intervention covered much but not all. Ferra points out the difference to Emeric. These figures represent farms and businesses that endured the shortage without equivalent help. The note is recovering, but the cost is now measurable.

Chapter 34 — The Chain

On March 16, Ward's field company lands at lower Fairhythe. The proclamation is read from the quay, broadcast by loudhailer, and distributed in printed copies. Lower Fairhythe opens almost immediately. Barge families, carters, and traders resent the tolls and restrictions imposed by the upper town. But upper Fairhythe remains fortified, its porters holding the gate and chain.

The next morning, two porters who have surrendered are beaten by angry lower-town residents. Ward personally stops the attackers and has them detained. The pardon must apply to everyone who qualifies, including the people who previously served the enemy.

On March 18, the proclamation is read at the river chain and upper gate, but defenders won't yield. Ward plans to cut the chain posts under cover, likely provoking a firefight with the battery. Emeric proposes another method. Ward weighs the law and military risk and approves it.

On March 19, Emeric transforms publicly into his full dragon form on the lower quay. The town watches as he enters the river. The defenders flee. He lifts the massive chain from its posts and lays it on the bank, removing the obstruction without physically attacking anyone. He returns, changes back, and borrows a coat.

Ruth Penhale watches from her barge. Sayer once gave her family a fair judgment; Ruth remembers that kindness while condemning Sayer's rebellion. She also dislikes a king who can dismantle a fortified river defense almost casually. Power used lawfully can still inspire fear.

Over the next two days, upper-town porters surrender in small groups, laying down their coats and guns. The weigh-house releases suspended grain settlements. On March 22, Keller's bailiffs arrest Sayer in her hall. She orders the notary's register handed over because her house's own law demands it, and asks to walk rather than ride in a cart. They allow it. She walks under arrest through the town she controlled.

News of the dragon's intervention reaches Pryor's Gate. The defenders now know that ordinary walls and chains will not hold Kaldo's king, making their mysterious dead-zone stones more important.

Chapter 35 — Two Villages

With Fairhythe secured, Ward moves toward the March. His forces need transport, food, and a supply line. Local carters refuse service, so the field command requisitions grain carts and teams from Cray Bottom and Stile Green. Those same carts carry the villages' remaining winter grain down from the hills before spring roads open.

Ferra sees the danger and brings the villages' grain records to Ilsa, reviewer of the Assembly's emergency spending. She requests a fast stay. Ilsa refuses because the field command has shown urgent military need and stopping the requisition could strand the troops without supplies. The decision is defensible on the evidence. Ferra believes she would have imposed a stay conditional on alternate grain deliveries, but that pen is no longer hers.

Both villages run short. Families begin eating seed grain intended for planting. Agatha Vell's neighbours blame the crown.

At Stannary, Treloar opens his works to the Inspectorate after Fairhythe's surrender. Most wardens disarm, though some miners remain loyal because they remember when the pumps worked and wages arrived. The pumps are now failing, money is short, and dangerous maintenance has gone undone.

On the Saltreach coast, Merryn Pellow kills a Drifter pup about 1.6 metres long, larger than the winter specimens. It fed near an exposed shard deposit. Spring is restoring the young creatures' movement and growth. In Coldharbour, unemployed hired men drift into banditry while Malloch's knights share the proceeds instead of stopping them.

The villages finally get a hearing. Reeve Bessa Lorne of Stile Green describes what people have eaten and what they may now be unable to plant. The emergency committee overturns the requisition, but the loss has already occurred. Agatha can condemn Pryor's hereditary rule and still mourn the pension clerks who once sent help faster than the new state.

Ferra sits beside Agatha in the gallery without speaking. She knows what she would have signed and knows she no longer had the power to sign it.

Chapter 36 — Dunholt

On March 28, a former watchman brings Corrick information about a suspicious shipment at Dunholt. Roughly two hundred firearms have been crated as plough-shares. Six wagons and a spare team are booked to leave at dawn on March 30, heading toward the March. Fulk Garside is expected to accompany them.

Corrick immediately calls the field command through the public reporting line. The duty officer logs the warning and explains the obstacle: Dunholt's ducal liberty prevents Inspectorate entry without a specific writ supported by firsthand sworn evidence. The magistrate won't sit until after first light, possibly too late to stop the convoy.

The Hearth debates intervention. Kezia initially vetoes it. Examining schedules, they find that the wagons could split, making partial disruption insufficient. One member asks why they can't seek the carters' permission. Doing so openly might alert the organizers and send the freight away early. Kezia knows the choice belongs to the people affected, not the Hearth, yet lifts her veto and admits she is assuming authority that isn't hers.

On March 29, five pairs enter the depot and systematically remove approximately two hundred gunlocks. They take two labelled firearms and the day book as evidence, leaving guns and crates intact. Gethin's rebuilt hand slips on a cold part; he accepts his partner's help. A notice is left for the carters.

Around two in the morning, a clerk returns unexpectedly carrying the waybill book. Kezia realizes it may be essential to proving the operation and protecting the carriers. She goes back for it, passes it to a lookout, and is captured by the depot's night men. The alarm reaches field command; duty bailiffs arrive.

Gethin wants to rescue her. Corrick refuses to commit another unlawful entry. At dawn, he and Gethin surrender with the locks, exemplars, day book, and a sworn statement. The waybill book reaches the court and is logged. By noon the magistrate can issue the writ. Fulk has already fled south.

The convoy doesn't move. Liss Whitlow reads the Hearth's notice, sees the bailiffs at the gate, unhitches her team, and turns back. The other carters follow. Corrick, Kezia, and Gethin are remanded to Kestle gaol. They prevented a dangerous shipment and broke the law to do it.

Chapter 37 — The East Stope

The Dunholt inventory establishes that two hundred locks correspond to 198 firearms left in crates and two exemplars taken by the Hearth. The barrels are bored for ammunition associated with the Church-traded firearms used at Pryor's Gate. False freight descriptions, industrial records, and the route establish an illegal manufacturing operation, but not necessarily Wystan's personal knowledge. He is charged April 3. Innocent carters are released, wagons returned, and depot workers' pay protected.

In Cinderhale, Mabel learns that her brother Fulk has disappeared. She receives a letter from Corrick at Kestle but burns it unopened. She continues legally trading flour with the League. The scale Fulk gave her remains on the counter; she uses it every day.

On April 2, Runa turns twelve. Emeric returns from field command for one evening and gives her a broken radio with replacement parts. Together they repair it at the kitchen table. She discovers an aptitude for mechanical work and finds a damaged joint partly from the smell of hot shellac. The activity reminds her of her father Orren. She asks Emeric to tell her again about his distinctive knock: two quick and one slow. She knows the story but wants to hear it. Ferra gives boots and a book; Imani gives a shuttered flash lamp for reading under the blankets.

Stannary approaches disaster. The crown's inspector finds failing pumps and inadequate supports in the east stope. On April 5, he issues a written order closing it until its timbers are replaced. Treloar cannot afford the work, partly because money that should have maintained the mine was spent on wardens' equipment. The eastern face remains the most profitable, so he quietly orders his crew to continue.

At the same time, shift captain Jory Tregarth has men repair the abandoned Monk's Rise behind fresh timber lagging near an old haulage incline. The passage is missing from plans supplied to the crown and provides a hidden route between Stannary and the March. It is connected to Pryor's preparations, but nobody working there knows what is coming. On Sunday night, April 9, the east stope's timbers begin to creak. The town above remains unaware.

Batch 5: Where's My Wife

Chapters 38–47 | April – June, Year 19

Chapter 38 — The East Stope

On April 10, disaster strikes Stannary. The east stope, closed by the crown's mines inspector five days earlier, is still operating. Supports were never replaced. Pumps have repeatedly failed, and water has weakened the material surrounding the pillars. Cal Pascoe, a nineteen-year-old pumpman, is underground when the structure gives way.

Nine workers die. Forty-one survive in two isolated pockets beneath the rock. Cal is among them, trapped beneath a slab with his legs pinned but still conscious and talking.

News reaches Kaldo. Imani immediately prepares a response with Sister Inga's capable emergency team. The situation presents a specialized complication: several trapped miners have deep blood-vessel damage held partly shut by the pressure of the rock. Releasing that pressure without controlling the injuries could kill them. Imani's hollow view can identify the damaged branches.

Ferra argues against her going because Stannary lies close to the armed March. Ward, speaking by set from field command, also warns of the security risk. Emeric doesn't overrule her. He knows the decision belongs to the surgeon and promises that Ward will establish a cordon. Imani flies to Stannary with Inga's team.

The engine-house hall is converted to a hospital. Ward arrives with a Crestless detail and establishes a perimeter. His men check three known exits from the workings. A fourth passage, the Coffin Level, is marked on available maps but believed impassable because its sump is flooded. Fresh lagging near the old lower incline draws little attention amid all the repairs.

A dispute develops over a religious relic. Nineteen trapped workers are March men, and their families ask for Saint Ilda's prayer over them before extraction. Mother Brannagh insists that Brother Cuthbert conduct the ceremony with a consecrated stone. Imani suggests saying the prayer outside, using an empty case, or holding the stone beyond the ward on a cord. Brannagh sincerely rejects each alternative.

Ward objects to any stone entering a treatment hall where Imani relies on magic. Cuthbert refuses to open the consecrated case. A compromise is made: the container is weighed, double-sealed, and sensed by a Kaldo mage to hold one ordinary stone. Ward records his formal protest. Imani signs beneath it, accepting the risk because denying the ceremony may delay lifesaving treatment. The chapter ends with both signatures in the log.

Chapter 39 — Release

By April 12, rescuers reach the two pockets of surviving miners. Sister Inga organizes treatment for all forty-one. Her team prepares before anyone is extracted, administering fluids, watching pulses and rhythms, planning staged release, and arranging care for kidney damage caused by prolonged compression. It is a capable medical operation that doesn't depend entirely on Imani.

Imani treats six miners whose deep thigh vessels are partly torn and held shut by the rock. Hollow view lets her find the branches and place ties through narrow openings while the slabs are lifted incrementally. She completes two cases Wednesday and three Thursday. Cal Pascoe remains until Friday because his slab is especially difficult to move.

On the third day she has her hands in Cal's thigh, an important tie only partly placed. She asks Cuthbert to move the lamp closer. He panics and forces the concealed stone, driving mana from the hall. Imani loses her magical sight but finishes the immediate ligature by touch. Then six conspirators hidden among the March rescue volunteers reveal themselves.

Imani draws her mundane revolver with one hand and shoots a nearby attacker in the hip. Sir Edwin Hartley, an unarmed knight-orderly, attempts to stop Cuthbert. Another conspirator stabs him. Sir Edwin dies within a minute. A knife is placed against an injured patient's throat. Imani orders the other orderly to stand still and offers herself in exchange for the patients.

The attackers accept. They fasten a drain cuff to her wrist. Inside the dead zone, it locks mechanically but cannot yet drain mana. Four attackers escort Imani and Cuthbert into the supposedly impassable Coffin Level. The stabber and the man she shot remain behind. From the porch an orderly fires a mundane signal flare.

Imani disappears into the dark passage as the hospital staff continue the rescue without the specialist whose sight had been keeping Cal alive.

Chapter 40 — Black

At about 2:10 in the afternoon, Emeric is speaking with Ward at Fairhythe when his bond with Imani vanishes. There is no pain, fear, movement, or distance: she is simply gone, the way death might feel. In Kaldo, Ferra feels the same disappearance at her desk and sits on the floor. For nearly half an hour, neither knows whether Imani is alive.

Then the bond returns. Imani is frightened, moving, alive. As the kidnappers take her beyond the dead zone, her cuff begins draining her connection and the bond thins to a thread. Emeric takes the K Discovery into the air with Ward, following the sense of nearer and farther. Ferra prepares another ship from Kaldo. Runa insists on coming. Emeric objects to taking a twelve-year-old into an active rebellion. Runa answers that Imani is her mother. Ferra does not stop her.

By the time Emeric's ship reaches Stannary, the connection has gone black again: Imani is inside another dead zone. Ward searches for a route through the abandoned workings. He remembers an entry on the Dunholt charge sheet: Kezia Varley was once a Stannary shot-firer. She is held in Kestle gaol. Ward asks to put her on a radio set, but a magistrate's order is needed, delaying her participation until evening.

Kezia sketches the old workings on the back of a charge form: the Monk's Rise, the haulage incline, a ladder climb, and the Old Gate adit inside the March. The duty clerk transmits her description measure by measure. Asked why she helps, she answers: Gethin's hand. She doesn't want her assistance counted at sentencing. She wants it done.

At almost the same time, Runa reaches the airship above the mine. She identifies fresh-cut timber, lamp oil, and Imani's scent near the old incline. Her turn matches Kezia's drawing. The original dead zone has already dissipated, so Runa can follow the path without entering one. She leads two Crestless through flooded workings and up the hidden ladderway.

At the Old Gate adit, the covering detachment has gone and two carriages have departed. One contains Imani's shawl. Runa rejects it: a shawl isn't a person. The second carries Imani's fear and sweat and the cuff's stone. Runa follows its trail nine miles with the airship, until the air suddenly becomes unreadable at the edge of a dead zone.

Back at Stannary, Inga struggles to save Cal Pascoe. Imani's tie was temporary; the damaged branch needs definitive repair that the remaining team cannot locate by mundane means in time. Cal dies shortly before eleven. Inga enters the death in the hall's book. A girl has found the boundary of her mother's prison, and a young man has died because the surgeon treating him was taken.

Chapter 41 — The Count

Through the night, Ferra studies the bond's intermittent returns. Every few hours, Imani becomes perceptible for a moment before disappearing. Ferra records each flicker against the clock. Pryor has made two dead zones: the keep and the old Holt fort. The fort uses one large stone and stays constant; the keep has a rack whose stones are forced in rotation. Its switching creates brief gaps. Every flicker matches a change in the rack.

By dawn, Ferra knows that Imani is in the keep, not the decoy fort. Finding her is no longer the principal difficulty. Getting her out alive is.

Ward's troops approach the pass but stay three miles below the keep, held there by the gate guns. Runa gathers more evidence from the night wind along the cliff. She locates Imani's room on the east range's ground floor, identifies medicine and sick people, and smells sulphur and saltpetre beneath them: stored powder. She begins mapping guards by tobacco, sweat, and dogs.

Inside, Imani is made to work in the infirmary with her cuff on. Fourteen bedridden servants depend on her care. Davey Corve, a nineteen-year-old March conscript, guards her room; his father is one of Pryor's sergeants. Imani studies the people around her for a way to reduce danger without triggering a massacre.

The keep receives medicine through an account connected to Vantic's House. Imani uses a return slip and Kaldo's pharmacy shorthand to send a message identifying fourteen patients, the east range, powder beneath it, and the instruction to wait.

Outside, Quarrie and Ward approach under a flag of truce. Pryor demands withdrawal of the Authorisation from the March, a hereditary charter made untouchable by future statute, amnesty for his supporters, and exemption from the note's monetary system. Imani would be released only when those terms become law. Quarrie says the Assembly alone can answer, and relays the proposal.

At dusk, Agatha Vell arrives with March reformers nobody summoned. They offer to stand as civilian witnesses to any surrender, determined that the March's future won't be settled only by its hereditary lord and Kaldo's army.

Chapter 42 — Six Days

On April 17, Keller interprets the Assembly's surrender proclamation. Guards who knowingly keep a hostage confined are not automatically covered by the ordinary pardon. However, anyone who frees or protects hostages can apply to Quarrie individually.

Inside the keep, Davey reads the ruling in the town's news-sheet. He asks Imani what case-by-case treatment means. She explains that he could earn protection by helping free people. He asks whether it might cover his father. Her answer is harder. Davey spends a whole watch in silence.

Tullio reads Pryor's demands and fears Imani may be transferred to Ostrel. He sends his agent a new instruction: release the next tranche only while the guest remains at the Gate. He believes keeping her inside the March preserves leverage and a chance of recovery, but the words could equally be evidence of financing continued captivity. He keeps a duplicate.

Ward prepares an infiltration using Runa's cliff map. Crestless in MK3 stealth suits climb under night vision. Their magical systems function outside the zone; inside it they use mundane infrared monoculars. On the third night, the wind turns. Runa loses the scent of the wall guards, and Pryor has posted a new picket with a dog after the parley. The dog detects the climbers. Rather than provoke gunfire near the infirmary and powder, the team withdraws without a shot. The failure isn't Runa's nose; the wind changed.

On April 18, Ferra flies to Kaldo to take part in the Assembly's decision on Pryor's demands. The terms are refused. That day, Imani's coded slip reaches Vantic, who forwards it to Ward, confirming Runa's account of the infirmary and powder.

By April 19, the keep's guards know Keller's ruling. Davey understands his choices may determine his future. Quarrie prepares the form for conditional passage but refuses to fill in a finding until she has the facts. Amara's relay surveillance reveals only roofs. Ward plans another cliff ascent at the two-o'clock guard change, with Runa monitoring scent and wind from outside the zone. Inside, Imani prepares her own move with Davey and eleven conscripts.

During every brief flicker of the bond, Imani sends calm. Emeric understands it as wait, and he waits because she asks. The chapter ends when the evening wind turns favourable and Ward says the operation is tonight.

Chapter 43 — Where's My Wife

At 12:40 in the morning on April 20, Imani's brief return through the bond brings terror instead of calm. Runa simultaneously smells the keep's stable yard waking: horses, harness, and convoy men preparing to move. Pryor has advanced the transfer. Imani may be carried across the pass into Ostrel before dawn.

A mounted outrider races up the road to alert March pickets. Runa surges forward before Ward can stop her, chasing the horse at extraordinary speed. It panics at the wolf scent. She drags the rider down and pins him by the throat until Ward arrives. She doesn't kill him. Her birthday flash lamp falls into the road, still shining.

The bond and Runa's evidence convince Emeric that waiting until two is impossible. He transforms into his full dragon form on the ridge. His roar shakes the country, carrying ragged words everyone understands: "WHERE'S MY WIFE?" The defenders turn toward the gate, letting the Crestless begin climbing the cliff in the distraction.

Emeric advances on the towers, which stand outside the dead zone. He increases the gravity on their parapets to force the gunners down. Hal Belk crawls under the weight. Emeric, furious, presses harder and hears the man struggling to breathe. He releases the pressure on purpose. He has stopped short of killing him, but has used dangerous force against a held position before the required proclamation was read.

Inside the keep, Imani breaks from the men preparing her transfer. Davey opens the infirmary corridor for walking patients and eleven conscripts to escape into the yard. Conspirators from Stannary seize the powder cellar beneath the east range. The Crestless climb through the window and secure fourteen bedridden patients above the explosives, but cannot storm the cellar with a lit match beneath so many lives.

Matt Lowrie, one of the conscripts trying to escape, runs for the cellar stair to stop the match. Davey's father shoots him. Davey sees it. Matt dies under Imani's hands.

Quarrie arrives and faces a hostage crisis within the rescue. She can offer conditional exile to some offenders, but cannot erase a witnessed murder. She reads her finding aloud as she writes: she can buy the patients' safety with passage, not purchase a murderer's immunity. Three men receive conditional non-prosecution for specified non-murder offenses and exile over the pass, without a declaration of innocence or protection from later-proven murder. Davey's father is excluded. Quarrie tells Emeric that the pass binds the crown, not only him.

Davey walks alone to the cellar mouth. His father hands him the match and emerges with his hands raised. This is a decision to protect his son and the patients, not a sudden redemption. The three others depart under Quarrie's instrument. Corporal Piet Neate, Sir Edwin's friend, aims at Tregarth. Emeric steps between them. Neate lowers the rifle but accuses the king publicly of protecting men implicated in the killing. Emeric protects his right to speak.

At Agatha's urging, Pryor's officers surrender their lord. Imani warns Ward of a plan to destroy the pass bridge; a Crestless pair intercepts the men carrying out that plan. The bridge remains standing.

Emeric and Imani finally walk beyond the dead zone together, and the bond returns in full. In Kaldo, Ferra sits down on the floor again, laughing with relief. Runa reaches Imani first. Imani kneels and embraces her daughter before addressing anyone else. Later, Imani says she was telling Emeric to wait and had been planning her own move. He says he sensed terror without knowing its cause. Neither understands the other's entire experience. She calls him late and loud, then goes back to her patients.

Chapter 44 — Royal Force

The rescue succeeded, but Sir Edwin Hartley, Cal Pascoe, and Matt Lowrie are dead. The patients held in the keep survive, yet that fact cannot erase the choices that put them at risk. On April 21, Imani requests a medical review of Cal's death and her decision to permit the relic inside the treatment hall.

She also refuses Ferra's request for the names of families who supported the kidnapping, information a patient revealed in confidence while she treated him. She distinguishes those past disclosures from the imminent plan to destroy the pass bridge, which she warned Ward about immediately. The disagreement strains their relationship.

Quarrie convenes the gate inquiry under the Authorisation. The panel hears Ward, Emeric, Hal Belk and other tower men, Runa, and witnesses. Runa describes the stable-yard activity she smelled before the rescue. Quarrie accepts her extraordinary senses as evidence, not as an automatic verdict. Ward supports her account with a stable boy who heard the order to harness horses.

The finding has three parts. Emeric had reasonable grounds to intervene before the planned time because the transfer appeared imminent. But his gravity press violated the Authorisation: no proclamation was read at that gate before force. Whether his roar changed the risk to the infirmary, or whether a stealth-only rescue might have saved Matt, cannot be known. Matt was murdered by the man who shot him.

Emeric is censured. The committee adopts the Rule on Royal Force for the rest of the campaign. His personal force now requires the proclamation, prior written field-command approval, and an order filed with the reviewing officer. Hal and the other tower men receive compensation. Emeric accepts the restrictions.

Runa has nightmares about the two carriages and the fear that she might have followed the wrong one. Imani sits beside her when she wakes. Ferra stores the flicker log in a drawer rather than displaying it.

Before they recover, a new threat arrives. Earl Malloch of Coldharbour has gathered approximately 1,800 men, including house knights, hired fighters, and foreign mercenaries financed by imperial credit. Two dead-zone stones escaped from Pryor's keep in the rescue's confusion and reached him. Malloch's host burns Fennick's Cross and hangs its reeve. The field command issues the proclamation; Malloch refuses.

Ward briefs Emeric on the stones, warning that a dead zone will prevent his external magic. He asks what Emeric will be when the stones are forced. Emeric answers: standing up.

Chapter 45 — The Black Moss

On April 27, the field command confronts Malloch's host on the Black Moss. This time the legal requirements come first. Ward signs the royal force order. The herald reads the proclamation across the field by loudhailer and drops leaflets offering pardon and pay to anyone who lays down arms and leaves. Only then does Emeric depart.

He approaches at extraordinary speed from Pryor's keep. Fennick's Cross is burned, its reeve has been hanged, and Imani was in a cell a week earlier. Emeric is furious. His dragon form passes through the low sky at roughly 900 to 1,000 miles per hour. Fire leaks along his wings and down his length, trailing behind him. A shepherd sees what looks like a falling star going somewhere. A Kaldo-educated soldier calls it an asteroid.

The host sees the blazing figure in its last few seconds. Emeric hits the bog about two hundred yards short of the front rank, using gravity to brake himself but still landing violently. Peat and water erupt around a thirty-yard crater. Men fall, horses bolt, and the front ranks scatter. Nobody dies at the impact: by luck, not careful control, as Ward will later say.

Emeric stands between Malloch's men and the east road. Anyone who abandons the rebellion may drop his weapons and walk away under the proclamation. Whoever tries to stop surrendering men will be restrained. For fifteen minutes they choose. Around 1,300 leave, including some household knights and foreign mercenaries with contracts still in force. About five hundred remain.

Emeric changes down as the count ends. Malloch forces both dead-zone stones. The soldiers see the dragon shrink, cheer, and charge. The zone suppresses external magic and keeps Emeric from changing back, but not the formidable physical strength of his inward enhancement. He tears through armoured soldiers and mounted knights. Breastplates fail under his strikes; horses and riders go down. The men who thought they were attacking a weakened dragon discover that the smaller body is still lethal.

Fifteen minutes later Malloch is dead, last on the rise with the spent stones. None of the people on the east road have been touched. Among the roughly five hundred who remained, nobody is left alive and wounded.

Imani and her team search the Moss for an hour and find no patient to save. The burial detail begins; some young knights vomit and nobody mocks them. Nell Gorse asks Ward about her missing son, whose name isn't on the surrender list. Ward tells her what is likely rather than giving false reassurance.

The engagement will be judged lawful, but its legality cannot make the outcome clean. Emeric sits by the road until Ward makes him eat. Imani confronts him about what the law permits someone with his power to do. He has no satisfactory answer.

Chapter 46 — The Door

The Black Moss changes Emeric's reputation beyond Kaldo. Armand studies the accounts and draws a troubling historical conclusion. Church histories have long claimed that dead ground defeated dragons, but Emeric knowingly entered a zone and destroyed an armed host. The zones alone cannot explain the ancient outcome. Perhaps ambush, numbers, and planning mattered more than the official histories admit. He doesn't yet know the name behind that conflict.

Quarrie's panel judges the Black Moss lawful: proclamation given, escape route protected, field command countersigned, enemy stones forced. There is no censure. It records, however, that nobody dying in the crater was luck, and that lawful and clean are different things.

Surviving mercenaries take a new name abroad. Foreign newspapers and, by May 7, pulpits in Ostrel call Emeric the gold-eyed demon king of Kaldo. Most people inside his own realm don't use it.

On the Isle, Brega and Petrok arrive from the Sun Shore to inspect the buried city and their dead. Ward joins the joint survey because Brega trusts him. Tess's bore maps, the Islanders' knowledge of sand, and Petrok's earth-and-shard senses identify an old outer wall and the lintel of a buried door. Brega and Petrok enter alone. Ward waits outside with his back to the opening.

After an hour they return. Brega asks for the door to be closed again. The Islanders refill it. No Kaldo official insists on knowing what the two saw. That decision belongs to the families with standing among the dead.

May 8 is day 120 of the note's transition. Qualifying old coin debts convert at par and can be paid in crown notes. Mabel settles a western factor's invoice in notes and obtains a receipt despite the agent's grumbling. Vantic's House begins recovering payments from the commercial claims it bought during the winter crisis. The March's discount on notes gradually disappears with Pryor's exchange system under interim public control.

On May 9, Petrok takes the orc families' chair on the Isle Works Review. The long-empty seat is filled. The review can finally decide the hollow's future.

Chapter 47 — Part Two

With the rebellion defeated, Emeric returns to the unfinished Isle Works Review. Petrok has joined, and the review can consider the hollow under the Isle. Emeric renews his request to install a listening post on its sealed cap. Something else may remain beneath it, and the warning about more than one such room still troubles him.

The review hears evidence from Tess on the Drifter core markings, including "born" and "under the Isle"; from Neris on unusual echoes; from Mother Sabbe and the Islanders on the silent listening coil; and from Brega about the buried doors. It considers the sealed warning from Ostrel concerning other rooms in the world. Petrok asks Emeric whether he holds back information that could alter their decision. Emeric says he cannot prove what he fears and will not demand a ruling on an unproven suspicion. It is true, but incomplete.

On May 26, the review meets at the Islanders' hall above the cove. It rules that the hollow is closed to every crown, Randillia's included. Access is controlled jointly by Islanders and the orc families. An iron cover is set over the existing cap with two locks requiring both keys. Mother Sabbe receives one key and Petrok the other.

Emeric's proposed listening post inside the sealed area is refused. Monitoring outside the fence may continue. The hollow is sealed, not burned or destroyed: whatever remains below is untouched, and Seyr's clock is not disturbed. The review cannot establish exactly what the hollow contains. It finds that uncertainty is a reason for restraint, not unrestricted opening.

Emeric objects once. The ruling denies him access to the very place connected to the danger in the Ostrel letter. But the findings are binding, and he signs with Harrold. That evening, he tells Ferra and Imani that the clock won't care what a review thinks. Imani points out that the hollow is sealed rather than destroyed, and someone holds the keys. Ferra says, "That's two." The first safeguard removed unilateral emergency power; the second removes a crown's control of the hollow. Emeric accepts the count.

Kesrath later accepts the restriction for Duvrenne's crown, asking for a witness whenever the hollow might be opened. The review's work is complete. Vantic also learns that her House lost around 7,000 Gold Rand in the winter intervention. Credit on the tower is repaid and the pledge released.

On June 8, the March Emergency Authorisation reaches its ninetieth day. No armed rebel force has remained in the field since Malloch's defeat. Ward reports that no renewal is needed. Emeric would prefer another thirty days, with Church activities and foreign paymasters unresolved, but does not ask for it. At midnight the authorisation expires. Ward returns the field-command seal to the Assembly's clerk. Nothing dramatic happens, and that is the point: a power granted for an emergency ends when the emergency ends.

Batch 6: The Courts

Chapters 48–57 | June – August, Year 19

Chapter 48 — The Report

The March Emergency Authorisation has expired, but its consequences remain. On June 12, Kaldo News prints Quarrie's complete report on Emeric's actions at Pryor's Gate, with Ward's testimony, the tower men's accounts, Hal Belk, Emeric, Runa's scent evidence, and the stable boy who corroborated her warning. Emeric had reasonable grounds to intervene early, but his gravity attack violated the authorisation because the proclamation hadn't been read at that gate. The inquiry cannot establish whether his roar increased danger to patients or changed the circumstances of Matt Lowrie's murder.

The Black Moss report appears alongside it. That engagement was lawful, though the report states plainly that lawful is not necessarily clean. The publications offer two different judgments on the same king: censured for one use of force, cleared for another.

Quarrie recommends the Rule on Royal Force be made permanent. The temporary authorisation has ended, taking its limits with it. Without new legislation, another emergency might be authorised without those restraints. Corbin and the crews' representatives introduce a standing rule requiring a proclamation at the place, written approval from the field commander before royal force is deployed, and an order filed with the reviewing officer for every future Assembly authorisation.

The Assembly passes it June 23. Emeric doesn't oppose it. He signs, knowing it binds him and his successors. Privately he tells Ferra that one day the rule may cost a king an opportunity he cannot recover. She answers that the next king may be much worse than him; that is why the protection is necessary.

Quarrie also rules on Davey Corve's individual application for pardon. He opened the infirmary corridor and helped move patients and conscripts out during the rescue. The qualifying pardon is granted and made public. The crown pays Hal Belk and the other tower men compensation for Emeric's unlawful gravity attack. Hal asks if the king will come personally. Emeric sends a letter instead, which Hal leaves unopened for a week.

The monarchy remains powerful, but another portion of its authority is now governed by rules the king cannot waive at will.

Chapter 49 — Hers

Maudie's civil case against Pelham's collectors finally reaches judgment at Cinderhale. The courthouse hearth where the Hearth burned the working rolls has been scrubbed clean. Maudie represents thirty-one named tenants and submits the official assessment register, marked payment slips, receipts, and written complaints Pelham's agents ignored.

Opposing counsel argues that the destruction of the rolls damaged evidence and created disorder. Maudie doesn't defend the fire. She acknowledges it made her work harder, then shows that the surviving records establish the tenants' claims without the destroyed rolls.

The court strikes Pelham's duplicate demands. Agents cannot collect money already paid. But legitimate assessments remain due: misconduct by collectors doesn't exempt tenants from lawful obligations. The court expressly refuses to endorse the Hearth's destruction of the records.

Maudie's victory does not become Corrick's vindication. She won with records, lawful procedure, and the testimony of people whose rights were violated.

On June 24, the midsummer quarter day, widow Tench makes the payment she truly owes. She pays in crown notes at par and receives a receipt. At home she places it in the tin beside her old demand slip. For the first time in months, the paperwork on her table reflects the law. The case belongs to Maudie and the tenants, not to the Hearth.

Chapter 50 — Grievance Isn't a Conspiracy

On June 26, the Hearth's criminal trial opens at Kestle. Keller recuses himself because of his earlier involvement with the Isle review and the Dunholt warrants. Judge Agnes Fairlie of the southern circuit presides, with a jury drawn from outside the accused's holding.

Corrick, Kezia, and Gethin face charges arising from the watch-house operation, Pelham's burned rolls, and the illegal intervention at Dunholt. The prosecution makes its strongest case. The Hearth organized covert operations, entered places without authority, removed or destroyed property, and took decisions belonging to courts and public institutions.

The defense doesn't deny the actions. It contests the broad conspiracy allegations and the assertion that the League itself was a criminal association. Maudie testifies about the genuine grievances driving the group, while refusing to treat grievance as a license to break the law. The broader conspiracy charge against League officers fails. Individual criminal actions still have consequences.

The prosecution tests the Hearth's covenant: that nobody innocent loses body, liberty, wages, home, property, work, or legal rights. Evidence from the watch house, the dues rolls, Dunholt's carriers, and the firearms is examined. No innocent loss is established within the defined categories. But the prosecutor asks what Corrick would say if another group, equally convinced of its righteousness, destroyed the League's hall. Corrick cannot provide a principle that licenses his group's unilateral authority but denies it to others.

Five Hearth members remain unidentified. Corrick refuses to name them, though he doesn't lie under oath. Kezia tells him he has decided for them too, just as he repeatedly decided for others.

Ward testifies about Corrick's report before Dunholt. In the corridor he confronts Corrick: with an earlier lawful warning, a detachment might have stopped Fulk in the March with guns and duke-linked witnesses. Corrick answers that no warrant existed until their illegal entry produced evidence, and the guns could otherwise have reached Pryor before Imani's kidnapping. Neither can prove the alternative course.

In a parallel courtroom, Sir Edwin Hartley's killer is convicted of murdering the unarmed orderly during Imani's seizure and sentenced to death. Corporal Piet Neate watches without the satisfaction he expected. The chapter ends with Ward and Corrick still disagreeing, neither able to prove his decision would have prevented more harm.

Chapter 51 — A Year

July brings sentences for the Hearth and the man who murdered Matt Lowrie. Davey Corve testifies against his own father, describing the powder cellar, the evacuation, and the shot that killed Matt as he tried to prevent the explosives being used. Murder is established. The bench also considers the father's later choice: he handed the match to Davey voluntarily and surrendered, preventing the fourteen bedridden patients from being killed. That does not erase Matt's death but affects the sentence. He receives twenty-five years rather than death.

Davey visits his father one last time. His father asks if Davey's mother is eating. Davey says she is. They have nothing else to say. Davey later refuses Imani's offer of hospital training, takes his owed pay, and returns home to the March. His future remains his decision.

On July 14, the sentencing bench rules on the Hearth. Corrick and Kezia get a year each; Gethin receives six months. They admitted unlawful entry, destruction, and taking evidence. Their motives, voluntary surrender, preservation of documents, avoidance of innocent injury, and prevention of the gun shipment mitigate the offenses, but do not grant them the authority they claimed. One judge publishes a dissent seeking longer sentences: careful wrongdoing is still wrongdoing.

Kezia's assistance in identifying the Monk's Rise during Imani's kidnapping is excluded from mitigating reasons at her request. Ward honours it. Sentences run from the day passed, without credit for remand. Corrick and Kezia therefore remain imprisoned until July of the following year, and Gethin until January.

Maudie calculates the full confinement periods, including time already spent at Kestle. Corrick and Kezia will serve roughly fifteen and a half months in all; Gethin, roughly nine and a half. She pins nominal and actual terms side by side on the League's wall. The Hearth is convicted, but the public can see what those sentences really mean. The three are transferred to Turnstone, the realm's open prison.

Chapter 52 — Neither

On July 18 and 19, Kaldo's hospital board examines Cal Pascoe's death. Imani asked for the inquiry. Sister Inga recounts the rescue, the medical teams' preparedness, and the conditions under which Imani accepted the relic inside the treatment hall.

The board reconstructs the facts. The case was weighed and sealed; a mage sensed its contents; Ward warned of the danger; Imani accepted the risk because refusing the religious ceremony might prevent or delay treatment for the March workers. The kidnapping removed the only surgeon with the hollow-view ability to locate Cal's damaged vessel. Imani tied it while deprived of sight, but the temporary repair needed further treatment. Cal died before it could be completed.

The board refuses either to absolve or condemn her entirely. It finds a defensible risk decision with a death in it. Imani accepts both parts. She will never call the impulse to save Cal wrong, but will not pretend the cost was zero. Sister Inga proposes a change: whenever possible, a temporary blind tie receives a visible mundane marking that a second surgeon can locate. The system must not depend entirely on one specialist.

Later in July, the Medical Pass Office opens in the March. It comes from Ward and Imani's unresolved professional disagreement. Ward wants armed escorts for medical teams, but those escorts could cause March families to reject treatment. Imani wants neutral passes, but unsearched passes could permit another Cuthbert to carry a concealed stone into a hospital.

The Office includes Ward, Imani, Mother Brannagh, a healer selected by March families, and an Assembly health-committee representative. It creates bilateral searches for blessed objects entering treatment areas and enforceable penalties against either side that breaks the rules. Brannagh insists that Cuthbert betrayed the blessing, not that the blessing itself was wrong. Ward and Imani each give up part of their preferred policy and sign. The institution exists because neither is permitted to settle the question alone.

Chapter 53 — Never Your Years

The capital courts begin judging the rebellion's leaders. Pryor's prosecution presents the hostage-taking, the powder cellar, the proposed transfer of Imani to Ostrel, and organized resistance to the crown. At Pryor's request, his hereditary charter brief is read in full.

He describes three centuries of roads kept open, widows supported, and families protected, arguing that an unequally composed Assembly could remove all those guarantees without the March's consent. The grievance is genuine. The court doesn't pretend otherwise. But it distinguishes the complaint from the decision to make war and imprison the queen. The jury convicts Pryor of treason and he is sentenced to death. The judgment acknowledges the Assembly's imbalance and says the answer is reform, not armed captivity.

Agatha attends. She remembers a pension clerk who could send grain to a family with a word, and Pryor refusing his own people a vote on their future. She can grieve the loss of a functioning institution while believing it must change.

Sayer's trial concerns the two orders issued after the Inspectorate served its entry order. Her courier produces the signed original he kept in his strongbox. Defense counsel attacks his motives: he concealed the paper to protect himself. His motives can be questioned, but the notary register independently records Sayer's instruction to resist with fire, dated two days after lawful service.

Ruth Penhale's son describes the attack on the commissioners' barge. The prosecution cannot prove that Sayer specifically ordered the four killings: the battery fired before the barge reached the chain, and the required intent for murder cannot be established against her. She is not convicted of murder. But the evidence proves intentional armed resistance to lawful entry, and she is convicted of treason.

Two of the three sentencing judges vote for death. The dissent would impose the longest prison term because the four deaths aren't attributed to her in law. Ruth speaks against the execution. Sayer gave her family a fair judgment once, but Ruth doesn't excuse the rebellion. The dead commissioners, she argues, need no additional body. The bench hears her and leaves the sentence unchanged. Sayer is condemned to hang.

Chapter 54 — No Royal Favour

On August 7, forty members of the League petition Emeric to pardon the imprisoned Hearth. Pim Barrow carries the request. Emeric meets them at the League's hall instead of the castle. He acknowledges he has the royal power of clemency. They ask him to use it, and he refuses.

He doesn't call Corrick or Kezia evil and doesn't dismiss their good work. He admires it. He would personally like to spare them prison. But overriding an independent court for people he likes would turn royal favour into a privilege stronger than equal application of law, the very condition the League is trying to end. He also believes Corrick would refuse such a pardon and Kezia would hand it back. The forty leave without clemency; some workers later accuse the king of praising the Hearth privately while letting its members suffer publicly.

That week, Kaldo's computing program reaches a milestone. In the Motion Shop, Merrin Sallis and the team assemble valve racks, a ferrite-core memory plane, and a card reader. A stored program performs arithmetic, tests, and loops inside the machine's own memory. Dov signs individual transistor boards only after proving their reliability.

The first useful job calculates the Issue Board's weekly redemption schedule for county offices. It finishes before lunch a task human clerks thought might take weeks. Those clerks haven't simply been dismissed: Ferra arranged training so they could participate in the new system. Emeric watches from the back. He knows more advanced ways to program the machine but doesn't interfere. The achievement belongs to the people who built it.

Elsewhere, Keller closes the inquiry into Cuthbert and possible central Church involvement. Cuthbert receives fifteen years, but the inquiry cannot prove that Church leadership ordered the kidnapping. The Sealed Confidences Article protects one source, Cuthbert's confessor. Other stone records, manifests, letters, and journeys were examined but remain inconclusive. Imani recognizes that the confidentiality rule she supported operated as intended and hates its result in this case.

On August 12, Emeric visits Corrick at Turnstone. He speaks of the constitutional transition he expects the realm to undergo, describing Corrick as being in its funnel. Corrick reminds him that Maudie has counted the months. Understanding what a system gets wrong isn't the same as repairing it. When Emeric says he knows, Corrick refuses to let knowing count as enough.

Chapter 55 — Nine to Three

Tullio's trial concerns two distinct offenses. The first is knowingly financing a prohibited private armed command after legal notice. His financial records and duplicate receipts prove it. The second, capital count requires proof that he intended to advance an armed rising.

The crucial evidence is his April 17 instruction: release the next tranche only while the guest remains at the Gate. The prosecution reads it as financing Imani's continued captivity. The defense says he was trying to keep her from being moved to Ostrel, where rescue and negotiations would be harder. Pryor's later attempted transfer against that condition makes the defense's explanation plausible. Neither interpretation can simply be dismissed.

Vantic sits behind her brother. She understands how evidence could condemn him and how much of his conduct remains connected to his belief that he was protecting the kingdom's finances.

The jury finds Tullio guilty of financing the prohibited command. On the capital charge the first ballot splits nine to three in favour of conviction. Further consideration produces no unanimity, and the court declares a mistrial on that count.

The independent prosecutor reviews the admissible evidence and decides another trial is unlikely to settle the intent question. With thirty years secured on the proven offense, the prosecutor formally declines to retry the capital count. It is a mistrial, not an acquittal; Tullio's capital intent remains legally unproven. Emeric cannot order a retrial and doesn't attempt it.

Tullio receives thirty years and loses the crown book. When the result is announced, Vantic's first reaction is relief, which she can barely admit. The evidence was enough for decades of imprisonment, not enough for the rope.

She visits him. Tullio blames Pryor and still insists he was protecting the kingdom's money. He is alive, imprisoned, and furious with her. Vantic leaves with her coat buttoned incorrectly and notices only when she reaches the street.

Chapter 56 — The Engine

The summer's final major trials concern Treloar and Wystan. Treloar is prosecuted for the nine deaths in Stannary's collapse. The Crown offers the mines inspector's written closure order, evidence of failing supports, and testimony from a Grayrock mining engineer. Kezia's signed earlier warning about the dangerous timbers is admitted. The defense attacks her credibility as a convicted saboteur, but the court evaluates the warning's date and measurements on their own merits.

Treloar's geologist argues that an old fault contributed to the collapse. The jury accepts that several causes were involved but finds Treloar's decision to keep working a stope formally closed for safety was a substantial cause of the deaths. He is acquitted of participating in the armed plot, convicted of gross-neglect homicide, and sentenced to twenty years. At Turnstone, Kezia reads the news and returns to her bench without celebrating.

Wystan's case concerns firearms illegally produced in his industrial works. The prosecution proves disguised freight, clandestine manufacture, ammunition specifications, and the route to Pryor's Gate. But it cannot automatically turn those facts into proof of Wystan's personal knowledge. The defense demonstrates delegated approvals and documents signed by Fulk Garside. The jury acquits him for want of proof that he knew. At least one juror visibly dislikes it.

The verdict leaves Fulk's disappearance unresolved. When Corrick calls him a coward, the accusation reaches Mabel through their business correspondence. She replies that neither knows whether her brother fled from guilt or because his life was in danger. Corrick apologizes for the accusation, not for doubting Wystan. Mabel keeps his letter.

At Kaldo's Sky Yard another technological milestone arrives. Sorrel watches the first bench run of the Mark 1 turbojet. Bram and Tess have assembled a centrifugal compressor, combustion chambers, and turbine, with a mundane starter and kerosene rather than magical propulsion. The compressor spins up, fuel ignites, and the engine's noise stops every hammer in the yard.

Tess watches the temperature as it runs for roughly four minutes. Approaching the test limit, Bram shuts it down. The test succeeds. Sorrel stands beside it with her hands over her ears. She hates the noise but understands that she will eventually fly the aircraft it powers. Technological progress continues as the courts finish judging the recent past.

Chapter 57 — The Hanging Morning

At the end of August, three death sentences remain: Pryor for treason, Sayer for treason, and Sir Edwin Hartley's killer for murder. Their automatic appeals reach the full bench and are dismissed August 28. The executions will proceed.

Imani opposes the death penalty. She addresses an Assembly committee, arguing that a securely imprisoned person is no more able to escape than her immobilized patients were. She asks what protection an execution provides when the condemned is already held under control.

Sir Edwin's sister answers. Her brother entered a hospital without weapons to protect injured people. The man who killed him deliberately stabbed an unarmed orderly. To her, the sentence is not merely a calculation about escape or future danger; it concerns the state's response to the dead and those they leave behind.

Neither woman persuades the other. The committee records both positions and does not change the courts' judgments.

On August 31, the executions take place in Kestle gaol's yard before the required witnesses and officials. The state deliberately avoids hanging Pryor at the Gate, where his execution site could become a shrine to his followers. Two men and one woman are hanged.

Imani doesn't attend. She spends the morning at the Medical Pass Office beside Mother Brannagh. A March midwife needs permission to cross into territory where she will treat patients. Imani reviews the documents and signs the pass; Brannagh completes her part. Neither mentions what is happening at Kestle.

Later that afternoon, Pryor's followers publish his charter brief again. His death hasn't ended the grievance he represented. The courts have completed their judgments, but Kaldo must still decide what kind of country it will become. Chapter 57 ends with Imani signing a medical pass while the state's executions take place elsewhere.

Where Book 3 Stands After Chapter 57

These chapters carry the story from the Isle Works Review's consequences through a currency crisis, an armed rebellion, a hostage rescue, a devastating battle, and the trials that follow.

By the end of August, Year 19, Emeric has surrendered or accepted substantial restrictions on unilateral emergency powers, control of the Isle's oil and hollow, personal military force, prosecutorial discretion, and the ability to disregard independent judgments. He remains king, but institutions now make important decisions without him, and sometimes against his wishes.

The political questions remain unsettled. The March has been offered representation but still suffers structural inequality. Cinderhale continues demanding a fairer state map. The Hearth's convictions expose the difference between justified grievances and lawful authority. The courts have punished the rebellion's leaders without proving the Church's central involvement.

The realm continues advancing. The crown note survived its first crisis; the new computing machine ran a stored program; the Mark 1 jet engine completed its first bench test; and the Medical Pass Office was established through compromise rather than a single ruler's decree.

Beyond politics, the mysteries remain. The Drifter mother is missing, and young survive outside Kaldo's knowledge. The hollow is sealed but not understood. The Church's history of dragons is increasingly suspect, and the name Ava is unexplained.

The next political confrontation is the Titles Act, which will test whether the representative system can be changed through the same lawful institutions now constraining Emeric. Chapter 58 begins there.
