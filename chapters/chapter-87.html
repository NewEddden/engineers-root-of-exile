Chapter 87 - The Ride He Did Not Take

He claimed his mother's stones in the morning, and the whole court thought it was grief, which was exactly what he needed them to think.

The claim was his by right, an old provision of the treasury that let a prince come of standing take an allotment from his own line's holdings, and he had come to the capital meaning to make it long before the title or the trap, because in a vault the crown had never bothered to guard well sat the one thing in the realm he wanted more than any coin and that no one else in the realm wanted at all. Amara's stockpile. His late mother had gathered, over a life no one had understood the shape of, a great quantity of what the court called hollow crystals, control stones that emitted no mana signature under any standard testing and were therefore filed by every mage and every assessor as defective, broken, worthless, the dead ends of the crystal trade, and she had kept them, quietly, in numbers, and when she died the stockpile had sat in the treasury as an oddity, a dead queen's inexplicable hoard of junk that no one claimed because there was nothing in it to want.

He claimed it, and he let the claiming look like exactly what the court wanted it to look like, a son reaching for a dead mother's things out of sentiment, a soft and slightly foolish act of grief, the marcher lord who conquered a duke going misty over a box of his mother's worthless keepsakes. He let them think it, and the thinking cost him nothing and hid everything, because the grief was also real, that was the part he had not expected and could not fully perform his way around, the ache of a name he did not get to say enough surfacing as he signed for the last physical thing of hers in the world, the same ache that had risen without paperwork when Corbin had once pressed a single one of these into his palm and said their mother had given him one once. She had given the whole realm's supply of the most important object in it to a vault and a legend of worthlessness, and no one had ever asked why the queen hoarded junk, and he thought he might be the only person alive who knew the answer, that a control stone read as worthless because the world tested it for the wrong thing, that its emptiness was not a defect but the whole point, root-level access wearing the disguise of a dead end, a fortune in the one resource his enterprise ran on sitting in plain sight behind the single most effective camouflage there was, which was that no one wanted it.

He walked out of the vault with it, and no one stopped him, and no one would have dreamed of stopping him, because a man does not guard the door a beggar is leaving through with a sack of stones no beggar would take. Corbin, hearing of it, found it faintly curious for exactly as long as it took him to file it under sentiment and dismiss it, because Corbin's whole read of the world could not accommodate a version where the hollow crystals were worth carrying, and that failure of imagination was the same failure that let a stockpile of treasure sit in a vault for years and let a marcher lord carry it out under the cover of grief. Emeric let him dismiss it. He wanted nothing more in that hall than to be dismissed.

Then it was time to leave, and leaving was where Maeve's warning came off the shelf and became the whole of his attention, because a plan aimed at a departure was a plan built on a predicted departure, and the departure they had predicted was standing in the courtyard with its horses already in the traces.

The carriage was fine. That was the thing about it, that it was fine, a good capital carriage sent to bear a new-made Marquis home in the honor his rank now demanded, and there was nothing to see wrong with it and every reason to climb in, and a man without a warning and without a way to check would have climbed in and been borne off toward the frontier trusting the wheels under him the way a man trusts a floor. He had the warning. He also had the way to check, the way no one in that courtyard knew he had, and he used it as a man might who was only making a last unhurried inspection of his conveyance, running a hand along the frame, resting his palm a moment on the wheel, the ordinary fussing of a lord in no hurry, and under the fussing he delved.

The stone was not in the carriage but he did not need a stone in the carriage, because he had grown these five years past the raw beginnings of the thing and could read a made object through contact the way he read anything made, the geometry of it, the forces in it, what had been done to it and where, and what had been done to it was in the axle. Someone had worked the axle. Not broken it, that would have been found, but weakened it with the particular cleverness of a man who wanted a thing to hold at a walk and at a mile and to fail at speed on the frontier road far from the capital's eyes, to fail as an accident fails, a wheel come off at a gallop on a bad stretch, a carriage overturned, a Marquis dead of the plain misfortune that takes travelers every year, no hand anywhere near it, no one to accuse, the deniable death the careful men preferred over any death that left a mark. He read the whole of it through his palm on the wheel and did not change his face, because the second most important thing after knowing was not letting the courtyard know he knew, the same discipline that governed the ink, that confusion about the method he could give the enemy freely and confirmation he would give no one, and a marcher lord who announced he had somehow divined the sabotage in his own axle was a marcher lord teaching his enemies exactly what he could do.

So he did the only thing that beat a plan built on a prediction, which was to make the prediction wrong. He did not refuse the carriage, refusing it was a tell. He used it. He had it loaded and sent off on the frontier road bearing his colors and his baggage and a pair of guards who understood the road and would keep to a walk and to the safe stretches and abandon the thing the moment it was decently out of the capital's sight, a decoy carrying his absence toward the ambush that expected his presence, so that whoever waited on that road for an accident would wait, and watch a Marquis's carriage roll past exactly on schedule, and learn nothing from its emptiness until far too late. Let them spring a trap on a box of baggage. Let them wonder, after, how the box had come to be empty.

He left the capital the way the capital had not arranged for, because he had brought the means to, because a man who planned for failure surfaces had planned for this one before he ever rode in. The small covert flight element had come with him to the capital folded into his party as servants and outriders, a contingency he had hoped not to spend and was glad past measure to have, and in the grey hour before the decoy rolled out they took him and Ferra up off a quiet roof at the city's edge and into the sky, and the capital fell away below them into its carved and beautiful snarl, and no one in it looked up, because no one in this world had learned yet to look up.

The flight home was not the clean thing the word escape wanted it to be, because nothing he built was clean and the sky least of all. They flew high to fly unseen, up over the cloud deck where no eye on the ground could track them, and high was where the cost of the sky lived, because mana thinned with altitude, thinned the higher they climbed toward the line where it failed entirely, and a flight suit ran on the mana it could draw, and the higher they flew to stay hidden the less there was to fly on, so that the whole passage home was flown in the narrow band between the height that hid them and the height that would drop them, thin cold air and thinning power, the suits laboring where lower they would have sung, every man in the element feeling his lift grow shallow and his margin grow small. It was a real danger and not a footnote, the escape route running along the edge of the physics that made escape possible at all, and they held the band the whole way, high enough to be a rumor and low enough to stay aloft, and it took everything the suits had and left nothing spare, and that was the price of a sky no one could follow them into, that it was also a sky that barely held them.

Ferra flew carried, and being carried was its own cost paid by the guard who carried her, both his arms and all his thrust committed to the holding so that he crossed the whole sky a defenseless man, and there was no one above the clouds to menace him and it was still true, still the same shape as every rescue and every extraction, that a person borne to safety was borne by someone who spent his own safety to bear them. She did not complain of it and she did not pretend it was comfortable, and somewhere over the dark folded country between the capital and the coast she said the only thing either of them said in the air, flat and cold and exactly right, that a court which raised a man at a banquet and rigged his axle before the banquet's plates were cleared had told him everything he needed to know about what the title was worth, and he did not answer, because there was nothing to add to a true thing.

He came down onto his own frontier in the first grey of the next day, marked and elevated and targeted and alive, carrying out of the capital a title that honored and bound him, a fortune in stones the whole realm believed was worthless, the certain knowledge that the capital had tried to kill him in a way he could never prove, and the shape, handed to him in a shut room by a sister who was nothing to the world and everything to him, of the faction that had done it. He could accuse no one. The axle was a decoy's problem now, sprung or unsprung on an empty road, and the men who had worked it would deny it and the denial would hold, because they had built it to hold, the same deniable machinery he watched Corbin use and the careful men behind the duke use, reached for now against his own life. He had won the war and been raised for winning it and been marked for the raising and nearly killed for the mark, all in the space of one visit to the place that had exiled him, and every piece of it was the same fact seen from a different distance, the fact that had opened the road and frightened the peers and climbed to the emperor's cold attention, the fact that he had become too large and too capable to be left alone, and that there was no longer any height he could reach that did not put him more plainly in the sight of the thing gathering, out past the frontier, to bring him down not by axle or by army, which had both failed, but by the one instrument left that could rule against a man the law could not touch.
