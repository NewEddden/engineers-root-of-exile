Chapter 84 - The Good Cloth

The summons to the capital came with a season's notice, and Emeric spent the first of it deciding not what to say when he got there, because he already knew that, but what to wear, which was the same decision wearing different clothes. He had gone up the roads once as a disgraced boy dressed to look smaller than his disgrace, and he had spent five years since being a man the world read as an ornament because being read as an ornament had been the thing that kept him breathing, and the reading had started as survival and settled into habit the way survival always did, until the habit was a second skin he had stopped noticing he wore. The notice made him notice it. He was going back to the room they had cast him down in, and they meant to lift him in it, and a man lifted in fine capital colors was a man saying the capital had made him, and Emeric had not been made by the capital. He had been made in the mud on the coast, out of a plague and a ledger and two years of letting good men be wrong on their way to being right, and he found, turning the notice over, that he was done presenting as less than that. The clothes would say it before his mouth had to. That was the whole of the brief, and it was a brief for a man he did not yet have.

He had the man by the end of the week, because the talent-finding channel he had built to turn up overlooked minds had turned one up months before and filed him under a use Emeric had not yet needed. Abloh had come out of a report the way half of Kaldo's best hands now came, named by neighbors who meant it as a complaint, a tailor in a Grayrock market town who ruined more cloth than he sold because he would unpick a finished coat three times over a seam no customer would ever see, who talked about a sleeve the way a mason talked about a load-bearing wall, who had opinions about the hang of a hem that he delivered as though the hem had wronged him. A strange man, the report said, in the tone the report always used, and Emeric had learned by now that strange in that tone meant a person the world had no slot for, and a person the world had no slot for was, often enough, a person built for a slot the world had not thought to make. He had brought Abloh to Kaldo, given him a room and a wage and a standing order to hire and to fail on the barony's coin until he stopped failing, and Abloh had built, out of that permission, a shop.

It was a real one by the time Emeric walked into it, not a court tailor's back room but a small works with the beginnings of a discipline in it, carders and spinners and two weavers at broad looms, a cutting floor where the good cloth was laid and measured and no scrap wasted, and over all of it Abloh, moving between the benches correcting angles of the wrist and tensions of the thread with the exact unrestful attention Emeric spent on a lathe. Emeric had told him the truth of what it was, which was that the barony would need a cloth trade one day, finished garments and not raw wool shipped off for other hands to make rich, and that this shop was the seed of it and would grow past the making of one man's coats. Abloh had heard the future in it and then set it aside, because Abloh did not care about the trade, Abloh cared about the coat in front of him, and Emeric had decided he could use a man who cared about the coat more than the empire the coat was seed to, because the empire would be built by men who thought about empires and the coat would only ever be right if someone in the room refused to think about anything else.

The design they worked out across three long evenings, and the working out was the part that mattered, because Emeric came in with a direction and Abloh came in with a craft and neither of them alone would have arrived at the thing they left with. Black, Emeric said, and gold, the capital's own colors thrown back at it, because the capital wore black and gold as though it had invented restraint, and Emeric meant to wear black and gold as though restraint were a thing he had considered and declined. And a device, he said, because every man in that room would carry his house on his chest, generations of blood worked in thread, and Emeric had no house and no blood worth the working, was a third son of a low-born mother the old lines had never counted, and he was not going to borrow a device or leave the space blank, he was going to make one, the way he had made everything else.

He told Abloh what it should be, and Abloh, who did not know the half of what he was building it for, built it exactly. A rose, gold, coming up out of black ground. Not a rose given but a rose grown, briar and thorn and bloom all at once, the ugliness kept in with the beauty because the ugliness was the point, a thing that had climbed up out of ruin and armed itself with thorns on the way. It was the truest sentence Emeric could say about Kaldo without opening his mouth, that what he had was made and not inherited, engineered out of wreckage by hands that had chosen to build rather than born to own, and when Abloh laid the first worked sample on the bench, gold vine crawling up out of a black that drank the lamplight, Emeric looked at it longer than he meant to. He had spent his childhood making himself less to stay alive and his adulthood letting a town believe he was hollow, and here was a device that said the opposite of both, and the strange lurch under his ribs when he looked at it was the recognition that he was about to stop hiding, and that stopping would cost him the safety the hiding had bought, and that he was going to do it anyway.

The black fought them. Emeric had known it would, and Abloh discovered it the hard way, which was the way Abloh discovered everything. Kaldo could weave a cloth as fine as any in the realm now, but a black that stayed black, deep and even and holding its color through wear and light and season, wanted a chemistry the barony did not yet have, mordants and fixings Kaldo could only half do, and the vats came up again and again a shade short, a black that was truly a very dark brown in a hard light, or a black that took the dye unevenly and blotched, or a black that held for a fitting and greyed at the first wearing. Abloh unpicked and redyed and swore at the vats as though they were men who had promised him something and reneged, and the black they finally kept was expensive past any sense, three lots wasted for every one used, and it was still, Emeric saw and Abloh saw and neither said, not quite perfect, a fraction warm where it should have been absolute. Emeric filed the lack where he filed all such lacks, under a problem with a shape, and the shape of this one was a chemistry he did not have and would need, and he thought, without yet knowing he was thinking toward a mountain and a woman who held it, that somewhere there was a source of the acids and the fixings that would make a true black cheap, and that when he found it the cloth trade and a great deal else would turn on it. For now the black was a cost, the one thing in the whole outfit that was not solved, and he wore the cost the way he meant to wear the rest of it, openly.

The vest was where the craft went quiet and clever. It read, when Abloh had it built, as a thing between clothing and armor, close at the waist and high at the throat and firmer than a vest had any business being, and a careful eye in the capital would look at it and wonder, and that wondering was allowed, because the truth under it was plainer than the wondering would guess. It was cloth. Layered and quilted and packed close, a stiff dense body of it built up the way a padded jack was built, cut to sit like tailoring and to turn a careless blade or a shoved elbow like a coat of plates, and it hid nothing magical because there was nothing magical in it, no crystal, no rune, no thing that could be starved by a dead zone or cut by a mage who knew the counters, only good cloth folded and stitched into a shape that happened to be hard. Emeric had been firm on that. He would not wear a protection that died when the mana died, would not build his own body into the single-point fragility he refused to build into anything else, and so the vest under the gold roses was mundane to its last thread, a thing a smith with no gift at all could make again from the pattern, and the roses spilled down the front of it thick enough that the eye followed the flowers and forgot to ask why the flowers sat on something so unyielding.

The sword they dressed last and dressed as a lie that told the truth. A real blade, balanced and edged, and around its guard Abloh and a metalworker between them wound a tangle of gold roses and thorned vine, blooms of gold and a deep worked blue climbing the base of the steel until the weapon looked like a thing left in a garden long enough to be claimed by it, an ornament, a rich man's toy, a decoration a wastrel might carry to a banquet because it was pretty. It would fool no one who knew how to look and it would fool everyone who did not, which was the entire population of the room split exactly along the line Emeric cared about, and the beauty of it was not a disguise over the function but the same thing as the function, because that was what he was and had always been, a working thing wearing decoration, a man the capital had read as ornament for five years who was about to stand in front of them and let them keep reading him as ornament right up until the moment the reading failed.

He had them make a second set, and the second set was Ferra's, and hers he specified with as much care as his own because she was not going to the capital as a decoration however much she meant to be mistaken for one. He was bringing her because the capital was people and people were the one system he could not read, the blind spot he had carried since the coast and would carry to the grave if he did not post a guard on it, and Ferra was the sharpest reader of a room he owned, and he was walking her into the one room he could not read himself and dressing her so the room would look right past her while she took its measure. So her gown was built as much as sewn, Abloh laughing for the first time in the whole commission when he understood what she wanted, gold drawn down the black in long deliberate lines like the ruled columns of a ledger made lovely, a strict gold cross laced taut across the waist so the whole thing had an armored line under its elegance, panels edged in gold like plates, and under the long fall of the skirt, where a court lady would show a slipper, dark structured boots, because Ferra had told Abloh flatly that she had never once in her life dressed to be unable to move and did not intend to start in a room full of people who might make her regret it.

Abloh finished the last of it the night before they rode, and he laid the two sets out on the long cutting table under the good lamps and stood back from them with his arms crossed and his face doing the thing it did when a seam had finally stopped wronging him, and Emeric stood beside him and looked at the clothes he had spent a season deciding to wear. They were not a disguise. That was the whole of what he had built, across the shop and the device and the fought-for black and the clever cloth and the pretty lethal sword, a wardrobe that was the exact opposite of every stitch he had worn for five years, because everything before had been cloth chosen to make him smaller and this was cloth chosen to make him exactly his own size. He had dressed as less than he was for so long that dressing as himself felt, standing there, like a kind of exposure, and he made himself hold the feeling and name it and keep it, because it was the correct feeling, it was the feeling of a man taking off the thing that had kept him safe, and he was choosing to take it off in front of the people it had kept him safe from. The self was the message. He had spent a season sewing it into gold thread so he would not have to say it aloud, and in the morning he would put it on and carry it up the carved roads into the room that had unmade him, and let it speak.
