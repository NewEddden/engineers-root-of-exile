Book 2, Chapter 29: The Long Night

Lord Cassel came back to Kaldo on the Monday, the eighth of June, on the regency's business, and Harrold met him in the long room at ten and watched him not look at Amara.

It was the paper mill again, and the river rights above Wayford, and a letter from the capital's council about the tram that two lords wanted and one didn't. Cassel had brought it himself rather than send it. He was a narrow, careful man of about Emeric's age with his father's long jaw and a good grey coat, and he set out his papers on the table in the order he meant to discuss them and discussed them in that order, and Harrold, who'd sat across tables from a great many men with papers, liked him for it more than he'd expected to.

Amara came in at eleven with the Works' figures for the mill's new boiler, because Tess had asked her to and because she'd decided, Harrold suspected, to come in.

She was in the grey dress with the high collar and the gloves. She put the figures down at Harrold's elbow and said good morning to the lord from the capital, and the lord from the capital stood up, and bowed, and said good morning, my lady, in a perfectly level voice; and then sat down and went on with the river rights.

He didn't go white. He'd gone white in April, at Tamsy Brook's, over his fish, and asked Saban about her the next morning with a coin in his fingers. He didn't do it again. He watched her. Not rudely; the way a man watches a thing he's decided to understand before he says anything about it. And he was polite to her. When she spoke about the boiler he listened to her and answered her and called her my lady each time, and didn't look at her face any longer than he looked at anyone's.

Harrold didn't explain anything. Saban's rule: never deny, never explain. He gave Cassel a room in the castle's east wing for the week, because the mill would take a week, and Cassel thanked him and took it.

"He knows," said Amara afterwards, in the corridor.

"He thinks he knows."

"He's deciding what he thinks." She pulled her gloves straight. "Let him. He's the kind that'll take a week over it."



Declan's call to Ward went through the line room at four that afternoon, and Harrold was in the room for it, because Declan had asked him to be.

He'd asked properly. He'd come to the long room on the Friday with Osric beside him and asked Osric's leave and then the regent's, as the seal required, to take the home knights up onto the ridges above the river at night, on drills. Three nights. Up the gorge's north side and along the top above the old bridge and the dam. Because, he'd said, standing at the end of the table with his hands behind his back, that was where he'd put mages if he were the Church. High, above the water, where they could see the whole valley and nothing on the ground could reach them fast.

Harrold had given it. Osric had given it. And now Declan wanted Ward.

The line room was the fibre's end, a long cool room under the castle with the boards down both walls and Ambrose's people at them with headsets, and the big set at the end that reached the relays. Ambrose put the call through himself. It went up to the net and out along it, west, a long way, and came back with the hiss of all that distance under it, and Ward's voice in it, from the K Amara's bridge deck at the Grey Mark.

"Ward."

"Captain. It's Declan." He'd never stopped calling Ward captain. "I've the regent's leave and Osric's for night drills on the ridges above the river. Three nights, from tonight. Mage hunting. I'm asking if it's right."

There was a pause on the set. Not long. Ward's never were.

"Where would you put them," said Ward.

"The north side. Above the old bridge and the dam. High."

"Then that's where you drill." The set hissed. "It's right. Do it in the dark, with the glass. Do it till your knights can find a man on that ridge with their eyes shut."

"Sir."

"Declan."

"Sir?"

"You were right about the line in April," said Ward. "And about the drill in May. I'm told." A pause. "Keep being right."

Declan stood at the big set with the headset on and his face doing nothing, and Harrold, at the end of the room, saw his ears go red.



The drop came at eleven minutes past one on the Wednesday morning, the night of the Tuesday, and nobody in Kaldo knew it was coming until it had come.

Harrold learned afterwards how it had been done, from Saban, sitting in the long room at noon with the relay's log in front of them both. At four minutes to one the relay over the western approach, the one the Church's route would cross, had beaten its beat as it always did. Ten seconds after that beat something had burst a dead zone on it at Band height. The relay had dropped. A zone at that height, in that thin air, closed in minutes; and the relay had caught again and beaten again before its next beat was missed, and gone on beating. Its last gasp had gone up the chain to the Well, and Orla's night board had logged it. A relay that dropped and caught wasn't a dead relay. It went in the book as a fault, for the morning. Nobody raised the city.

A quarter of an hour.

"That's what they bought," Saban had said. "With the listener. They'd learned our beat. They knew to the second when that drum would talk and when it'd go quiet." He'd put his finger on the log. "If I'd changed the beat in May, when we heard them, they'd have known they'd been heard. And they'd have done something else. Something I couldn't see."

"Would you change it now?"

"I'd do it again," Saban had said. "The same. I'd like that minuted."

But at eleven past one Harrold didn't know any of that. He knew that he was asleep, and then he wasn't, because the castle's bell was going and somebody was running in the corridor, and through his window the sky over the north of the city was full of hulls.



He saw them from the window in the half-dark: four, five, long and black and slow, low over the city, coming in from the north-west with no lights. And under them, round them, the Shell.

It wasn't finished. It wouldn't be finished for a year. Bram's core had grown enough of it to hold some of its faces, and the faces it held stood up over the city in the dark like the panes of a great cracked bell, faint, glassy, catching the street lamps from below; and between them the dark faces, the ones the core couldn't carry yet, were simply nothing. Air. The hulls were coming over those. Every one of them over a dark face, where nothing stood.

All but one. One came in through a lit face at the north, slowly, at a walking pace, and the face let it through. It was too slow to be anything. That was what the Shell had been taught: a fast thing was a missile and a slow thing was a bird. A hull at a walking pace was a cloud.

And then everything they dropped, they dropped from inside.

The drop teams came down out of the hulls on lines and on wings, in the dark, onto the city. And with them, falling first, scattered over the streets in a great ragged pattern, the dead zones.

Harrold felt them land. He felt every one of them: a slap of nothing on the air, here, and there, and there, all over the north of the city, the stone lamps in the castle corridor going out one after another down its length; and his own light, the thing in him that was always there under his skin, going small and hard and far away like a fire seen from a hill.

And the streets stayed lit.

He stood at the window and watched them stay lit. Every lamp on the Gate Avenue, every lamp in the lanes below the castle, every window in the Works across the river, white and steady. Not one went out. Kaldo's lamps didn't run on stones. They ran on wire from the Works' dynamos, and a dead zone couldn't touch a wire. The Church had dropped darkness on a city that didn't use stones for its light.

He could see the drop teams in the lit streets. Every one of them.

Then, from the harbour, the first gun. And from the Answer Tower on the hill, the second.

Along the castle's east wing, one window was lit, and a man in a nightshirt was standing in it with both hands on the sill, looking out. Cassel.



The line room was the first thing they went for, and Ambrose held it; and Saban, at the harbour set, held everything going out, so that nothing the Church might be listening for left Kaldo that night but what he chose.

Harrold heard that afterwards too. He didn't go there. He went where Amara was going, which was the dome.

She met him at the castle's postern in the dark, out of the corridor where the lamps had died, in the golem; and the golem was changed. He'd seen it fitted and seen it shot at, in the yard at the Works in the winter, and he'd never seen it like this: the helm closed. A smooth curved shell had come down over the face he knew and locked, and on its front, low, where the mouth would be, there was a small dull red glow.

"The lamp," said Amara's voice, out of the shell, flat and close. "Tess's. The filament one. It sees heat. It doesn't care about stones." The shell turned toward the north, toward the dark patches across the city where the zones had fallen and the stone lamps were dead. "I can see them. In the dark bits. All of them."

"The dome," said Harrold.

"The dome."

They ran.

The hush mines had come down on the dome's quarter in the first minute. Harrold found that out on the way: a squat black thing like an iron loaf, lying in the gutter of the dome lane, with every stone lamp for twenty yards round it dead and the glass of the nearest window gone dull. The drop teams were throwing them. At the line room's vents, at the harbour's stone-fed gear, at the dome's own lamps and pumps and the stones in its frame. A hush mine killed every stone near where it landed. It didn't kill anything else.

Amara's heart was a stone. Sealed, in its housing in the golem's chest, where Tess had put it. One of the mines landed in the lane ten yards from her as they came round the corner. Harrold saw her stagger. Then he saw her not stop.

"Still here," said Amara, and went on.



The dome stood at the end of its lane in its yard of gravel with its glass catching the street lamps, enormous, a hill of glass, and in it, under it, the tree.

The densest air in Kaldo. You felt it from the gate: a weight in the chest, a richness, like walking into a warm kitchen out of the snow. And somewhere under that glass, on her bed of moss at the tree's roots, Amara's own body, sleeping, healing, a little more every month, in the one place in the world that could do it.

The drop teams were in the yard. Twenty of them. More coming down on lines out of the dark above, where a hull hung over the quarter with no lights, a long black shape against the stars.

Amara went into them.

Harrold had known her since they were young. He'd seen her fight as a tower mage, terrible and precise. He'd never seen this. The golem went across the gravel at the drop teams faster than anything that size should move, and where it went men went over; she didn't cast. She didn't need to. Through the helm's dull red eye she saw them in the dark corners of the yard where the hush mines had killed the lamps, every one, the heat of them, and she went to each one in turn. One of them threw a cast at her, a spear of white, and it hit her chest and spread and went out, and she didn't slow.

Harrold fought.

He was a knight. He'd been a knight before he was a king, and he'd been one again since his stone, and he had the light. Not here; the zone they'd dropped on the dome was over the yard and over him, and his light was a small hard spark far down inside him that he couldn't reach. He had his sword and his body. He used them. He stood in front of the dome's door with his back to the glass and the tree and her body behind it, and anybody who came at the door met him.

He didn't have to. He knew that while he did it. The golem could look after itself. The dome had its own stones and its own pumps and Orla's people. Amara's body was asleep under a hill of glass. There was no reason for a man to stand in front of a door with a sword when nobody had asked him to.

He stood there.

And the zone over the dome died.

He felt it go. It went in minutes: not from the edges, the way zones went at sea, but all at once, swamped, as if somebody had poured a bucket of water on a candle. The dome was the densest place in Kaldo, and the Church had known that, and hadn't known how dense. The air round the tree simply drowned the zone. And his light came up in him again, all at once, like a breath he'd been holding.

The stone lamps in the dome's frame came back. The yard lit.

And over the dome, the hull opened its belly.



Amara saw it before he did.

She was in the middle of the yard with a man on the ground at her feet, and her helm came up, and she said, "No," quite loudly, and then she went.

Up.

Harrold had seen the golem fly. He'd seen it lift off the Works' yard in the winter on Tess's test, slowly, a few hundred feet, and come down again. This wasn't that. She went up off the gravel like a stone out of a sling, with the two armoured canisters on her back roaring, both of them, white, and the whole yard lit by her going. Straight up. Into the dark at the hull.

The whole city saw her. He thought of that afterwards. Every window in Kaldo was lit and every face in every window was turned to the sky, and up through the middle of it went a woman, a shape of a woman, on two columns of white fire, faster than anything, into the black over the dome.

She hit the hull.

He saw it from the ground. A flash, high up; and then the long black shape tilted, and something went out of it in a burst of sparks, and it began, slowly, to turn over.

But it had let go.

He saw them falling. Not one thing. Many. Out of its belly as it turned, before she'd reached it: bright points, a dozen, more, coming down out of the dark over the dome on long curving paths, some glowing, some not. Guided casts. He could see them correcting as they fell, bending toward the dome's light the way moths bend toward a lamp. And among them, darker, heavier, falling straight, the bombs.

Amara was up there with the hull. She couldn't catch them. Nothing could catch all of them.

He lit the sky.

He didn't think about it. He'd been holding the light down since the zone died, and he let it go, up, out of him, all of it, straight up into the dark over the dome's quarter: and the night went white. As bright as noon. Every roof and street and chimney in the north of Kaldo standing out sharp in it, and the sky over them blue-white, and in that sky, plain as a flock of birds, every falling thing.

The guns saw them.

The harbour battery. The Answer Tower on the hill. The two flak towers at the Works. They'd been firing at shadows, at hulls they could hear and not see, for ten minutes; and now there were targets in a white sky, falling, slow, one after another, and Kaldo's gunners did what they'd drilled all winter. The sky over the dome filled with bursts. The first falling things went in flashes, high up. Three. Five. More.

Not all.

The guided casts were still coming. Eight of them, perhaps, bending in toward the dome's light. And the dome was lit, a hill of glass catching his light and throwing it back.

So he moved it.

Mirage was his craft. It always had been. He took the light the dome was throwing off and bent it, and laid the dome's image down two hundred yards to the east over the empty ground of the old drill field: a hill of glass, lit, standing in the field, exactly where nothing was. And he dimmed the real one. He took its light away into himself and held it there.

The guided casts turned.

He watched them do it. They'd been coming at the dome and they saw the dome over the drill field, and they bent, every one, and went to it, and came down on the empty field in a long ragged stitch of fire, one after another, and burned grass.

And then there was one left.

He saw it last of all because it wasn't bright. A dark thing, heavy, falling straight down out of the white sky onto the dome's crown. A casing. An overloading stone in a steel shell, no guidance, no light, nothing to follow but the line it had been dropped on, and nothing to stop it. It didn't care where the dome looked like it was. It was going to hit where the dome was.

The guns were reloading. Amara was a mile up with a falling hull. His light was useless; light didn't stop a stone in a steel shell.

Only a body would.

He didn't decide it.

He was standing on the gravel in front of the dome's door with his sword in his hand, and then he wasn't standing on anything. He was going up, through the air, through the glass, and he was enormous. He felt it all at once, everything, the whole of it, from the jump: the size of himself, the weight, the wings opening off his back and catching the air with a crack like a sail filling, his body long and pale and burning with light from inside, the scales on it white as the inside of a shell, his neck stretching, his head turning up to the thing falling at him.

He put himself over the crown.

Wings spread. Both of them, wide, over the glass, over the tree, over her body, as far as they'd reach. And took it.

It hit him in the back between the wings. He felt it go, all of it, everything a stone holds when it's forced to let go of it at once, against his scales; heat and light and a blow like the floor of the world coming up. Glass went everywhere. He heard the dome's panes go under him in a long rolling crash, and felt the crown's ribs take his weight and hold, and his own body shudder all through, every scale.

And then it was over, and he was lying across the top of the dome on the cracked ribs with his wings fallen round it, smoking, and the glass still falling, tinkling, into the yard below.

He looked down through the broken panes.

The tree stood in the dome under him, its branches lit, untouched. And at its roots, on her bed of moss, under a layer of fine glass dust that had come down on everything like frost, Amara's body, sleeping, exactly as it had been.



He couldn't change back.

He tried, lying there. He reached for himself, for the man, and it wasn't there to reach. He was this, all of it, and the thing that had put him into it hadn't left a door open behind it. He'd come in all at once without asking. He didn't know how to go out.

He lay across the dome's crown and breathed, and every breath hurt along his back where the blow had gone in, and the white light was going out of the sky as his own light went out of him; and the city was coming back, the guns firing at the last of the hulls going away north, and below in the yard, men shouting.

Amara came down out of the sky beside him.

She landed on the dome's ribs on her two feet, light, as she'd landed on the Works' yard in the winter, and the canisters on her back hissed and went quiet. They were spent; he could see that. She'd climbed on both of them, all the way, and come back on what was left. And on her chest, on the round dull plate over the stone that was her heart, the plate Tess called the catch face, there was a dent. Deep. A fist-sized crater in it, still glowing faintly red at its edges, where the hull's last fire had come down on her as she hit it, and the plate had held.

Her helm opened. The shell drew back off the face he knew.

She looked at him. At the whole of him, lying across her dome, white, smoking, enormous, with his wings fallen round the glass and his head on the ribs beside her feet.

He looked back at her out of a face that wasn't his.

"You missed one," he said.

His voice came out of him low and huge and strange, and it was still his.

Amara put her hand on his neck, flat, on the white scales, and left it there.



On the Wednesday morning Tess had the catch face off the golem and on the Works' bench by seven, under the big lamp, and turned it over in her hands, and looked at the dent without saying anything.

"Not the castle gate, then," she said.



Corbin had been in Doran's old chapel since ten, and at a quarter past one he still hadn't finished.

It was a small stone room at the end of the lane under the castle, with six benches and a plain table and one window, where Doran had said his offices for years before anyone made him a cardinal, and where he still came on a Tuesday night because, he said, Saint Edwy's was too big for a man to think in. Corbin had found him there in the spring, by accident, and come back. Most Tuesdays since. He hadn't told anyone. He didn't think Doran had either.

They'd been talking about Augustine.

They talked about Augustine most Tuesdays. Corbin talked; Doran listened, and asked a question now and then, and let the silences go on as long as Corbin needed them to. Tonight it had been the last winter. What Augustine had said to him, in the cathedral's little room, about his brother. What he'd believed. How easily. What he'd done because of it.

"I want to know," Corbin had said, "whether it's a thing a man like me gets. Redemption. Or whether it's for people who were only stupid." He'd been looking at the table. "I wasn't only stupid. I wanted to believe it. I wanted him to be what Augustine said he was."

Doran had been quiet a while.

"That's the part you need to finish saying," he'd said. "Go on."

And Corbin had opened his mouth to go on, and the castle's bell had started.

They'd gone to the door. The sky to the north was full of shapes. Then the guns. Then, a few minutes later, a light coming up over the north of the city that turned the night white, and in it, small and high, a thing going up into the dark on two columns of fire, and Corbin had stood in the chapel's doorway with his mouth open and known exactly who it was and not let himself think about what it meant that the whole city was watching.

And then, from up the river, the blast.

It wasn't like the guns. It came down the gorge from the west, a long deep shuddering boom that he felt through the lane's cobbles into his feet, and the chapel's one window rang in its frame; and after it, under it, a rumble. Not stopping. Getting louder.

Corbin went to the end of the lane, where it came out over the river.

The gorge ran down from the west between its two high ridges to the old bridge and the town, and at its head, three miles up, the dam stood across it: the new one, the first stage, half full since April, with the plant house at its foot where the liquid mana store was. He could see where the dam was by the lamps along its crest. They were going out. And over the dam's crest, where the lamps were going out, something white and enormous was standing up against the sky.

Water. The reservoir, coming over.

And then the second blast: the plant house at the dam's foot going up, a bloom of green light that lit the whole gorge from end to end for an instant, and after it the air. He felt the air change. It came down the gorge in a wave ahead of the water, thick, rich, so dense that his own stone woke in his chest and every hair on his arms stood up. The liquid mana store. The whole of it, gone up into the gorge at once.

And in that thick air the water was doing something water didn't do.

It was stacking. It came over the broken dam and down the gorge, and instead of spreading, it climbed. It drew itself up. He could see figures on the ridges above it, small and dark against the white sky, on both sides of the gorge, high, where Declan had said they'd be, with their arms up, and the water climbing for them. Higher. A wall. A wave, coming down the gorge between the ridges at the town, growing as it came, the whole reservoir stood on its edge and pushed.

Three miles. He thought: a few minutes. Less.

Below him, on the river's near bank, was the lower ward. Thousands of people, asleep or at their windows looking north at the light in the sky. They weren't looking west.

He walked down to the bridge.



He didn't run. He didn't know why not. He walked down the steps from the end of the lane to the old bridge, the stone one, the oldest thing in Kaldo, where the gorge came out between its ridges at its narrowest and met the town; and out along it to the middle, to the crown of its arch, with the river black under him and the rumble coming down the gorge at him getting louder all the time.

Footsteps behind him. He turned his head.

Doran. In his old black cassock, with his hands in his sleeves, coming out along the bridge after him at an old man's pace.

"Go back," said Corbin.

"You hadn't finished," said Doran, and stopped a yard from him, and stood there.

Corbin looked up the gorge.

It was coming round the last bend. White at the top, in the white sky's light, and black under that, a wall of water higher than the bridge's towers, higher than the castle's lower curtain, filling the gorge from ridge to ridge, with a sound in front of it like the end of the world. A mile. Less. He could feel the thick air it was pushing in front of it go past him, over him, into him, and his stone woke up in him and opened like a hand.

He didn't know what he was going to do.

He knew that he had to be still. That was the only thing he knew. If he could be perfectly still, all of him, and make the thing in front of him still too. Make it stop. Hold it. Not push it; nothing could push that. Hold it where it was, the way you'd hold your breath. He'd been holding his breath his whole life. He'd been still in front of his father and still in front of Augustine and still in front of his brother, and he'd hated it every time, and he knew how.

The wave came round the bend.

And something in him went out to meet it that wasn't him.

He felt his back split. Not hurt; split, the way a seed splits. Something came up along his spine, hard and cold and clear, out of his skin, through his shirt, along his shoulders, ridged, and he knew without looking that it was crystal. He felt things fold against his back, heavy, tight, and lie flat. His hands on the bridge's parapet had changed; he could see them in the white light, clawed, scaled at the knuckles, with a shine on them like ice. He wasn't any bigger. A man's height. A man's shape, still, mostly, standing on the bridge's crown in the shreds of his shirt with crystal along his spine and his wings folded tight behind him.

And in front of him, he made the world stop.

He didn't know how. He'd tell Ferra later that he didn't know and she'd write it down. He looked at the wave and he was still, absolutely, all of him, and he made the still go out of him into it; and it went.

The wave stopped.

It stopped above the bridge. The whole face of it, from ridge to ridge, frozen. Not ice; water, still water, glassy, standing up into the sky higher than the bridge towers in a great curling wall, every drop of it held where it was, with the spray at its crest hanging in the air like thrown glass and the white light of the sky shining through it green. A quarter of a mile up the gorge, and over the lower ward's first roofs, and stopped. Not a sound. The rumble had gone. The roar had gone. There was only the river under the bridge, running quietly as it always had, and the night, and the held wall of water standing over the town.

Doran said something behind him. Corbin couldn't turn his head. He couldn't move anything. If he moved, it would fall.

"Don't," said Corbin, through his teeth. "Don't touch me."

"I won't," said Doran.



The city saved itself. He watched it do it.

That was all he could do: watch. He stood on the bridge's crown with his clawed hands on the parapet and held the wave, and everything else, all night, was other people.

Tess was first. She came along the river road from the Works at a run with a field set on her belt and two of the works crews behind her with a cart, and stopped at the end of the bridge and looked up at the wave, and at him, for about the space of a breath. Then she shouted to him.

"Can you hold it?"

"Yes."

"How long?"

"I don't know."

"Then hold it," said Tess, and turned round and started shouting at the crews.

The old marsh. He heard her say it. North of the river below the gorge's mouth, where the land had been drained when his grandfather was a boy and the old dykes still stood; if they blew the dyke at its head, the marsh would take water. Not all of it. A lot. The crews went at a run up the north bank with the cart. Twenty minutes later there was a flat hard bang from the north and a column of earth went up in the white light and came down, and Tess was back at the end of the bridge with her set.

"It's open," she shouted. "The cut's open. Now let it down. Slowly. A hand's width. On my count."

He didn't know how to let it down a hand's width. He tried. He let a little of the still go out of him, a very little, at the foot of the wall, where it met the water still in the gorge; and the foot of the wave moved, and ran, a hand's width of it, sideways into the open cut, and he caught the rest and held.

"Good," said Tess, across the water. "Again. On my count. One. Two. Now."

He let it down a hand's width.

Armand's students came at two. The ones who'd stayed in Kaldo when their master sailed, in their grey coats, out of the tower's annex on the hill; Corbin knew one or two of their faces from the long table. They went to the old sluices along the river's banks below the gorge, the ones the mill owners had used a hundred years ago and nobody had opened in twenty, and opened them, one at a time, with crowbars and their own hands and what water magic they had, so that every hand's width he let down had somewhere to go that wasn't the lower ward. One of them shouted figures to Tess. Tess shouted counts to him.

A hand's width. Hold. A hand's width.

All night.

His arms shook. His legs shook. The crystal along his spine grew cold and then colder and then he couldn't feel it at all. The still went out of him a little at a time on Tess's count and every time it went he thought he'd lose the rest of it with it, and every time he didn't.

Doran stayed on the bridge. He didn't say anything. He stood a yard behind Corbin with his hands in his sleeves, all night, and when Corbin's legs went at about three he didn't touch him; he only said, quite quietly, "Stand up," and Corbin stood up.



The ridge was above him and he couldn't look at it. He heard it.

He heard the rifles first, high up on the north side, a long way off: the knights' self-loaders, the flat hard crack of them, many, all along the ridge's top. Declan's knights, on the ridge where they'd drilled the night before, going for the mages. He heard the casting stop, up there, where the figures had been holding their arms up to the water. One. Then others. The water they'd stood up into the wave stopped pushing against him from behind, and the whole held wall went easier in his hold, a little, all at once, and he knew the knights had done it.

Then something else on the ridge. A roar, not a man's. And the rifles again, faster. Then quiet.

Rylan came down to the bridge at four.

He came along the river road from the ridge path with three knights behind him, walking, slowly, and one of the three was carrying something over his shoulders. Rylan stopped at the end of the bridge and looked at the wave, and at Corbin, and didn't come out along the bridge. He spoke from there, so that Corbin could hear him without turning.

"The mages are down," he said. "All of them on the north side. Eleven. Two on the south, taken by the tower's people." His voice was very level. "They had a juggernaut. On the ridge, with them. Guarding them. We shot its handler, the one in the grey robe, the way Arthur did in the winter. It didn't stop. Its last order was to kill whatever came up the ridge. It came for us."

Corbin held the wave.

"Declan held it," said Rylan. "While we finished the mages. He went at it and held it and we finished them. Then we drained it. It's alive. We have it in chains on the ridge." He stopped. "It killed him. Before we could drain it. He'd held it long enough."

The knight behind him with the weight on his shoulders didn't move.

Corbin couldn't turn his head. He couldn't do anything. He stood on the bridge's crown with the wave in his hands and Declan on a knight's shoulders at the end of the bridge, and he held the wave.

"I'm sorry," he said. It was all he had.

"Yes," said Rylan, and went on along the river road with his knights toward the castle.



Near dawn the last of it was down.

The sky over the eastern hills went grey, and then pale, and the white light from the north had long since gone out, and the wave that had stood over the lower ward at one in the morning higher than the bridge towers was a low glassy step of water a few feet high at the gorge's mouth, held, with the marsh full behind the cut and the river running fast and brown and high under the bridge.

"Last one," said Tess, across the water, hoarse. "On my count."

Doran said, behind him: "You were telling me something."

Corbin stood very still with his hands on the parapet.

"I was telling you something," he said.

"You were."

So he finished it. On the bridge, in the grey light, with his back to the old priest and his face to the water, holding the last of the wave; low, so that only Doran heard. That he'd wanted it. That he'd wanted to hate his brother, and Augustine had given him the reasons, and he'd taken them gladly. That the stupid part hadn't been believing. The stupid part had been how good it felt.

Doran heard him out. He didn't say anything about it. When Corbin had finished he said, "Thank you," as if Corbin had given him something.

"Now," said Tess, across the water.

Corbin let it go.

The last of the water ran out down the gorge's mouth into the river and the cut and the sluices, and the river took it, and rose, and ran on, brown and loud and ordinary. And the still went out of him, all of it, and with it the cold along his spine, and the weight on his back, and the claws, and the shine; easy, all at once, like a breath let out, and he was a man standing on a bridge in the shreds of a shirt with his hands on the parapet and his legs shaking so hard he had to lean on the stone.

He didn't fall down. He'd decided he wouldn't.

Somebody was coming along the bridge from the town end. Not running. A big man, walking slowly, grey all over, with glass dust in his hair and on his shoulders and his face, and his coat burned through across the back, and his hands black.

Harrold.

He came out along the bridge and stopped beside Corbin at the parapet, and put his own burned hands on the stone beside Corbin's, and looked at the river.

Rylan had come back along the river road. He stood at the end of the bridge, and said across it, to the regent, that the juggernaut was alive on the ridge in chains, drained, and that the knights asked whether it should be put to death.

"No," said Harrold, without turning round. "Keep it."

Rylan waited.

"It did what it was made to do," said Harrold. "Somebody made it. That's who I want." He looked at the water. "Keep it alive."

Rylan went.

Harrold and Corbin stood at the parapet of the old bridge side by side in the dawn, with their hands on the stone, and looked at the river going by.

Neither of them said anything.



The held messages came to the Rim on the Wednesday, the tenth, at a little after nine in the morning, in one long stack on Linnet's set, and Ferra read them in order.

She read them at the table in the hard room with the lamp lit and Imani beside her, because Linnet had brought the slips over herself at a run, and her face had been enough. Twenty-three of them. The night of the ninth, all of it, from the line room and the Well and the harbour set and the castle, everything Kaldo had sent west to the fleet in the twelve hours after the drop, held at the Grey Mark's relay and then the K Discovery's, and come through in a rush when the ring's set had caught the morning's clear air.

She read every one. Then she read them again, in order, with her pencil.

The drop. The quarter of an hour. The lit streets. Ambrose and the line room. The tank yard, the oil reserve burning, Osric and the fire company unable to save it. The dam. The liquid mana store. The wave. The dome. The bombing hull, down in the north field. The bridge. The ridge. Declan.

And then Harrold's. Two lines, in the line room's hand, sent at six on the Wednesday morning.

Changed at the dome. All of it at once. Couldn't come back for hours. Didn't decide it. H.

And Corbin's, under it. Longer. Corbin had always written more than Harrold.

Changed on the old bridge. Only partly. Held the wave all night; let it go at dawn and came back easily. Didn't decide it either. Don't know how. C.

Ferra turned to the back of her book.

The page was there, with its two columns and its heading. Need, or nature? Zale's name at the top, and under it, across both columns, the line she'd written on Sunday in his cabin: The question is asked.

She wrote Harrold's name under it. Then Corbin's.

Then she sat for a while, looking at the three names.

A light dragon woken where light had stopped being any use, over the one thing in the world he'd have put his body over. A stillness woken in a man who'd spent his whole life being still in front of people, at the one moment the city needed exactly that. And a sea dragon woken drowning, in the sea, by a man who'd crawled into it before he could walk.

She wrote it across both columns, under the three names, in her smallest hand.

Nature picks the kind. Need picks the moment.

And under that, after a moment: Two of them say so now. Under the question.

Imani had read the slips over her shoulder. She'd read Declan's twice.

"Send it down," she said.

Ferra didn't look up.

"Send it down to him," said Imani. "Now. With a runner. Today. He has to know."

"He'll know on Friday," said Ferra.

"Ferra."

"It's over." Ferra put her pencil down. "It was over before we heard about it. It was over by dawn on the Wednesday. Nobody can fly home in time to matter; there's nothing to fly home to do. Harrold and Corbin did it. The city did it." She put her hand flat on the stack of slips. "He's at the floor in a hearing about the night he couldn't hear either. The sitting's set to rise on Friday. If I send this down today, he comes up the stages today, in the middle of the hearing, in front of the whole court, and they know the land king's home burned and he ran. And it changes nothing at home. Nothing."

"It's his mother," said Imani. "It's his brother's people. It's Declan."

"I know whose it is."

Imani looked at her across the table.

"I'd send it," she said.

"I know you would," said Ferra. "I'm not."

She didn't send it.



The sitting rose on the Friday, the twelfth, at noon, as it had been set to.

The watch's runner brought it up: Ressar had called the recess for the stones at the close, to read an older line before the court went on. It would sit again when the Keepers had read. Nobody knew how long. The watch's runner also brought a name, which Ferra wrote in the margin: the narrow-faced young man who'd spoken for the Rim's house at the floor all week, in Ushar's empty seat, was Sevrin, Ushar's cousin.

Emeric came up the stages at a little after three.

She was on the last stair, where she'd waited for him on Sunday, with the stack in her hands. Imani was beside her. He came up out of the black pool on the pilot's hand and stood on the last step with the water running off him and took the helm off, and she saw his face, and he saw hers, and he knew before she'd said anything that something had happened.

She gave him the stack.

"They came on Wednesday morning," she said. "All of it. The night of the ninth. I had them on Wednesday at nine." She kept her voice level. It was the accounts voice. "I held them. I didn't send them down."

He held the stack. He didn't look at it yet. He looked at her.

"I'd have wanted it," he said.

"I know," said Ferra.

Neither of them said she'd been wrong. Imani, beside her, didn't say it either, though she'd said it on Wednesday and Ferra knew she still thought it.

He looked down at the slips in his hand, wet already from his fingers.

Amara's was on top. Ferra had put it there.



The Kaldo News, Wednesday 10 June, year 17

The night of the ninth. Church hulls came over the city a little after one in the morning and dropped men and darkness on the north quarter. The darkness did not take. The street lamps stayed lit. The castle asks that anyone who found an iron thing like a loaf in a gutter leave it where it lies and send for the Works.

The dam. The first stage of the river dam is gone, and the plant house at its foot. The wave that came down the gorge did not reach the lower ward. It was held above the old bridge from about half past one until dawn and let down a hand's width at a time into the old marsh and the mill sluices. Everyone in the lower ward is accounted for.

What the city saw. A shape of a woman went up over the dome on two columns of fire and brought down a hull. A great white beast lay across the dome's crown at a quarter to two and took a bomb on its back. Something stood on the old bridge all night. The castle says nothing about any of the three. Most of the city says it saw all of them.

Sir Declan. Killed on the north ridge above the river, holding a Church war-beast while his knights finished the mages who raised the wave. The knights say they had drilled there the night before, at his asking.
