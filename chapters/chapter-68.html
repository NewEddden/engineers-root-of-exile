Chapter 68 - The Faculty and the Physician

Callen came around the way good men come around, which is not all at once and not because they were argued into it but because the work put a thing in front of them their pride could not deny, and Callen's pride, whatever its faults, was a craftsman's pride and answered to results. The demonstration at the water had cracked him. He had watched the prince throw a rune's fine strokes up huge and sharp with a ground lens, Callen's own art turned to a use Callen had never imagined for it, not to help a carver set a stroke but to show a man a thing too small to see, and the cracking had widened all season as Callen was made to understand, project by project, that the man he had come prepared to despise as a dabbler was in fact the only person in his experience who used a craftsman's tools to ask questions instead of merely to make objects. That was a thing Callen had not known could be done. Once he had seen it done he could not stop seeing the uses, and a lens-grinder who starts seeing the uses of his own glass beyond the grinding of it has stopped being merely a grinder, which was, Emeric noted without comment, the whole point of bringing such men down from the tower.

The microscope was the natural place the two things met, Callen's glass and the question of the small, and Emeric had held the instrument in reserve for years for exactly the person it now found, which was not a villager and not a courtier but a physician, someone with the framework to read what the glass showed. He gave it, in effect, to Imani, and he gave it to her the way he had learned to give all his best things, not as a marvel from his hand but as a tool she could make her own and improve and, in time, forget had ever come from him, because a thing the barony needed him to have handed it was a thing that failed the day he died, and Imani was going to outlive him and had to own the seeing herself.

She had grown. That was the plain fact of her and the most satisfying thing in the barony to watch, more satisfying than the foundry or the road, because the foundry was iron and did what iron was made to do while Imani was a person becoming more than she had been. She had come to him years back an unremarkable practitioner, competent, careful, no worse than the run of physicians in a poor district and no better, a woman who set bones and dosed fevers and buried the ones she could not save with the ordinary sorrow of her trade. He had handed her the Sanitation Protocol, the whole zero-mana architecture of clean water and separated waste and rat-controlled grain that had broken the plague, and he had handed it to her as knowledge, a thing to apply, and had expected her to apply it, and she had done more than apply it. She had extended it. She had taken the principle underneath the protocol, that disease traveled by paths a careful mind could map and cut, and she had found paths he had not mapped, in the birthing rooms, in the fever wards, in the handling of the dead, and had cut them, and the district's death rates had come down in places his protocol had never specifically touched because she had understood the protocol well enough to write new lines of it herself. He had given her a tool and she had become a person who made tools. That was the only kind of gift he trusted.

The microscope, in her hands, was not going to be a marvel shown to skeptics. He had thought about it as a persuasion device years ago, in a plague village, and set it aside because a villager with no germ framework saw a lens full of wriggling nothing and thought sorcery, and the instrument only earned its keep with a mind prepared to read it. Imani was that mind now, more than she had been, and she took the microscope not as proof of anything to anyone but as an eye, a way of finally seeing the paths she had learned to map by their effects alone. She would spend years with it. She would be wrong with it, at first, the way everyone was wrong with a new eye, seeing patterns that were not there and missing ones that were, and she would correct herself the way she had corrected herself into competence and past it, and somewhere in those years she would stop being a physician who had been given good knowledge and become, quietly and without any single moment of becoming, one of the small number of genuinely excellent minds the barony had grown, a Great Inventor in the only sense that mattered, which was that the seeing was now hers and would not die with the man who lit it.

The thing that neither of them saw, because there was no reason yet to look at it, moved through the same rooms that season and went unread. Among the faculty Armand had drawn down from the tower was a mage of the ordinary sort, competent at the ordinary readings, and this mage, passing among the works as the faculty did now, noticed a small oddity and filed it as an oddity and thought no more of it, because it fit nowhere in anything he had reason to study. The men who worked the ink and the pantograph, the copyists and the fine-line hands whose whole day was spent close over the mana-touched work, read a shade differently to a mage's sense than other laborers did. Not much. A faint wrongness, a background hum where a man's mana ought to have been quiet, the sort of thing a mage registered the way you register a room's temperature, below notice, present. The mage did not connect it to the ink, because no one had told him the ink did anything to a man and he had no cause to suspect it did. He did not connect it to anything. It was a detail with no home, and a detail with no home is a detail a trained mind sets down and forgets, and he set it down, and forgot it, and the faint wrongness in the ink-workers went on being a thing that was true and unread, walking around the works in the bodies of the men who carried it, waiting for someone with a reason to look there, which no one yet had.

Emeric, who had the most reason of anyone to look there and knew the least about why, spent the season pleased. The medical capability of the barony had jumped, and jumped in the right way, into a person and not into a device, Imani carrying the seeing forward under her own power. Callen had come around and was worth every difficulty he had brought with him. The tower's exiles were becoming Kaldo's faculty, the way he had hoped and faster than he had hoped. He walked the works in the good weather and saw a barony that was, in nearly every direction he looked, succeeding, and he did not look in the one direction that would have shown him the fuse, because the fuse wore the face of a good outcome, healthy men doing skilled work, and a man does not go looking for a disaster in the middle of his best season, least of all in the faces of workers who have never been better. The leak walked past him in the yard, in daylight, and he greeted it by name and asked after its family, because to him it was not a leak. It was his people, thriving. That was exactly what made it dangerous, and exactly why he could not see it, and the season closed with the fuse one quiet turn shorter and no one in the barony, not the physician, not the mage, not the prince, aware that it was burning at all.
