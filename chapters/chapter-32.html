Chapter 32 - A Guard's Work

Every fix, every upgrade, every change the barony made opened it to some new danger. Emeric had made it his work to get ahead of that, to build something that closed off as many of those dangers as he could, from as many directions as they might come. The coalition's attack had shown him one thing plainly: enemies were everywhere, and they gave no real warning before they arrived. A small barony, growing fast, could be made to stand against an army many times its size. He had proven that at the gate. But proving it closed the door on a frontal attack, and a door shut on one side of a house only sends a determined man looking for a window. There was very little doubt left in him that the next attempt would be quiet. Infiltration was nothing new.

The next task was to remove any possibility that someone could enter the barony unaccounted for.

A man who walked through the front gate could be noted, sent on his way, watched if it came to that. Anyone who truly meant harm would not use the front door. The trouble was the wall itself. Guard rotations could be tracked and predicted. A guard had to walk the parapet to be sure no one was slipping over it, and he had to do it in the dark, and he was rotated out of his shift before the night was through, and the man who took his place started cold. Emeric needed a system that would do a guard's work, in the dead of night, from dusk to dawn, with no room for human error.

He had built things before that made a problem go away and stay away. A boiled-water rule. A furnace. A wall. Those problems had held still while he took them apart. This one would not. This one had a man behind it who thought, and waited, and would study whatever Emeric raised and go looking for the seam in it, the way the coalition should have gone looking and had been too proud to. So the thing he built could not be a better guard. A better guard was still a man on a schedule, and a schedule was a thing you learned by watching from the treeline for a week. The thing he built had to be the opposite of a man. It had to never rotate, never tire, never start a shift cold, and never, not once between dusk and dawn, look away from a stretch of dark because nothing had happened there in a hundred nights.

It could not fight. He settled that early, on the second night of drawing it, and the settling of it was half the design. A wall that fought back on its own was a wall that would one day crush a lost farmer or a stray dog, and worse, it was a wall that did his deciding for him, and deciding was the one thing he did not want to hand to a carving in a stone. The wall did not need to win anything. It needed to never blink. Let it see, and let it say what it saw, cleanly, to a man who was awake, and let the man do the deciding. The seeing was where human error lived. The deciding was where a man was still worth more than any rune he could cut.

So he built it to see.

Every living thing carried a little mana, the same faint background warmth he had felt under his own palm the night he stood on the wall and understood he had built it facing the wrong way. A body crossing the graded approach in the dark still carried that warmth whether the moon was out or not. A Sense Rune could be tuned to it the way it was tuned to anything, made to notice that specific signature and nothing else, so that a fox and a man and a mule each pulled a different weight on the reading. He drew the nodes small, one to a stretch of wall, each one watching its own length of the approach, each one wired back along the parapet to the gate. Two lines ran between them, and getting the two lines right was the part that took him longest to make plain even to himself. One line was thick, and carried the mana that fed the nodes their power. The other was thin, and carried only what the nodes had to say. Fuel down the fat groove, word down the narrow one, the way the great screen in the capital would have needed both if anyone had ever thought to build one, and the split mattered because if a node's report cost as much mana to send as the node cost to run, the whole thing would drink itself dry inside a night.

The last decision was the quietest, and he was proud of it in a way he did not say aloud. The nodes made no sound. Out on the wall, where an intruder was, there was nothing to hear, no bell, no flare, no warning that he had been counted. The sound happened inward, at the gate, where the watchman sat. A node that caught something lit its own small lamp on a board in front of the man, and the man knew which stretch of dark had a body in it before the body knew it had been seen at all. That was the whole trick, and it was a trick a bell could never do. A bell told everyone at once, the town and the intruder alike. This told one man, quietly, and left the intruder walking forward in the dark believing himself alone.

Bram fabricated the housings, and on the third one he stopped and turned it over in his hands and said the mortar bed Emeric had specced would sweat in the wall through a wet winter and green the carvings inside of two years, and cut the seat a different way without being asked, and Emeric filed it with the ore and the air-blast under the growing list of things he had been confident and wrong about, and thanked him, and meant it.

Ferra raised the only objection that could have stopped him, and she raised it the way she raised everything, with a number. "None of this is stone," she said, not asking, laying it beside the covenant in her head the way she laid everything beside a ledger. "You're carving into a wall that already stands. You're not raising a single new thing."

"Not one," Emeric said. "Vantic can stand on the parapet the whole time, and there's nothing there for her to write down. No fortress growing. Only a wall that already exists, learning to pay attention."

Vantic did stand on the parapet, once, the morning the first line went in. That was the covenant working exactly as written, the resident factor with the lawful right to walk his works and read them, and she read the groove in the stone and the small dark carving at the end of it and said nothing for long enough that Emeric understood she had decided it was not worth a letter and wanted him to know she had decided it. Then she said, "A barony that arms its wall is a barony that expects to be attacked again," in the flat way she said everything, and wrote nothing, and went back down. It was not a threat. It was a woman telling him the shape of the thought her house would have if he gave it a reason, so that he would not give it one. He took the warning for what it was and kept the work looking like maintenance, because in every way that a covenant could read, it was.

He tested the first true stretch on a moonless night with Rylan, because Rylan had the wall that week, and because the whole point of the thing was to take that wall off him.

Rylan was one of the four who had ridden down from the capital and stayed, a squared, unhurried man who had never once in two years given Emeric a reason to learn more about him than that he did his job and did not talk while he did it. He walked the parapet the way he always did, slow, deliberate, checking the dark stretches by habit even now, and he watched Emeric crouch at the gate over the little board of unlit lamps with the mild patience of a man waiting for a clever thing to either work or not.

"That'll do my rounds, then," Rylan said. Not sour. Just placing it.

"Most of them. The night ones. It sees the whole approach at once, and it doesn't get tired, and nobody can learn its schedule because it doesn't keep one."

Rylan considered that, looking out over the black slope where his own eyes could make out nothing. "My boy asks what I do up here," he said, unexpectedly. "Finn. He's six. I tell him I keep the counting straight, so nobody sneaks a wrong number past us in the dark." A small, dry breath that was almost a laugh. "Suppose I'll have to tell him the wall counts now."

"The wall counts," Emeric said. "You still decide what the count means."

That landed somewhere Rylan liked, and he nodded slowly, and then he said the thing Emeric would remember later, without knowing yet that he would. "That's the part, my lord. It can light every lamp on that board. Somebody's still got to be the one who gets up out of the chair and goes." He said it plainly, the way Bram corrected a mortar bed, a man stating a true thing about his own craft to someone who mostly understood it. "A wall that watches is a fine thing. But it doesn't answer. Answering's ours."

Emeric heard it as a staffing note. He filed it that way, under how many men the board still needed within a run of the gate to make the seeing worth anything, and he was right that it was that, and he did not hear the rest of it, the part that was about men and not schedules, because the part about men was the part he had never learned to hear. He was already looking back down at the board.

Out on the third stretch, something crossed the approach. A lamp woke on the board, small and clean and certain, third from the left, and held.

Rylan looked at it. Emeric looked at it. Neither of them had heard a thing.

"Fox," Rylan said, after a moment, reading the weight of the lamp the way Emeric had built it to be read, light, low, wrong shape for a man. "Too small and going the wrong way for trouble."

"The wall thinks so too," Emeric said quietly, and felt the thing work under his hands, felt it do in silence the exact job that had cost men their sleep and their night vision and their predictable, learnable rounds for as long as there had been a wall to walk. It worked. It saw in the dark, and it said what it saw, and it woke no one it did not need to wake.

He bonded the control to himself that same night, because a thing this new had to answer to a hand he trusted absolutely before it answered to any other, and his own was the only hand he trusted that far. He told himself it was temporary. Once it was proven, once he understood it the way he understood a furnace, he would open it out, teach it to answer to Ward, to the men on the board, to whoever needed to reach it when he was not standing at the gate. A system that lived and died with one man was not a system. He knew that. He had known it since the parapet, since the wrong wall. He would not build that mistake again.

He did not check whether the opening-out could be done. It did not occur to him to check. Where he came from, a key was a key, and a key you held was a key you could copy, and hand to a deputy, and take back. He set the bond to himself and moved on to the next stretch of dark, certain of a thing he had never once tested, and the wall watched the black slope for him, and lit its clean small lamps for no one but him, and would answer, when the night finally came that it mattered, to no one else alive.
