Book 2, Chapter 19: Dead Ground

The court came up again on the Wednesday, the twenty-seventh, at ten, and Emeric knew what it was going to be about before he'd got to the bottom of the stair, because the old families of the shallows were there before him.

They'd never been first before. They were among the last in, always, at both sittings; they came up the shafts from the shallows' end of the city at their own pace and took the far end of the left-hand benches and sat together, apart, plain coats, grey colours, and didn't look at anyone. This morning they were already seated when the guests came through the bell door. All of them. More of them than at either sitting before: the old man who'd told the story of the party whose stones went dark, with the boy at his shoulder, and a dozen besides, and behind them, standing, a row of others who weren't on the benches at all and had come anyway.

And among the ones standing, at the end, a lean grey man with a hard narrow face.

Emeric had seen him once. On the door's steps, three weeks ago, in the guards' long coat with a bident upright in his hand, two steps up the stair from where the others had gone when the queen moved them back. Near enough. Near enough to hear Ilenne put into her mother's speech, quietly, what was in the K Amara's hold. And then at the binding, slate, all over, the only one of the twelve who hadn't gone pale.

He wasn't in the guards' coat today. He was in a plain grey one, like the others of the shallows. He stood at the end of their row with his hands at his sides and his colour held, and he wasn't looking at the guests' bench.

He was looking at the queen's empty seat.

"Dessaun," said Ilenne, very low, at the end of the bench. She'd seen where Emeric was looking. "His name. Of the old families. The shallows." She didn't look at the man. "He was at the door."

"I know," said Emeric.

Zale sat on her other side, a foot further from her than he'd sat at either sitting before, with his arm held in against his strapped ribs. He didn't look at her either.



Ushar stood up when the queen had sat, and this time he didn't give grounds and sit down. He spoke at length, and Ilenne put it into Kaldo for them a sentence behind, in her flattest voice, and Emeric listened to it go the way he'd listen to a man laying out a case at the long table at home, and saw within the first minute how well it was built.

He began with the stones. The First City, floated on stones, and the dead ground that came over the water in the war, and every stone under the city going dark at once, and the city going down with everyone on it. He didn't tell it. He didn't have to; Ressar had told it in this hall a fortnight ago, from the slab, and every person here had learned it in that same flat voice as a child. He only named it. You know what sank the First City.

Then he said that the land had brought it here.

Ilenne's voice didn't change. Emeric watched her not let it.

"He says: there is a stone," said Ilenne. "On the burned ship. Past the markers. In a room of iron. He says it makes dead ground. He says it's the same thing. The thing that sank the First City." She listened. "He says the land brought it into our water. He says the queen knew. He says she was told, and she let it stay, and she told the court nothing."

The hall made no sound at all.

Emeric sat in the king's coat with his back against the stone and his hands on his knees and didn't move. He felt it go through the benches. Not in their colours, which were held; in the room, the way you feel weather change in a house before anybody's said it's turned cold. He felt the old families at the far end of the left-hand benches go absolutely still.

And Ushar wasn't finished. He did the thing he did best. He didn't argue it; he put it beside things. He put it beside the old promise, the one nobody here had read: eight armed ships past the wall under a gold-eyed king, and now this, in the hold of one of them. He put it beside the seal and the vent: the land ship on the wall the afternoon the glass went, and the watch's own line, that nobody could say she hadn't hurried it. One, and the next, and the next. Each of them true, or true enough. None of them said what any of them meant. He let the hall put them together.

The stones said the dead ground had sunk the First City. That was history. That this stone was that weapon, that the land had brought it here to use, that the queen had let it in: that was his. And there was nothing on the stones about it at all.

And he was standing in the dry hall saying it, where nothing he said was for ever, and if it had been false it would have cost him standing, and it wasn't false.

Emeric looked along the left-hand benches to the end of the old families' row.

Dessaun stood with his hands at his sides. He hadn't moved while Ushar spoke. He didn't move now. He was still looking at the queen, steadily, across the whole length of the hall, and he didn't look away.

That was all Emeric needed. Nobody had told him, and nobody would; but he was as sure as he could be without it who had carried it to the Rim's house, and he thought he could guess why. He'd seen men look like that at home, at the long table, when they'd done a thing they thought was right and were waiting for the cost. Not a man who'd sold anything. A man of the shallows, whose people had died on the land's coast, who'd heard the words dead zone in his own water and had waited to see whether his queen would tell anyone; and when she hadn't, had carried it to the one house that would.

The queen answered him.

She didn't stand. She spoke from her seat, with her hands on her knees, in a level voice, not long, and Ilenne put it over word for word, and Emeric could hear that this time she was afraid.

"She says she was told," said Ilenne. "At the steps. At the door. By the land king himself, before he went in. She says she was told first, before anybody. Before I could tell her." Her voice caught on that and steadied. "She says she put the ship past the markers. Her order. Hers, through the watch, written on the watch's slate at the door. She says the ship doesn't move without her word. It hasn't moved."

The queen said something else. Shorter.

"She says she didn't tell the court," said Ilenne, "because this court has no closed sitting. She says anything told to one hall is in every hall by night. She says she knew it. She says she chose." Ilenne listened, and her colour moved at the throat and was held. "She says she's watching it go into every hall now."

It was true. All of it. Emeric sat and listened to it and knew it was true, because he'd been there; and he knew it wasn't going to be enough.

It wasn't. He watched it not be. At the far end of the left-hand benches the old families sat with their colours held, and then, one and then another and then another, they let them go: slate. Hard dark blue-grey, from the throat down. The warning colour. Fear, Ilenne had said, or threat, or both; and they didn't put it away. They sat with it on them in front of the whole court and the queen and held it there.

Not all of them. Five, six. The old man with the boy. Two of the women standing behind. And at the end of the row Dessaun, who hadn't moved at all, and whose colour stayed perfectly level and grey; he'd done his slate three weeks ago, alone.

The court asked the land king.

It was the lean old merman on the second tier who asked it, the one who'd asked how eight ships stood in the air. He stood up and asked it courteously, through Ilenne, as he asked everything: what did the land king have to say about the stone.

Emeric stood up.

He'd thought about this since the night of the door. He'd thought about it on the K Amara's deck in March and in the K Discovery's hold with Ward and the inventory, and every way he'd thought about it had come out the same, and he'd known since the beast that it would be asked here, in this room.

He answered on the dry. He was standing in the dry hall, on the dry floor, and he said so first, so that nobody would mistake it; and Ilenne put that over.

"I won't sink it," he said.

The hall waited for the rest.

"I'm not offering to sink it in your sea, or give it to anybody here, or break it." He kept his voice level. "Not because I want it. Because none of those can be undone, and none of them are mine to do in your water. Sink it and it's on your floor for ever and you'll never know where. Give it to anybody in this room and somebody in this room has it. Break it and I don't know what it does, here, in water this thick, and neither does anyone." He looked along the benches. "It's on my ship because we've been at war at home and it came with everything else. It's past your markers because your queen told it to be. It hasn't moved."

Ilenne put it over.

"And the promise," said Emeric. "The old one. I'm not going to argue it. I haven't read it. Nobody here has. I'm not going to stand in your hall and tell you what it says when I don't know, and I'm not going to pretend I've kept it or broken it." He paused. "When I know what it says, I'll say."

That went along the benches. He didn't know how it landed. He didn't look for it.

Then he did the thing he'd decided on in the night.

"There's one thing I'll give the court," he said. "On the dry. It's the only thing I've got to give that's mine." He looked at the queen, not at Ushar. "While my ships are in your water, that stone doesn't come inside your markers. Not without your word. And if it does, it's on me. I answer for it. Not her."

He said it once. He said it slowly, so Ilenne could have every word of it. And then he said the other words, the two, here, on the dry floor of a dry room under the sea, where Ilenne had told the fleet every second evening for a fortnight was the only place a guest could say them:

"I will."

Ilenne put it into her mother's tongue. She put the last two words in exactly, and Emeric heard her voice go very carefully round them.

The hall didn't move.

He'd thought about what it was worth, all night, behind Maeve's curtain with Ferra's hand on his chest. Until now the K Amara's line had been the queen's port order and nothing else. If the ship came in, the queen's order had failed, and it was the queen in front of this court with nothing to stand behind. Now it was his word too. Said in her hall, before her court, on the dry, where a word could be broken but would be remembered if it was. If the ship moved, it was his before it was hers. That was something she could stand on. Not much. What he had.

Ushar stood up.

He said one sentence, quite pleasantly, and sat down again.

"He says," said Ilenne, "a dry word from a gold-eyed king is worth what dry words are worth."

Emeric didn't answer it.

He stood a moment longer on the dry floor with his hands at his sides, and looked at Ushar. Then he sat down. He felt the hall notice that he hadn't answered.



The queen gave the port order after that, and it was tighter than anything he'd said, and he'd known it would be.

She gave it to Taurre, from her seat, in the hall, through the watch, as she'd given the first one at the door; and Taurre cut it on his slate on his mark at the end of the right-hand bench, and Ilenne put it over in Kaldo, and Emeric heard it with Ferra's hand very still on her book beside him.

The burned ship would leave the markers. Not to come in; further out. To the watch's station at the west fetch, where the Rim's pilots met ships waiting to come in, a long way out: the Grey Mark, the watch called it, for the grey stone pillar on the reef there. Out of sight of the ring's watch posts, at her anchor, on the sea. She would come no nearer the ring than that until the queen's word came, through the watch.

Emeric asked one thing, through Ilenne, before Taurre had finished cutting: that escort three go with her. No hull of theirs lay alone, he said. Not since Kaldo. It was his knight's order and he'd not break it now. If the K Amara was holed out there, or burning, somebody had to be there to take her people off.

The queen heard it, and looked at him, and inclined her head; and Taurre cut a line more.

Thirty miles. Ferra wrote it. Emeric watched her pencil go down: Grey Mark, about 30 mi. W. K Am. and esc. 3. Out of view. And then a line under it, and the sum, which she didn't say aloud and didn't need to.

Two of the fleet's three armed hulls. The K Amara and escort three, out of sight of the ring for as long as they lay in Aumere's water. About four hundred people. About half of them. And about ninety of the knights. Three quarters. Ward, and the knights' aprons, and Arthur and Mira and Bren, and the weapons bay, and her dragon rifles.

And at the ring, escort one: her guns and Ruan's crews and her own knights. The four flight knights each on the K Discovery and the K Endeavor and hull four. The K Endeavor's hospital deck. About thirty knights, between all of it, for the ring and the gate and everyone at Aumere who could be reached from the water.

Most of the fleet's fighting strength, gone out of sight.

He'd chosen this. He'd known since the night of the door that it would come to something like it. He sat on the guests' bench with the coat on his back and didn't look at Ferra's sum.



Ward came on the set at noon, from the K Amara's bridge deck, a mile past the markers, and Emeric took it on the landing outside the hall's bell door with the sea beside him and nobody near.

"I've had it from Taurre's boat," said Ward. "The Grey Mark. Thirty miles. Escort three."

"Yes."

"We'll go." There was a pause on the set. Not long. Ward's pauses never were. "It's the queen's water. It's her order. We'll go. I'll have the knights by set from here. Voll has them on the spot at the ring. Sir Hadden Voll, escort one's knight-captain. Ruan has the guns."

"And I've the fleet," said Emeric.

"You've the fleet," said Ward. "Nobody's going to pretend that's the same thing."

"Thank you."

"I'll say one thing," said Ward. "Then I'll go and do it." He didn't change his voice. "That's twice. The door and now this. Twice a risk only you thought you could manage has been decided somewhere I'm not. This time on a set, with me a mile off." He paused. "I'm only saying it so it's said."

"I know," said Emeric.

"I know you know."

Emeric stood on the landing with the set at his collar and looked at the water. Then Ward said the thing Emeric had been waiting for.

"Let me put Arthur's lot on escort one before we go. Arthur, Mira, Bren. A few more off the aprons. The best I've got. Leave me the rest."

"No."

The set was quiet.

"It's what she's buying," said Emeric. He'd known Ward would ask. He'd known what he'd say. "From the old families. From the court. She stood up in front of all of them with nothing but a port order, and what she's buying with it is the warship gone, and her knights gone, out of sight, where nobody on the ring has to look at them. If I take the knights off her decks and put them on escort one at the ring, the warship's gone and the knights aren't. Every one of them who can count sees it. It guts it."

"It leaves you thirty knights," said Ward. "At the ring. Three days after something came up out of the sea at your brother."

"Yes."

The set hissed for a while.

"I'm coming out on the watch's boat tomorrow," said Emeric. "With the order. I want to talk to you on your deck. Not on this."

"I'll be on it," said Ward, and the set went dead.



The queen kept him back when the hall emptied.

He hadn't expected it. The court went down its shafts at two, slowly, the deep lords first, coughing, glad to be going; and the old families last, with their slate still on some of them; and Dessaun last of all, without looking back. The Keepers went with Ressar's slab between them. Taurre went with his slate. And when the guests rose to go, Ilenne put her hand on Emeric's arm.

"My mother," she said. "Stay."

So he stayed, with Ferra beside him, because Ferra hadn't moved and he wasn't going to ask her to. Zale stopped at the bell door, and looked back at Ilenne, and went out.

The queen came down from the black seat. She came the length of the empty hall on her legs, slowly, with the green dark of the sea behind the ribs over her, and stopped in front of the land king, and took her time.

She spoke. Ilenne put it over, a sentence behind, low.

"She says this is the dry hall," said Ilenne. "For strangers. She says the court comes up for strangers, and it doesn't like it, and it says nothing in here it means for ever." She listened. "She says the full court sits at the floor. Below. Near the Breath. In the water. She says that's where it hears a thing properly. A king properly." Her colour moved, and was held. "She says it won't hear a land king properly up here. Only down there."

The queen brought her right hand up from her side. Open. And turned it over, palm up, toward him, as she had at the door.

Come in. My house. My word.

"She asks you down," said Ilenne. "To the floor. To the full court. Not an order." She swallowed. "An asking."

Emeric looked at the queen's hand.

He knew what it meant. He'd known since Imani stood on the last stair above the stages with her set gone to rushing in her hand. Below, nobody could hear him. Not Ward, thirty miles off at the Grey Mark. Not the K Discovery. Not anybody. The last place a set would answer was the head of the bell stages, at the top of the first wet shaft. Past that, he'd be under the sea, at the bottom of a city, with the court, and nobody in Kaldo's fleet would know a thing about him until he came back up.

He'd thought he might be asked. He hadn't thought it would be today.

"Yes," he said. On the dry.

Ilenne put it over. The queen inclined her head, and lowered her hand, and went back up the hall.

Beside him, Ferra looked down at the field set on her belt. Then at him. She kept what she was thinking to herself.



The watch's boat took the order out to the K Amara on the Thursday morning at nine, with Taurre's slate in a case in the stern and a watch officer to deliver it and Emeric in the bows; and Maeve came too.

She hadn't asked. She'd been sitting on the landing when the boat came alongside the steps, with her hands round her knees, and when Emeric went down into it she got up and came down after him and sat on the middle thwart, and the watch officer glanced at her and at Emeric and decided it wasn't his business.

It was a long pull. The K Amara lay a mile past the outer markers on the flat grey sea, her scorched port side toward them all the way out, black from the rack bank to the waterline, the stone resin on her shell dull in the light where the crazing was. Escort three lay a cable off her starboard quarter with her guns trained fore and aft and her people at her rail. And the K Amara's aprons were full. Every knight Ward had was on deck, in working dress, along the rails, watching the boat come.

The officer went up the side with the case and gave Ward the slate in front of all of them, and Ward read it, and wrote his mark on the watch's tally, and gave it back; and that was the order delivered.

Then Ward looked at Emeric and jerked his head at the after end of the bridge deck, where nobody was.

They stood at the rail there with their backs to the ship.

"Say it," said Ward.

"Outside the markers," said Emeric, "if you need to save a life, you go. Anywhere outside them. Either hull. You don't ask anybody. You tell the watch the moment you move. That's inside her order. That's not a breach."

"And inside?"

"Inside the outer markers it's a breach. Of her order, and of what I said in the hall yesterday. Whatever the reason. Unless her word comes first."

Ward looked at the sea.

"And if her word doesn't come first," he said, "and somebody's dying at the ring, and I'm thirty miles off with three quarters of the knights you've got."

"Then it's your judgment," said Emeric. "And if you come," and he said this the way he'd decided in the night to say it, plainly, so that it was said before it ever happened and nobody could say afterwards whose it had been, "if you have to, come. It's mine. Not hers. I said it in her hall. If you come in past those markers, it's my word that broke. I'll answer for it."

Ward was quiet.

"You've given her something to stand on," he said at last, "and me a reason to break it."

"Yes."

"That's a hell of a thing to give a man."

"I know," said Emeric. "I'd rather give it you now than have you wondering at the wrong moment whose it is."

Ward turned his head and studied him. Then he nodded once, the nod he gave a thing he didn't like and believed.

"Mine to decide," he said. "Yours to answer for."

"Yes."

"All right," said Ward, and went back to his ship.

Ferra's word went out on the same boat, to the Rim's fish traders by way of the pilots' hall: that from Saturday the K Amara's and escort three's loads went out to the Grey Mark, under the watch's escort, and were weighed on the quay before they left and the empty crates weighed when they came back, as before. Emeric had read the slip. It was four lines long and there wasn't a word in it that could be read two ways.

And when the watch's boat was ready to go back, Maeve came down the K Amara's side with her kit bag on her shoulder.

She'd gone below as soon as they came aboard. She'd been gone an hour. Emeric had seen her go and hadn't asked where; he'd thought she'd gone to see Mira, which she had, and then she'd gone to her own berth on the knights' deck and packed everything she owned into the bag, her spare boots and her other shirt and her winter coat, and come back up with it.

She put it in the bottom of the boat and sat down on it.

"I'm going on the K Discovery," she said, to nobody in particular. "Sorrel's got a spare bunk. I asked her on the set last night."

The watch officer glanced at Emeric. Emeric let it be.

She hadn't asked him. She hadn't asked Ward either. She'd decided, and done it, and come down the side with her bag, and she sat on it now with her chin up and her hands round her knees and her eyes on the water, as she'd sat on the landing in the morning, and dared anybody in the boat to tell her to go back up.

Nobody did.

The boat pushed off. And as it pulled away from the K Amara's black side, Emeric looked up.

Mira was at the rail.

She was on the knights' apron, at the after end, by herself, in her working dress with her hands on the rail, watching the boat go. She didn't wave. Maeve didn't either. Maeve sat on her bag in the bottom of the boat with her face turned up to the apron, and Mira stood at the rail with her face turned down to the boat, and neither of them did anything at all; and the boat pulled away, and the distance opened between them, a yard and then ten and then a cable, the first time since Kaldo their berths had been on different hulls.

Bren was at the rail too, further forward, with half the aprons. Emeric saw him. Bren was waving both arms over his head at the boat, and grinning, and shouting something nobody could hear.

Emeric watched him. Then he lifted a hand to him.

Maeve, who'd seen him do it, looked at him in great surprise. He didn't explain.

The watch's boat put her aboard the K Discovery on its way in, bag and all, and Sorrel came to the rail herself to see her up.



The K Amara left on the Friday at dusk.

Emeric watched her from the breakwater's crown, beside Bram's drum on the stable wall, with Ferra on one side of him and Imani on the other and the pen drawing its slow line round the turning paper at his back. Zale had come up the stages for the post and was on his own ship's rail inside the gate with his crew round him. Ilenne wasn't with him.

The sun was going down behind the western islands, a long way off over the sea, and the sky was red and then a deep blue going purple, and the water inside the ring had gone to a dark glass with the city's glow coming up through it from below. Outside the ring, past the markers, the K Amara lay on the grey sea with her lamps lit, and escort three a cable off her quarter.

At a quarter past eight her engines started. He heard them come to him across the water, a mile and more of it, the slow heavy beat of her diesel that he'd heard in the yard the first day they ran it; and her anchor came up, and she moved. On the sea, as the queen's order had her lie. Escort three came round beside her a cable off. And the two of them turned, slowly, together, and went west.

She went with her scorched side to the ring.

She didn't hurry. She went at her cruising pace over the darkening water, toward the place in the west where the sun had gone; the long black burn down her port side from the rack bank to the waterline turned toward Aumere for as long as she could be seen, so that every person on the breakwater and the steps and the Rim and every hull inside and outside the ring could see what had been done to her coming here, and see her go.

Along the breakwater, the watch-fires came on.

One. At the gate, red, in the lamp in the arch. Then another, a quarter of a mile along the wall to the north. Then another. One by one, round the whole long curve of the breakwater and on along the islands of the Rim, the watch's fires came up in the dusk, small and orange and steady, each lit as the one before it caught, going away from him round the ring until they made a circle of light on the dark water, all the way round, closing.

The K Amara's lamps got smaller in the west.

Then they were two lights low over the sea, close together. Then one. Then nothing at all; only the dark, and the last of the red in the sky behind it, and the circle of the watch's fires all the way round the ring, and the pen at his back, drawing its slow line.
