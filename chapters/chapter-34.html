Chapter 34 - The Tell

He started looking for a second delver the way he started most things, quietly and without much hope, and it went nowhere, which he had half expected.

He did it the only way he could think to, which was to sit with a man near the Hollow Crystal and watch for the Pull to catch on anything, the faint drag of one mind noticing the mana in another. He tried it with Ferra, who felt nothing and said so and went back to her ledgers. He tried it with two of the smiths and a quick boy from the mine whom Jabari swore was the cleverest hand he had, and none of them felt the thing Emeric had spent years falling into by accident, and none of them had a dead mother's stone to sleep beside for the hours it seemed to take, and Emeric came away from three days of it with the same understanding he had gone in with and had chosen not to be afraid of. He was the only one. The road to a second delver, if there was a road, was longer than a season and he could not see the start of it. He filed it again, under later, the way he had filed it at the end of the night he built the thing, and he told himself the filing was patience and not avoidance, and mostly believed it.

He had the stones for it, if stones had been the thing lacking. Corbin's banquet gift still sat in the bottom of a chest, a fistful of Hollow Crystals handed over as an insult, unchanging, just like you, and Emeric could have pressed one into every willing hand in the barony and been no closer. That was the part he kept having to learn again. The stone was never the gate. Delving was hours, cumulative and dumb and unhurryable, the years he had spent with his mother's rune near his pillow logged one quiet night at a time, and under the hours a thing a man either had a flicker of or did not, and none of the men he had sat down had shown the flicker. A rune apiece changed the count of stones in the room and nothing else, not the count of hours, and not whether the hand holding it had the thing to feel with. He knew all of that and sat them down anyway, because hope is not an argument you win once and put away.

And it was not as if a man who could not delve was a man of no use to the wall. Half of what the network needed was eyes, not hands. A watcher at the board who learned which lamps woke and how often, who could tell a lie on stretch nine from a fox on stretch three, was worth his post and then some, and any steady man could be taught that much. What such a man could not do was reach. He could see a node gone wrong and he could not go and set it right, because setting it right meant putting a delver's hand into the rune-work and waking it, and waking it lived behind the same hours no spare stone could buy him past. So Kaldo could have watchers by the dozen and be no safer in the one place it counted. He did not need another pair of eyes on the board. He needed a second hand that could reach into the wall itself, and three days by the Hollow Crystal had told him plainly that he was not going to find one by handing rocks to willing men.

So the wall went on answering to one hand, and Rylan went on walking it, and it was Rylan who changed, and Emeric who did not see it for what it was.

He saw the surface of it. He was not blind to surfaces. Rylan had gone quiet in a way that was different from his usual quiet, a held quiet instead of an easy one, and he had stopped eating with the other three, and twice Emeric had come up to the gate at an odd hour and found him already there, awake, at the board, when the rotation did not have him on the wall at all. A man drawn to the thing that was taking his job, Emeric thought, the first time. A conscientious man learning a new tool. He filed it as that, and it was the kind of filing that felt like understanding and was not.

"Boy's with his aunt," Rylan said, unprompted, on the second of those odd nights, the way a man says a thing he has decided in advance to say. "Milford. My wife's sister. Better air than the wall in winter, and she's been wanting him." He said it looking out at the dark and not at Emeric, and Emeric heard a father being sensible about a child's chest in the cold months, and said something about Milford's plains being kinder than the river damp, and meant it kindly, and did not hear the other thing, which was that a man does not explain a child's whereabouts to someone who has not asked about the child. There was nothing in it for Emeric to read, because reading it was precisely the thing he could not do. He took the words and not the shape of them being said, and moved the conversation to the wall, because the wall was the thing he could read.

The questions came in over those same nights, and they came in the shape a good guard's questions came in, which was why they went straight past him.

"What do I do if it faults." Rylan asked it plainly, both of them at the board, the lamps dark and quiet. "In the night. Something goes wrong with it and I'm the man here. Who do I wake."

It was the right question. It was the question Emeric would have wanted the man on the wall to ask, and he answered it the way he answered any good question, fully, pleased to be asked, because the thing about his own systems was that he could not help explaining them well.

"Most of what looks like a fault is nothing," he said. "A node's stone runs down and the node goes dark. No sound, no warning, it just stops seeing its stretch. That's the one to know, because a dark node looks like nothing at all from in here. The lamp doesn't light because the node isn't there to light it, not because the stretch is empty. So you learn the board. You learn which lamps you've seen wake and how often, and if a stretch that usually catches a fox a night goes three nights black, that's not three quiet nights. That's a dead stone, and a blind piece of wall, and you tell me and I go swap it."

"And a real one," Rylan said. "Not a dead stone. The rune itself going wrong."

"Rarer. But then you wake me, and only me." He said it without weight, a known limitation stated by a man who intended to fix it eventually, and he did not notice how cleanly he was laying it out, or to whom, or that a held-quiet man was taking each piece of it and setting it somewhere careful. "The network's bonded to me. A true fault in the rune-work isn't a thing another hand can reach in and set right, because it won't answer another hand. It answers mine. I've been meaning to change that, to teach it a second man, but it isn't a thing I've cracked yet. So for now, if the wall itself is wrong and not just a stone gone flat, it's me who has to come out and put my hand on it. There's no one you can send instead. There's only me, and I come."

Rylan was quiet a moment. Then, mild, turning it like a man turning a work problem: "How would I tell them apart. A dead stone I can wait on till morning. A real fault I wake you for in the dark. In the moment, on the wall, how's a man to know which he's looking at."

"That's the good question," Emeric said, and he was glad of it, glad someone was thinking about the seam he himself thought was the interesting one, and he talked for a while about the difference, about how a starved node simply ceased while a faulting one might flicker or read wrong before it went, about the signs a man on the board might use to call it one way or the other, and he gave it all away in the tone of a craftsman sharing the fine points of his craft with the one other person who seemed to care about them. He thought, that night, that Rylan might make more of the wall-work than any of them. He thought he had found, if not a delver, at least a man who understood what the thing was for.

He walked back to the hall lighter than he had been in days, pleased with the conversation, pleased with the man.

Ward was the one who came close to seeing it, because seeing it was Ward's whole trade, and Emeric talked him out of it.

"Rylan's off," Ward said. He said it the way he reported anything, flat, without dressing it, catching Emeric in the yard the next morning. "Been off a week. Asking the men your hours. When you walk the wall, which nights you're at the board, how long before you'd notice a thing gone wrong out there. Keeps to himself and asks the wrong questions when he doesn't." Ward let that sit. "I don't like it."

And here was the moment the warning came, plainly, from the one man in the barony built to read other men, and Emeric took it and ran it through the only machine he had, which was the machine that turned people into reasons, and got the wrong answer with total confidence.

"He sent his boy to Milford," Emeric said. "Wife's sister. A man does that, sends his child off, he's carrying something, worry or money or a wife who's unhappy about the winter. And he's got the night wall and a new system taking half his purpose on it, so of course he's asking how it works and when I'm on it. He's the only one of the four who's tried to understand the thing instead of resenting it." He heard himself and it sounded like sense. It was sense. Every piece of it was a true and reasonable account of a troubled, conscientious man, and it was wrong in the one place that mattered, and he could not feel the place it was wrong because feeling it was the sense he had never been given. "He's the man I'd trust with the board, Ward. Not the man I'd watch."

Ward looked at him for a moment longer than the answer needed, the look of a man deciding whether to spend more on a thing than it seemed worth. Then he let it go, because Emeric was the lord and the answer was reasonable and Ward had nothing but a feeling and a week of wrong questions, and a feeling was not a thing you arrested a good soldier over. "You know him better," Ward said, which was not agreement, and went back to his work not satisfied.

Emeric forgot the conversation by noon. He had a dead stone to swap on the third stretch, a real one, an honest starved node exactly like the kind he had described, and he went out and put his hand on it and woke it and thought nothing of the lesson he had just taught, out loud, to a man standing at his shoulder, about how a fault could be told from a flat stone, and how the only cure for the first kind was Emeric himself, walking out into the dark, alone, because there was no one on earth he could send instead.

None of it had begun in Kaldo, and none of it had needed a man inside the walls to begin. It had begun in Milford, where a boy was spending the winter with his mother's sister, sent there by a father who wanted better air in his son's chest than the river gave him, the one wholly innocent act in the whole black chain. A child outside Kaldo's walls was a child Kaldo did not guard. The men who took him needed only to be somewhere the barony's reach did not go, and Milford was a day's ride and another lord's ground, and they were there and gone before anyone in Kaldo knew a thing had changed. After that Rylan was not a guard any longer, whatever he still wore on the wall. He was a door with a lie for a hinge, and the lie was the aunt in Milford, better air, the wife's sister who had always been wanting him, a story true right up to its last word and hollow underneath it, handed to Rylan to say so that a boy's absence and a father's distraction would both read as nothing worse than a cold winter and a soft heart. It had worked exactly as it was built to. The man it was told to had heard a careful father and nothing else.

And what the men who held the boy wanted was never the gate. Any gate opens. What they wanted was the wall behind it, how it woke the barony and how fast, and whether the waking could be turned around and made into a summons, a way to call the one hand that answered out into the dark on its own. That was a thing no one outside Kaldo could learn, and the man they now owned could learn it from only one person, the one who had built the thing and could not stop himself explaining it well. So Rylan asked. He asked in the shape of a good guard learning his tools, night after night at the board, and Emeric, pleased past all sense to have found someone who cared about the seams, laid the whole of it in his hands over a week of quiet. That the network answered one hand and only one. That a true fault could be reached by no hand but the lord's. That a starved node and a faulting one could be told apart by a flicker before the dark, which was to say that a faulting node could be counterfeited by a man who knew the flicker to fake. That the cure for the worse kind was the lord himself, walking out alone into the black, with no one on earth to send in his place. Every piece a man would need to raise a lie on stretch nine and stand a knife at the end of it, given away across a board in the tone of a craftsman glad of the company, to the courier of the man who had come to kill him.

The wall watched the black slope that night for the one hand it trusted, and Emeric slept well, easy in the two things he was surest of, that the wall would not fail and that the quiet, careful man he had spent the week teaching was the last of the four he would ever need to watch. The tells had come to him twice that day and he had filed them both, the child explained to a man who had not asked, the questions a shade too exact, and he had filed them under nothing, because he had not felt them as anything at all. They sat where he had put them, unread, waiting on a night he could not see coming, for a hand only he could answer with.
