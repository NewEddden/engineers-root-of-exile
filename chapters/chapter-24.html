Book 3, Batch 5 — Where's My Wife

Chapters 38–47 | Monday 10 April – Thursday 8 June, Year 19

The March Emergency Authorisation is a month old. Ward holds the field command under the Assembly's committee, Quarrie reviews every use of force, and force against a held place may come only after a proclamation has been read there. Fairhythe is open and Sayer is on remand. Stannary has submitted, but its east stope, closed by written order on 5 April, is still being worked short-timbered, its pumps failing, and an old ladderway has been repaired behind fresh lagging that appears on no plan. Pryor holds the March with Church carts going up to his keep by night. Corrick, Kezia and Gethin are on remand at Kestle. The orc families' member is on his way from the Sun Shore, and day 120 of the note falls on 8 May.

This is the batch of Imani's capture. The east stope comes down, Imani goes to the trapped men against advice, and in the middle of a delicate repair a frightened monk forces a stone and the hall goes dead. She trades herself for a patient's life and is taken up a passage nobody knew was open, and the bond with her goes black. What follows is six days of finding her and waiting at her own request, and then one night when the waiting stops and the king goes to the gate as a dragon and asks the only question he has.

The law follows him through all of it. A magistrate buys patients' lives with passage and will not buy a murderer's immunity. An inquiry censures the king for force used without a proclamation and writes a new rule for him on the spot. A week later, under that rule, he breaks an army on the Black Moss, lawfully, and the world gives him a name. Then the review reads its second part and seals the one place he most wants to listen to, and the Authorisation lapses at midnight with nobody asking for more.

Chapter 38 — Forty-One

On Monday 10 April, about twenty past ten, through the eyes of Cal Pascoe, a nineteen-year-old pumpman, the pumps at Stannary have stopped twice since Saturday, the backfill round the pillars has gone soft as porridge, and Kenver Tregarth's crew are on the east stope's face because Treloar said the timber was coming. The east stope comes down. Nine die. Forty-one are alive behind it in two pockets, Cal among them with his legs under a slab, talking.

At noon in Kaldo, through Imani's eyes, the field command's line reports forty-one alive and the dig at least two days, and the duty surgeon at Fairhythe says six have legs pinned in a way he does not like. Ferra argues against her going, because the Authorisation's war is at the March's edge and Stannary's boundary ridge is on it. Ward argues the same by set from Fairhythe: the hall is a day's ride from Pryor. Emeric does not argue; he knows better, and says only that Ward will put a cordon on it. She goes that afternoon on a hospital hull with Sister Inga's team on their pager bands. The engine-house hall at the mine's mouth, on the Stannary side of the boundary ridge, is cleared for beds, under Kaldo's rule that nobody carries a stone past the door, because a forced stone near Imani kills her sight.

Through Ward's eyes, he flies over from Fairhythe with a Crestless detail and sets a two-hundred-yard ring of posts. His men physically check the three known exits: the main shaft, the new ventilation shaft four hundred yards upslope, and the drainage adit six hundred yards downhill. Tregarth's gallery plan marks the fourth way, the Coffin Level, the old haulage incline from the hall's lower door, as flooded and impassable, its sump under water to the waist for thirty yards behind the collapsed east drift. The fresh lagging at its lower door reads as rescue work to everyone who sees it.

On Tuesday afternoon, on the hall's porch, through Imani's eyes, nineteen of the trapped are March men from over the ridge, and their families, through Mother Brannagh, want Saint Ilda's prayer said over the stone before anyone is moved, by Brother Cuthbert, the healer-monk who came down from Saint Ilda's with the relic case. Imani offers the prayer said outside, an empty casing, the stone held outside on a cord. Brannagh rejects each, sincerely; it is her faith, and Imani does not make her a fool for it. Ward asks to open the case, and Cuthbert refuses: it is consecrated, and no unordained hand may open it. The compromise is that the case is weighed, double-sealed, and a Kaldo mage senses one ordinary stone inside. Ward writes a protest into the hall's log that any stone can be forced. Imani signs under it that she accepts the risk, because the other cost is March men dying untreated because their families will not let them be moved without the prayer.

Chapter 39 — Release

Early on Wednesday 12 April, through Sister Inga's eyes, the rescuers break through to both pockets after two days. Her plan for all forty-one is lines in before any rock moves, fluids running, pulse and rhythm watched on every one, and staged release a few at a time, with a ward for failing kidneys set up in the hall's back room and tourniquets only where there is bleeding. It is a capable team, and it works without Imani in the frame. Through Imani's eyes, her sight finds the six whose torn vessels deep in the thigh the rock is holding shut, and she places ties by sight through a small opening as each slab is jacked up a finger at a time: two on Wednesday, three on Thursday, and Cal Pascoe last, on Friday, because his slab is the worst seated and the jacks need the others out of the way. The other thirty-five come out over the three days in Inga's stages, and by Friday afternoon twenty-nine are in beds in the hall and six still pinned.

On Friday 14 April, at about ten past two, Imani has her hands in Cal's thigh through a small opening, the tie half placed, and asks Cuthbert to bring the lamp closer. He panics and forces the stone early, and the hall goes dead. Her sight goes, and she finishes the tie blind, by feel, while the oil lamps stay lit. Five seconds later three of six attackers are inside, and she draws one-handed and shoots the nearest in the hip. Sir Edwin Hartley, a knight-orderly, unarmed under the hall's rule, goes for Cuthbert, and a fourth attacker coming through the lower door stabs him; he dies within a minute. A knife goes to a crush patient's throat, and she orders the second orderly to stand still. "Take me. Leave them." A drain cuff goes on her wrist; inside the dead zone it locks and does not drain yet. The six came in with the March volunteers who dug for two days, which is why nobody looked at them twice: Kenver Tregarth, who leads them out by his repaired ladderway; Pryor's sergeant, the father of a young guard named Davey Corve; two March men of the sergeant's; the stabber; and the shot man. Imani, Cuthbert and four of them go into the Coffin Level, while the stabber stays with the shot man and both are taken by the cordon. Imani walks into the dark of the Coffin Level with a cuff on that is not working yet, and hears the flare go up behind her as the orderly fires it from the porch.

Chapter 40 — Black

At ten past two, in the field command's room at Fairhythe, Emeric is mid-sentence with Ward when she is gone from him: not hurt, not frightened, just gone, the way a death would feel. In Kaldo, in the Chancellor's office, Ferra sits down on the floor. For half an hour they think she may be dead. At about quarter to three she comes back, frightened, moving, alive, as she is dragged up the old ladderway and out past the edge of the hall's dead zone. Then she thins to a thread, because the cuff, locked inside the zone where it could not drain, starts draining the moment she is out of it. He is in the air within minutes on the K Discovery with Ward, following the thread's nearer and farther toward the March. Meanwhile the pursuit learns from a miner that the Coffin Level is open after all and wades its flooded entry about fifteen minutes behind them, and at about forty minutes the kidnappers come out at the Old Gate adit, three hundred yards inside the March under forest, where Pryor's covering detachment stops the pursuit short with fire and a closed carriage is waiting. The K Discovery is over the hall at about ten past four. By then the carriage is inside Pryor's Gate, and at about four the thread goes black again and stays black: she is inside the keep's dead zone.

At about twenty to three Ferra takes the next fast hull from the line room's pad, and Runa is on it. Emeric, by set, says no to a twelve-year-old at a rebellion. Runa says into the set: she's my mother. Ferra does not stop her, and they reach the hall at about twenty to eight. In the first hour, on the K Discovery, Ward remembers a line from the Dunholt charge sheet that crossed the field command's desk: Kezia Varley, former Stannary shot-firer. He has the line room page Kestle's duty clerk. Kestle's governor will not put a remand prisoner on a set without the circuit's order, the duty magistrate signs it at about half past six, and from about twenty to seven, in Kestle's gatehouse, through Kezia's eyes, she draws the old workings from memory on the back of a charge form: the ladderway, where it meets the incline, the one ladder climb, the incline's mouth at the Old Gate adit, and no other way out past it. The duty clerk reads it down the line on the circuit's field set measure by measure, and the K Discovery's clerk redraws it. Ward asks why. Kezia says: Gethin's hand. She asks for nothing except that it never be put before the sentencing bench. She does not want it counted; she wants it done.

At about ten to eight, over the hall, through Runa's eyes, she sweeps the hillside and puts her finger on a spot where the incline's old air shaft breathes out: fresh-cut lagging, lamp oil, and her mother. The cordon lost twelve minutes there on Friday afternoon. Kezia's drawing arrives within the same quarter hour and matches her nose turn for turn; the old ladderway has a name now, the Monk's Rise. The hall's dead zone has cleared in five hours of dense rock, so Runa is not going into one. With two Crestless she runs the trail from the hall's lower door, through the flooded sump to her chest, up the Rise, along the incline's mile, to the Old Gate adit. The covering detachment has pulled back, the carriages are gone, and the tracks split. The decoy smells of Imani's shawl. The other carriage's wheel-step smells of her sweat and fear and the cuff's stone. A shawl is not a person. From about nine to half past ten, from the K Discovery low over the March road with her lights out, she follows the true carriage nine miles until it runs into air her pull cannot reach into: the keep's zone. Nine miles from the hall, a girl says the air has stopped.

Chapter 41 — The Count

At dawn on Saturday 15 April the hall's report reaches Ferra's log first thing. Imani's blind tie was temporary and needed a definitive repair within hours, the only surgeon who could see it had been taken, and Inga's team could not find the slipping branch by ordinary means in time. Cal Pascoe died about eleven on Friday night, and Inga wrote it in the hall's book in her own hand. Ferra copies the line exactly.

Overnight at the K Discovery's chart table, Ferra, who has no stay power left, does what she can still do. Every few hours, for a heartbeat, Imani comes through the bond and goes again, and Ferra logs every flicker against the clock. The decoy fort's zone is one big stone and never blinks; the keep's is a rack of stones forced in turn, and every flicker falls when its rack changes. By the first dawn, about ten to six on Saturday, the family knows exactly where she is. Finding her was never the problem after that. Getting her out alive was. That day a scout party watches the Holt fort by glass, the shawl carriage in its yard and its zone never blinking, and the field company moves up to three miles below the gate, held there by the gate guns.

On Saturday night, through Runa's eyes, she lies on the cliff side of the spur and maps the keep on the night wind: her mother's window, east range, ground floor, medicine; sulphur and saltpetre under it, powder; the wall counted post by post by tobacco, sweat and dog. Now they know about the powder before anyone moves.

Through Imani's eyes, the east range's ground floor is the keep's infirmary: fourteen bedridden household servants and the keep's sick and hungry from a winter of tolls nobody paid. She works it cuffed and by hand, her sight gone inside the zone. Davey Corve, nineteen, keeps her door; his father is the sergeant who walked her up the Rise. The keep's medicine comes up from the Gate's apothecary on Vantic's House's account, and the empties go back with a return slip. On Sunday Imani writes the slip in the dispensary's own dosing shorthand, which only Kaldo's pharmacists read: fourteen abed, east range, ground; powder below; wait. On Monday Pryor's steward, her patient with a gut ulcer, names the families who fed the hall party and lets slip a plan to fire the pass bridge if the crown comes. She keeps both.

On Sunday, under flag at the gate doors, Quarrie and Ward meet Pryor's steward; Pryor does not come down. His terms: the Authorisation withdrawn from the March; the March's charter confirmed by statute against any future Titles Act; amnesty for every March man under arms; the March's exchange and tolls kept in coin outside the Issue Act; and the queen released at the Gate on the day the statute is printed. Quarrie says only the Assembly can answer and sends them by set that night. Every flicker that day, and every day after, Imani sends calm. Emeric reads it as wait. She can feel he is close. He waits, because she asks. That evening Agatha Vell walks up the switchback with the March reform committee's delegation, which nobody sent for, offers to stand witness to any surrender, and stays at the field company's camp.

Chapter 42 — Six Days

Nobody reads the proclamation at the gate in the six days. A reading there is the Authorisation's warning that force is coming, and a warning is what would make Pryor move her over the pass. Ward and Quarrie hold it back on purpose, and Imani's calm agrees with them; Quarrie's own plan is to read it at the gate the moment her instrument is ready and a move is imminent. On Monday 17 April the Assembly debates Pryor's terms. Keller rules on the field command's question that the proclamation's hostage-taking exclusion covers guards who knowingly keep the queen confined, not only the men who seized her, but that a guard who frees or protects hostages may apply to the surrender officer for a case-by-case pardon. Treloar is arrested on Keller's warrant for the closure order and the nine dead.

That evening, in the crown book's counting house in the capital, through Tullio's eyes, he has read Pryor's terms in the evening sheet and knows Ostrel will be next. He writes one sentence to his agent at the Gate: release the next tranche only while the guest remains at the Gate. He means keep her in the kingdom where she can be got back, and use the money to force Pryor to terms. He keeps the duplicate, as always. The page shows the sentence and his reason, not which reading of it is true.

On Monday night, through Mira's eyes, a Crestless team of five, Arthur, Mira, Bren and two young knights, in stealth suits, goes up the cliff route on Runa's map. Their night glass works to the zone's edge; inside it the glass is dark, and they climb by the scouts' infrared monoculars, which are mundane. Halfway up the wind turns down the valley, away from Runa, and for twenty minutes she smells nothing but her own ridge. In that window Pryor posts a new picket with a dog, as he decided after the parley. The dog smells the team. They pull back without a shot. Runa's nose did not fail; the wind took it. Pryor doubles the cliff side, and Ward now plans only for a night when the wind sets toward her.

On Tuesday Ferra flies home at dawn to sit in the Assembly, and that afternoon it refuses Pryor's terms, printed by name. She keeps logging the flickers at her desk in Kaldo; the bond does not care how far. Imani's coded return slip reaches Vantic's counting room that day, and Vantic knows the hand and sends it to Ward by set within the hour; it corroborates Runa's window and Runa's powder. On Wednesday Amara's eye from the relays shows only roofs, since nothing can be seen through a zone. Quarrie prepares her instrument, the form drawn and the finding left blank, because she will not write a finding before she has the facts in front of her. Ward's plan is set for two in the morning, the night change of the guard. Imani has a plan of her own, with a nineteen-year-old guard and eleven conscripts. On Wednesday evening the wind sets toward the ridge, and Ward says tonight.

Chapter 43 — Where's My Wife

The night before, through Imani's eyes, Davey Corve read Keller's ruling in the Gate town's news-sheet that came up with Wednesday's bread, and asked her what case-by-case meant. She told him. He asked whether it covered his father. She told him that too. They kept the watch together without speaking.

At twenty to one on Thursday 20 April, through Runa's eyes on the ridge, the flicker brings terror, not calm, and through the same heartbeat she smells the stable yard waking: horses, harness and Pryor's convoy men. The transfer to Ostrel has been moved up, because the Assembly refused his terms and the queen is worth more over the pass than under his roof. The convoy's lead cart carries a padded box with two of the keep's rack stones, to hold a zone round her on the road so no bond and no dragon can find her. A convoy outrider bolts up the pass road, and Runa goes after him before Ward can grab her collar, at a greyhound's pace for a few hundred yards. The horse smells wolf and breaks. She takes the man off it at the bend and holds him by the throat, teeth out, until Ward arrives. Nobody dies. Her birthday flash lamp rolls in the road, still lit.

He stops waiting. On the wall walk one of Pryor's men is on watch, and below the cliff a Crestless knight lies up for two o'clock; Kaldo's own people are afraid of him too. The dragon is heard before it is seen: low, slow, circling, putting out the stars. His gravity comes down before he lands, men on the wall go to their knees not knowing whether it is gravity or terror, and the dogs go quiet. The heat comes with it, plate too hot to touch, a buckle branding a wrist; the Crestless feel it through their suits. Dust rolls over the parapet, and when it settles he is standing at the gate. He raises walls round the hold before he speaks, so no carriage leaves with Imani, leaving one gap open toward the Kaldo line. His roar turns every eye and gun on the walls to the gate, away from the cliff, where the team goes up now, at the roar, not at two. "WHERE'S MY WIFE." Ragged words inside a roar. He cannot feel her a hundred yards away, which is why it is a question. The second time he says it quietly, and his voice breaks. He offers the leave: walk out of the gap, weapons down, unharmed, not followed. A stable boy goes first, then a dozen. The rest stay, reading his long mercy as weakness. He says it flat: nobody left leaves here whole.

The press comes down on every armed man who stayed on the walls, the towers and the gate court, never on the keep's yard, where Imani is bringing the sick out. On the parapet it is twice the weight, enough to stop the men aiming, and bones break under their own plate. A man named Hal Belk crawls. Emeric presses harder, hears his breath go, and lets go on purpose. What stops him is one image: her hands, and her kneeling on these stones at dawn to mend what he breaks. He holds it there. Nobody dies by his hand at the gate. Between flickers he says "Imani?" and "Please," and the men at the gap understand that the dragon was calling, not raging. Behind the gate doors a spark field of crown pattern has been laid, and the team finds it by Runa's nose, hot copper, and goes over it rather than through it.

Inside, through Imani's eyes, three things happen at once. She breaks from the convoy men who have come for her in the confusion, Davey opens the corridor, and the walking sick and the eleven conscripts go out into the yard. The hall party, Tregarth, Davey's father and the two March men, seizes the powder cellar. The Crestless team comes in through the infirmary window where Runa said it would be and gets over the fourteen bedridden before the diehards reach the stair, but they cannot rush a lit match held over thirty lives. Matt Lowrie, a March conscript, one of the eleven, runs for the cellar stair to stop the match, and Davey's father fires on him from the stair. Davey sees it. Matt dies under Imani's hands in the yard.

At about five past one the field company and Quarrie arrive, with Agatha's delegation behind them. Emeric changes back at the zone's edge and walks up into the yard in his own body. Through Quarrie's eyes, she reads her finding aloud as she writes it by the yard's lamps: she can buy the patients' lives with passage; she cannot buy a murderer's immunity. Davey's father is excluded for Matt's murder, witnessed by his son and three others. The instrument is offered to the three, Tregarth and the two March men: specified non-murder offences only, not innocence, not a pardon, exile for life over the Cocan pass into Ostrel. To Emeric she says the pass binds the crown, not only him. Davey walks to the cellar mouth alone. His father hands him the match and goes out with his hands up. The fourteen above the powder are freed. Cuthbert is taken in the keep.

At about twenty to two, the minute Ward reaches the gate, Imani tells him what the steward let slip about firing the pass bridge. That is imminent harm, so it is not a confidence. Ward sends a Crestless pair up the pass road at once, and they take two March diehards with a keg at the bridge at about half past two. The bridge stands, and the bridge is the three's named route. At about three, at Agatha's urging and with her delegation as witnesses, Pryor's officers hand him over in the keep's hall. At dawn on the pass road the three walk out under Quarrie's passage. Corporal Piet Neate, Sir Edwin Hartley's friend, aims at the hall captain, Tregarth, and Emeric steps into his line. Neate lowers the rifle and accuses the king, publicly and ugly, and Emeric protects his right to say it. Quarrie hates signing it and says so. The yard is furious.

At about twenty past three, through Imani's eyes, she and Emeric walk out of the gate past the zone's edge together, and the bond floods back. In Kaldo Ferra feels it in bed and sits down on the floor again, laughing this time. Ward lets Runa go at the zone's edge, and she runs down from the ridge. Imani kneels in the road for her before she says a word to anyone else. Mother and daughter first. Runa shakes for an hour, and sleeps in the cart. Then Imani heals the men he broke, Pryor last, splints and pressure first, mana only as far as the cuff has left her, and he lifts plate off men so she can reach them. Nobody comments. Later, near dawn in the field company's camp, just the two of them: she was telling him to wait. He says she was terrified. She says she was about to move. The bond told him what she felt, not why, and neither of them is entirely right. "You're late, and you're loud." "Later. I'm working." She is: the keep's sick.

Chapter 44 — Royal Force

On Friday 21 April Imani asks Kaldo's hospital board in writing for a review of Cal Pascoe's death and of her own decision to work under the relic. It will sit in July. The same day Ferra asks her for the names of the families who fed the hall party, and Imani will not give them: past confidences stay sealed, and imminent harm did not. They do not speak until supper. Runa has nightmares about the two carriages, and Imani sits with her.

From Saturday 22 to Monday 24 April, in a tent with its sides rolled up at the field company's camp below the gate, through Quarrie's eyes, the gate inquiry sits under the Authorisation's own terms, because the Black Moss is coming and needs a rule in effect before the king leaves the ground again. The panel is Quarrie with two members of the emergency committee, the crews' man and the March member who spoke against the Authorisation, the one most likely to be hard on the crown. They hear Ward, the tower men, Hal Belk, and Emeric, who answers every question and asks none. Quarrie's own account of the yard is read in as a witness's, with the panel's leave. On Monday Runa testifies about the convoy in the stables with Imani beside her, and Quarrie accepts her nose as evidence and is careful to say it is not a verdict; Ward backs it with a stable boy who heard the order to harness given at midnight. On Tuesday 25 April the panel finds, first, that the gravity press was unauthorised force, because no proclamation was read at that gate, and censures it. Second, that going early was reasonable, on a moved-up transfer that Runa and the bond each signalled independently. Third, that whether the roar's timing made the infirmary more dangerous, and whether a stealth-only entry would have saved Matt, cannot be known. Matt's death was Davey's father's murder.

The remedies are in effect that evening, adopted as terms by the committee over the set. The Rule on Royal Force says that any force by the king's own person or powers needs the proclamation read at the place, the field command's written countersignature before he leaves the ground, and a written order filed with the reviewing officer. The tower men, Hal Belk among them, are compensated from the crown's purse, and the field command countersigns royal force for the rest of the Authorisation. The king reads it, says he will keep it, and does not say he dislikes it, which Ferra, by set, says is how she knows he does. Ferra keeps the flicker log in a drawer and shows it to nobody. On the king's request and her signature, the Board's band queue changes so that every miner in the kingdom goes to the front, Stannary's men first. The mines inspector says aloud what nobody else does: the forty-one would have been found in an hour.

On Monday Ferra finds that the field company's inventory of the keep's rack, checked against Saban's carts and the Gate's receipts, is short by two stones. In the roar's confusion two convoy men took the padded box off the lead cart and rode south-west, and on Sunday they reached Earl Malloch on the causeway. A deserter from Malloch's camp says where the stones went. Tullio is arrested. On Tuesday night, through the report of a Fennick's Cross carter's wife, Malloch's host of about eighteen hundred, Coldharbour's hired men, foreign sell-swords on imperial credit and his own house's knights, burns the March-edge village and hangs its reeve from his own gate. On Wednesday the field command's herald reads the proclamation at Malloch's camp on the Black Moss, and Malloch's answer is a rope's end sent back. From then the field command has a host in the field making war on the crown. That night in the keep's hall, through Ward's eyes, Ward briefs the king: Malloch has two of Pryor's rack stones, and he will force them. A zone freezes a dragon in whatever form he is wearing. "Then what are you?" "Standing up."

Chapter 45 — The Black Moss

At about five on Thursday 27 April, in the keep's yard, Ward countersigns the royal force order in writing as field command before the king leaves the ground, under the new Rule, and files it with Quarrie's clerk. At about a quarter to six the herald reads the proclamation at the field from the airship by loudhailer, and leaflets come down with it: pardon and pay for any man who lays down his arms and walks out. Only then does he go.

He comes from the keep country at his top sprint, past the speed of sound, about nine hundred to a thousand miles an hour, low, in a straight line, because nothing turns at that speed. He is angry, Fennick's Cross is still smoking, and his fire leaks: a streak of flame a quarter of a mile long across the dawn, silent until it is past, then a boom rolling over the moor like late thunder. A causeway shepherd ten miles off says a star had fallen and was going somewhere. A Kaldo-schooled lad in the field company has the school word for it, an asteroid. Malloch's host watches it come down toward them for the last six seconds. He does not brake properly, and lays his own gravity on himself as he hits. The bog takes him like water takes a stone, and a wall of dust and peat spray rolls two hundred yards across the open ground, knocks the front ranks flat and bolts every horse on that flank. When it settles he is standing in a crater thirty yards across, two hundred yards short of the front rank, fire running off his scales into the wet peat. Nobody dies, by luck, not by his care. Through a sell-sword's eyes, knights of Malloch's own household and sell-swords whose contracts still have a season to run look at the crater and decide no oath or contract covers this.

He climbs out between the host and the road to Fennick's Cross, a full dragon outside any zone, and drops his lines in his own voice inside a roar. Whoever wants to leave, leave now: drop the iron and walk down the east road; nobody will touch you, and the herald has your pay. Whoever stays gets no mercy from him. None. He makes leaving free: every provost or officer who moves to cut down a deserter, he presses flat. Before the rout two storm ballistae fire at him, and one bolt glances off a scale. He gives them the length of the herald's count, a quarter hour. About thirteen hundred walk: hired bog men, most of the sell-swords, boys, and about half of Malloch's knights, who break their oaths to him in front of him and walk past Kaldo's field company, give their names and are paid. About five hundred stay: the household knights who will not break, the sell-swords' paid core, and the men who did the work at Fennick's Cross. After a read proclamation and a free exit, they are an armed body making war on the crown.

On the count's last breath, before Malloch's hand moves, he changes down. The dragon collapses into something the height of a tall man, scales, claws, tail, horns, the gold eyes at a man's height. A heartbeat later Malloch forces both stones, and the zone comes down over the field. Five hundred men see the dragon shrink just as the stones go, and believe what the Church taught them, that dead ground beat the dragons. They cheer and charge. The zone takes his casting, his gravity, his fire; it cannot take the half-form's flesh, which is dragon flesh and stronger than a garrison. A man-sized body can choose who it hits, and nobody who walked is touched. It lasts about fifteen minutes. The first death has no duel and no speech, and his mates do not understand what happened. After that the field tells it by sound, then smell, then silence. Malloch is last, on the rise. Nobody is left wounded. It is the first time his restraint goes, and Imani is not there.

Imani's field team, sent up from the keep, walks the Moss for an hour, and Imani with it, and finds nobody to save. A surveyor turns back at twenty paces, unable to count what is left. Someone says carts, and Ward says bring shovels. The burial detail, field company and Crestless and young knights alike, does the work, and some of them throw up, and nobody makes fun of them. Ward asks him whether they all fought him. All of them? "They stayed." Nell Gorse asks Ward about her son, and Ward says likely; her son's name is not on the herald's list of those who walked. Emeric sits by the east road until Ward makes him eat. That night Imani asks him what the law gives him the right to do. Did he give them a chance? Did they know what he would do? Would he have stopped if one of them begged? She pushes on the gate, where he stopped when she was thirty yards away. He does not answer well, and nothing rescues him from the honest answer.

Chapter 46 — The Door

On Friday 28 April, in his rooms at the Tower, Armand reads the reports twice and says the line that will matter: if the dead ground did this little to a dragon that saw it coming, then it never beat the dragons by itself, and the Church's history is a lie. Something else won that war: dragons who did not see it coming, ambush, numbers, planning. From Monday 1 to Wednesday 3 May Quarrie's panel sits on the Moss: the proclamation was read, the exit was free and witnessed, the field command countersigned, Malloch forced the stones. Lawful. No censure. It says plainly that lawful and clean are not the same word, and writes down what Ward told him, that nobody died at the crater by luck, not by his care. From Saturday 29 April, through Saban's eyes, the thirteen hundred take a name home to Coldharbour and the sell-swords take it abroad, the foreign press prints it in the first week of May, and on Sunday 7 May Ostrel's pulpits read it: the gold-eyed demon king of Kaldo. The people who coin it do not know him. Inside Kaldo almost nobody uses it, and the Heirs' broadsheet prints it once and gets letters.

On Saturday 29 April, at the Claim's quay on the Isle, through Mother Sabbe's eyes, Brega and her grandson Petrok come off the April trade hull from the Sun Shore with two of the doors' families' people. Ward is there because Brega asked for him by name in March, and the Moss has let him go. From Monday to Thursday, through Ward's eyes, they make the joint survey promised long ago before any face inside the zone is touched: Tess's bore map, Islanders who know the sand, and Petrok's planting sense, which lets a warden of earth feel a wall under the ground the way Ward feels a man behind a door. They find the outer land wall where the map said, and on Thursday a door's lintel under the sand. Brega and Petrok go in alone for an hour. Ward stands outside with his back to it, as he once stood beside the statues facing outward. Brega comes out and says it is to be closed again. Nobody asks what is there. The Islanders refill it that afternoon. The dead are theirs to decide, and they have decided.

On Monday 8 May, day 120, through Ferra's eyes, Mabel pays her western factor's coin invoice, written last autumn inside the window, in notes at par at the county office, with a receipt; the factor's agent grumbles and takes them, because the law says he must, and she does not think about Fulk for nearly an hour. Vantic's shippers settle in notes at par, and she redeems them at the county offices on Thursdays like any bank. The March's discount closes to par within the week, with Pryor gone and the Gate's changers under an interim reeve. Whether the note holds through a bad harvest is the open question, and Ferra says so.

The spring Stone Hearing sits late because of the Authorisation, and Ferra opens her vault: about a hundred and eighty blank seeds held against the Church, plus this spring's drop of a hundred and fourteen, every blank now written with the keel at the count. The Hearing's new works round sits for the first time under one test, Ferra's: why won't a relay do it? Twelve rescue golems are granted, where Emeric asked for twenty. Tess shows her finder, a hand sensor stone that feels a band's cores through rock and timber and reads a body, never a name. One request is refused on the test, the rail signals, because relays can do it. Thirty pairs go to the groves, with a reserve floor of sixty that Ferra wins. On Tuesday 9 May, in the review's room in Kaldo, through Dacey's eyes, Petrok takes the orc families' chair, empty for eleven months. Keller reads the condition aloud and says it is met. Petrok says through the treaty-house's interpreter that his people have read the record in their own hands, and he will ask his own questions.

Chapter 47 — Part Two

On Friday 12 May the king renews his request for the listening post on the shaft's cap, in a paragraph: something else may live in the hollow and could come out the way the young did. From 9 to 19 May the review hears the evidence on the hollow: Tess on the marks, born and under the Isle, and the shore word, paper twenty-one unsealed to the members; Neris's one heart with an echo; Mother Sabbe; a year of nothing at the Islanders' coil; Honor Linden's page; Brega on the doors; and paper thirteen, the Ostrel letter's line about more than one room, read to the members in closed session only. Petrok asks the king one question: whether anything he knows and has not said would change their finding. The king says he cannot prove what he fears.

On Saturday 13 May, in the long room, through Ferra's eyes, Ward wants armed escorts for every healer who goes into the March, and Imani wants neutral passes sealed by both sides. Each is right about the other's flaw: escorts end access for March patients who will not open a door to a gun, and passes make a route for another Cuthbert. Neither wins. Ferra writes the question for the Assembly's health committee, an office with both of them on it and someone from the March, and Mother Brannagh, who has written to Imani condemning Cuthbert, will sit on it when it opens in summer.

On Friday 26 May the review reads part two in the Islanders' hall above the cove, because the finding binds that ground and both key-holders live by it. Present are Keller, the three delegates, Mother Sabbe, Petrok, Duvrenne's observer with Alina beside him, Dacey on the families' bench, the king and Harrold in the front row, Ferra at the back, recused, and Kaldo News. The finding: the hollow under the Isle is sealed and closed to every crown, the crown of Randillia included, and its key is held by the Islanders and the orc families together. It binds Randillia's crown by the king's and Harrold's signatures and is recommended to Duvrenne. Sealed means an iron cover set into the rock over the filled shaft's cap, with two locks, one key for Mother Sabbe and one for Petrok, both needed to open it. Nothing below is touched; it is sealed, not burned. The listening post is refused again; the coil outside the fence stays. On what else the hollow holds, the review cannot say. It records what is known, two hearts, born and under the Isle, the young's marks, a year of silence at the coil, and finds that not knowing is a reason to seal it, not to open it.

The king says once why he dislikes it: it shuts him out of the one place the Ostrel letter told him to fear, on the word of people who cannot see what he fears, and he cannot tell them what that is. He says the review has done what it promised and he will keep every line. He and Harrold sign that afternoon, and the two keys are cut at the Claim's forge and handed over in front of the clerk. That night, in Kesrath's guest rooms, he tells Ferra and Imani, who know what he fears, that the clock does not care what a review thinks. Imani says a sealed door is still a door, and somebody has a key. Ferra says, "That's two." He knows she is counting answers to Corrick's question, the strike being one, and he says yes again, and does not sleep well. The review rises, its work done and its records sent to the First Room's archive, and Petrok and Brega sail home on the June hull with Petrok's key. On Thursday 1 June, by Alina's hand, Kesrath accepts the recommendation for Duvrenne's crown, asking only that a Duvrenne witness stand at any opening, because of his cutters' camp and Remy Doucet and Luc Aubel. The same day Vantic learns her loss on the window: about seven thousand Gold Rand. Duvrenne's credit is repaid on 2 June and the tower released. She tells the king the number; he says the Board should print it; she says she sent it to them on Thursday.

The field company stood down on Monday 15 May, with no armed body in the field since 27 April, the March under interim public reeves and the pass bridge kept. On Wednesday 7 June, through the crews' man's eyes, the committee's last report lists force used under the Authorisation three times: the chain, lawful; the gate, censured; the Moss, lawful. About seventeen hundred were pardoned and paid, most of them the thirteen hundred who walked. On Thursday 8 June, the ninetieth day, nobody moves a renewal. The crews' man asks whether the crown wants one. The king says the crown wants what the field command reports, and the field command reports none. He would have liked thirty days more, because the Church's stones are still sold somewhere and Malloch's paymasters abroad are still paid, and he does not ask. The pardon pay and the interim reeves go on the ordinary budget, and Quarrie's instrument and every published finding survive the lapse. At midnight Ward hands the field command's seal back to the committee's clerk, and nothing happens, which is the point.

Where the Story Stands After Chapter 47

Imani is home. She was taken from the Stannary hall by a forced stone and a drain cuff, held six days in Pryor's keep, and brought out on the night the king stopped waiting. Cal Pascoe died because the only surgeon who could see his torn vessel had been taken, and she has asked for a review of her own choice to work under the relic. Sir Edwin Hartley and Matt Lowrie are dead. Davey's father is charged with Matt's murder, and Tregarth and two March men went into exile over the Cocan pass under Quarrie's instrument, which binds the crown. Pryor, Sayer, Treloar, Cuthbert, Wystan and Tullio, whose one sentence about the guest at the Gate is now evidence, all face the summer's trials, as do Corrick, Kezia and Gethin. Kezia drew the map and asked that it never be counted.

The king has been censured once and found lawful once. The gate inquiry censured his gravity press as force without a proclamation, wrote the Rule on Royal Force that night, and compensated the men he broke. A week later, under that rule, he landed short of Malloch's host, let thirteen hundred walk, and killed the five hundred who stayed in fifteen minutes inside their own dead zone. The inquiry called it lawful and said lawful and clean are not the same word. Abroad and in Ostrel's pulpits he is now the gold-eyed demon king of Kaldo, and Armand has concluded that the Church's history of the old war is a lie. Imani asked him what the law gives him the right to do, and he has not answered well. The Rule on Royal Force lapsed with the Authorisation, and whether the Assembly makes it a standing law is still open.

The Isle Works Review has risen. Its second part seals the hollow against every crown, with two keys held by Mother Sabbe and Petrok, and Duvrenne has agreed. Ferra has counted it as the second answer to what keeps the next king from taking it all back. Brega went through an old door under the sand and had it closed again. The note held through day 120, Vantic lost about seven thousand and has her tower back, and the March's discount has closed. Twelve rescue golems and a finder have been granted, and miners go first for bands. The Authorisation lapsed at midnight on 8 June with no armed body in the field.

This batch is about a man whose love and whose power point the same way, and the laws written to stand between them. He waited because she asked, and then stopped when she was afraid. He stopped short of killing at the gate because of an image of her hands, and did not stop on the Moss, where she was not. The institutions censured, permitted and sealed, and he obeyed every line, but the question Imani asked him is still open.
