Book 3, Batch 10: Needs

Chapters 91–99 | Sunday 9 December, Year 20 - Tuesday 30 April, Year 21

The Titles Act is law and the League has voted without the king in the room. The Mage Corps, about seventy mages strong, has spent its first year on the old capital's works and on the Grand Library's footings. Kaldo has electric buses, a glow grid that can spell a name badly, and a bench arm that closes when someone thinks about closing a hand. The groves stand at about five hundred trees, and Ferra's margin holds nineteen Watcher sightings that rise with her air column. Corrick has a docket number and an adjourned appeal. The south road has been promised the cultivation method.

This batch is the bridge into the year skips. The chapters still keep their dates, but the gaps between them open to weeks, and each one starts with what moved while nobody was looking. The king turns from the long fight with the money houses to the work he came home to do, raising the whole kingdom, and finds that the hardest part is not building but promising only what can be staffed and paid for.

The world outside starts to press in. A border lord from Velmoor comes south to ask for what his own court won't give him. A town chooses its sewers over the king's power station. And one spring night something comes up out of the sea into Aumere's harbour, and the merfolk queen rings Kaldo to ask whether it belongs to them.

Chapter 91: Needs

On Monday 10 December, in her office, through Ferra's eyes, the week since the League's last scene has gone the way weeks go. The cards have ended and the visitor passes have begun. The Library's footings went in before the frost. The Year 21 budget is due before the year turns, and the Corps goes kingdom-wide on it. Ferra builds the sheet she means to put in front of the king: seventy-two mages, about two hundred readers by the year's end, the stewards' list of towns, and the cost of one town's water works, clinic and power line, written not in coin but in crew-seasons and nurses.

On Tuesday and Wednesday, in the castle's long room, through Emeric's eyes, the council of needs sits. Ferra, Imani, Bram for Kaldo's works, Weekes for the old capital's works and the Corps, Linus Vance with the corridor book, the steward Hale and the crown demesne's steward, Aldane's steward on the phone, and Osric for what a new grove close costs the Watch. Emeric wants to stand up at the budget and promise every town in Randillia clean water, a clinic and electric light by Hollow Night Year 22.

They take it apart one piece at a time. Weekes says a crew of six digs one town's water works in a season, and every crew also has to train the locals who will run the works after it leaves, or the king has built a fountain that stops. Seventy mages is a dozen crews. Imani says a clinic with no trained nurse is a building with a lie on the door, that nurses and readers take a year or more to make, and that no mage makes them faster. Ferra reads him the line items he would need and the taxes they would take, and reminds him whose purse it is. The stewards say a duke's land needs the duke. Waylon says yes for Aldane before the question is finished. Tarrowmede has not answered a letter since the Titles Act. The pilot towns have their own councils now and decide their own order.

He loses the promise. What he gets is the essential floor first, safe water, waste, a clinic and a road for food, in Kaldo's states and the crown demesne first and the rest by steward and by the towns' own leave, with the Corps riding beside the holdings teams and every crew leaving trained hands behind. Ferra will print a ledger every quarter, district by district, showing where things work and where they do not. The line in his budget speech becomes every district on the ledger, and the ledger in print. He dislikes the word uneven on its first page. Ferra prints it anyway.

On Wednesday evening, in the Works' board room before the night hull back to the capital, through Weekes's eyes, he pins twelve crews to their first jobs. Brask's river power station goes at the top, because the king wanted one place to show the kingdom and Brask asked first. Weekes thinks the king has picked the wrong job, and says so to nobody.

The council of needs runs over and Emeric misses Runa's Wednesday in the yard, the first one he has missed. He tells her so himself that night and that he sent Ward. The next week he moves a sitting so he can be there.

Chapter 92: The First Picture

On Thursday 20 December, in the Assembly, through Corbin's eyes, the Year 21 budget comes to the floor. The Corps asks to hire about a hundred and sixty more. Penhallow's and Tarrowmede's members cut it to about a hundred and twenty, because every house mage who leaves a lord for the Corps leaves a town without its well-mage, and they read out a list of names to prove it. The king does not argue it on the floor. Corbin, who moved the budget through committee, notes that it cost him the Corps' year and that he let it go.

The same sitting passes the Highways Act. The trunk roads in Linus Vance's corridor book will be built as kingdom works and the states will build the branches, and the first highway will run from Kaldo to Milford, because the farm trucks need it. Wystan votes for it; his furnaces sell the reinforcing bar. The mills' lawyer from the south road votes for it too, which surprises nobody but Maudie.

That afternoon, in the ration hall beside his church, through Doran's eyes, the vote comes out of a box on a table. It is television, the kingdom's first broadcast: a grey, flickering picture the size of a dinner plate on a picture tube that grew out of Tess's bench oscilloscope, carried from a camera behind one of Ivo's lenses to the radio mast and out to about forty sets in Kaldo's halls and clinics and, by relay, Milford's hall. There is no magic in it. Kaldo News chose what went out first, not the crown, and its editor said the first thing a kingdom should see on a screen is its own money being spent. Parish women and dockers watch the members they elected vote, and a farmer who has never seen the Assembly asks which one is his. That night Doran writes his bishop, honestly, that anyone in Kaldo can now watch the Assembly spend, and that he expects Ostrel to hear of it by spring. He does not write what he thinks Ostrel will make of it.

On Saturday 22 December, on the Works Town flats, through Tess's eyes, the maglev runs its first mile. The bench rig has hummed on her bench since summer. Now a sled loaded with sandbags floats a finger's width off a mile of steel on the electromagnets alone, the chips correcting the gap faster than her old relays ever could, and goes round at walking pace and then at about sixty miles an hour. Then she lights the sealed stones as well, and it runs on both lifts at once, which is the rule: if one fails, the other carries. Her old failed page is pinned to the control hut's wall.

In the hut afterwards Ferra asks what the trucks on the new highway will burn. "Burn nothing we can plug in," Emeric says. "Burn nothing from the ground we can grow." Batteries for the trucks, made fuel for the jets, and the oil in the ground kept as a material and a reserve. Ferra writes it in the book. Tess asks what happens when somebody finds a lake of oil under a duke. Then he'll be rich, Emeric says, and we won't buy it.

On the ration hall's wall beside the grey moving picture, Kaldo News has pinned Ivo's first colour plate: Neris on the K Endeavor's deck with her colour up, caught in a long exposure she sat still for. Doran writes his bishop that the grey one moves and the coloured one does not, and that he does not know which frightens the Church more. In the control hut, on the shelf over the magnet rig, Tess's library: volume nine with a pencil in it, and the Second Course's first two volumes, dog-eared where the chip line needed them in the spring.

In the control hut, through Tess's eyes, the open line's chip slices have come back dead every batch since October while the Forge's lots work, so nobody can blame the design. Two floors down, Wren's first programming class meets on the assembler and the shared library.

Chapter 93: The Long Table

On Tuesday 25 December, in the castle's long hall, through Amara's eyes, the Long Table fills as it does every year. Maeve and Mira sit together at the first table since the pumpkin patch made it public, and the family makes nothing of it and Rowena makes a great deal of it. Zale and Ilenne are up from Aumere again, and Ilenne gives without keeping a tally. Runa, thirteen, has made everybody something again, and this year the things are useful, which she is proud of. Bren gets a whistle he cannot make stop.

Amara sits in her golem, which cannot taste the goose. Her own body is mending in the dome. She tells Harrold that this is the last Long Table she will sit at in this. She does not promise a date. Imani hears her and does not correct her, and Amara notices.

That morning, through Ferra's eyes, Kaldo News asked to carry the Long Table on the sets. The family said no; it is a family's table, and the Assembly is what the sets are for. The editor took it well and printed that he had asked. Ferra thinks it was the right answer and the first of many times they will be asked. Before the pudding Emeric tries to take Ferra and Imani out of the hall, and Ferra says after the pudding, and he stays for the pudding. Mochi sits on the end of the table until September takes the warm spot. On New Year's Day the sky gets its thirty-seventh entry.

Chapter 94: The Class

On Monday 7 January, in the back room of Cinderhale's clinic, through Gethin's eyes, the south road's first cultivation class begins. Twelve people sit on benches with the printed warning sheet on every chair, and the sheet is read aloud before anything else. Gethin has signed up because the king said, outside the League's hall in September, that he would make this available in a couple of months, and it has been four, and Gethin wants to know whether it was a promise or a trick. A reader from Mabyn Hoskin's class teaches the Kindling and the Current. In Cinderhale's thin air that is all she will teach; the press that forms a core needs denser air and a reader beside you, so it is done at Wayford's Kindling hall by the river close, a day by train, for those who have had the Current a month.

On the first day an older woman pushes too hard, and the reader sees her go wrong and stops her, using the very signs of deviation that were read out an hour before. The woman is fine. Gethin was watching the reader's hands rather than the woman, and he understands what the warnings are for. At the end of the second hour he feels it, a warmth with nowhere to go. He says nothing grateful. He comes back on Thursday.

On Sunday 13 January, in Cinderhale's parish church, through Maudie's eyes, the priest preaches that what Kaldo hands out free in a clinic back room is the Light, the Church's for two thousand years, and that it has been stolen. Some in the pews nod. Maudie knows two of them are in the class. On Thursday the class is a third smaller. Nobody from Kaldo answers the sermon, and for the first time Maudie wishes somebody would.

On Thursday 17 January, at the circuit's court at Kestle, through Corrick's eyes, the appeal against the delay on his complaint is decided. The inspectorate must hear his complaint within sixty days and publish its backlog count every month in the county offices. There are no damages for the delay; the statute gives the court no power to award them, and the judge says so in one sentence. Corrick reads the timetable on the steps. It isn't his, and it will do. He signs for the copy.

Chapter 95: Downstream

On Monday 4 February, from the window of Kaldo's works hull, through the eyes of Lord Gerent Brannick, Kaldo comes up out of the haze. He is warden of Brannick's Ford, a Velmoor river crossing over the northern border from Aldane, where he can see Aldane's lit towns from his own wall at night and his people cross to look and some do not come back. His court has not answered his letters about it, so he has come himself. He sees glass towers standing inside an old stone wall, a great blue dome at the centre with no tower between it and the South Gate, the frame of a needle tower still climbing, a star of museum wings around a stadium outside the walls, and eight bronze spires leaning outward along the wall. He sees nothing over the city at all. He asks his steward what holds the towers up, and the steward does not know.

In his guest room he finds the light switch, and flicks it on and off, and on and off, until his steward clears his throat. At home a light stone in his hall is the most valuable thing he owns.

On Wednesday 6 February, in Imani's office at the hospital with Ferra there, he asks for a clinic at the Ford. The answer is honest. Kaldo cannot build in Velmoor without Velmoor's crown, and the Corps does not cross borders. What Kaldo can do is give two of his people places at its nursing school, to learn washing, the register, the tube and setting bone, and sell him a clinic kit at cost. What it will not do is give them the Kindling, which goes abroad only by treaty and never to a Church-bound crown. He is angry for a minute. Then he asks whether a clinic without the Kindling is still a clinic. Imani says yes, a good one, and means it. He takes the places and the kit.

On Wednesday 13 February, at Brask, through Emeric's eyes, the king arrives to approve the river power station, a weir and turbines at the confluence, with Weekes's first crew on site and the survey stakes in. Doctor Mair Penmarric, who kept Brask's still-sickness patients breathing by hand beside Kaldo's medics two years ago, meets him at the stakes and asks him to walk with her first. She takes him downstream. The town's drains run into both rivers above the confluence and out below it, and the lower town's wells drink from the same gravel. The summer flux comes every August. Her book holds thirty-one children buried from it last August, and a column of earlier years. She does not argue against the station. She asks which one he would rather open first.

He knows before she finishes. He says aloud what a weir does to a river carrying a town's sewage: it slows the water and holds the filth against the wells. On Friday 15 February Brask's elected council votes sewers first, nine to two: an intercepting sewer along both banks, settling tanks and a trickling filter below the town, the lower town's wells closed, and a main laid from upstream. The Corps will cut the trenches with diggers and a steam shovel beside it, and Brask's own men will be trained to run the works. The station goes to next year's budget, and the king agrees in writing. When Tess suggests by phone that one of Mochi's budded slime colonies could work in Brask's tanks, the way one now works beside Kaldo's own recycling plant, he says no. A colony needs a king slime to lead it, and Brask has no Mochi; what a town is left with has to work by itself.

On the train home he phones Weekes and tells him to move the crew. Weekes says yes, Your Majesty, in the tone of a man who knew in December.

From the hull and then on the ground, Brannick sees a February city dressed the way no court dresses: jeans of indigo twill, now with Kaldo cotton in it, jackets with buttons and the new zips, joggers and rayon jersey under coats, women in trousers at work and nobody looking twice, children in rubber boots. A Kaldo chambermaid's plain jersey is better cloth than his wife's best. He asks what it costs, is told, and does not believe the number.

Chapter 96: Names

On Wednesday 6 March, Lantern Night, through Maudie's eyes, the lanterns going up over Kaldo's lanes are on the sets for the first time, watched in Milford's hall and nowhere in Cinderhale, which has no set yet. Maudie hears about it on the radio and thinks Cinderhale should have one.

The League's strike fund now pays relief to sick millhands, and the League prints its ledger on the hall wall, every name and every sum, as it always has. One line names Jago Crewe, a millhand off with a bad chest, and his family's relief. On Monday 11 March the mill dismisses him for unreliability.

On Monday 18 March, in the League's back room, Maudie does not call it the price of being open. She changes the rule that night: names will go to an independent auditor elected by the League, and the totals, the decisions and the money stay on the wall. Corrick objects. We used to publish every name, he says. And one of those names lost his job, Maudie says. "A secret ballot isn't dishonest because a mill lord can't read it." He concedes, not graciously. The League's lawyer files a retaliation complaint for Jago Crewe and amends the backlog suit, to ask for the inspectorate's backlog by age and by class of complaint, with no complainant's name. Jago Crewe's year without wages begins now. Nobody counts it against the old Hearth; it is the open League's mistake.

The same day, through Corrick's eyes, the dye works is fined for the thirty-two-hour shift, thirty silver and the overtime paid back to three men, eight months after he filed. He goes to tell the three men himself and finds one of them already knew from the backlog board in the county office. Corrick laughs, for once.

Chapter 97: Is It Yours?

On Friday 22 March, at night, on the harbour wall at Aumere's Rim, through Zale's eyes, something comes up the fetch line into the moorings: a shell over a soft body, longer than a merfolk skiff. The night glass on Zale's helm is no help. The thing is as cold as the water it came out of, so the heat layer shows nothing, and the light grid shows only what is lit, which is the harbour lamps. The merfolk see better in dark water than any glass. Aumere's watch drives it off with bidents and a boil, heating the water around it, and that is their fight and their decision. Zale holds a line on the wall and pulls a wounded watchman out. Three die. The thing goes back down the fetch line, and nobody kills it.

When the thing's shell comes up over the wall's lip at him, Zale is dry and on stone, and there is no time to jump, and he does what Ilenne has spent three years telling him to, the way a merfolk child asks for legs on the Rim's steps instead of waiting for the air to force them: he asks, and stops telling himself he can't. The partial form comes without the sea, wings and all, weaker than in the water because he is out of his element, and it is enough to hold the line on the wall with the wounded man behind him. It was never the sea. Ilenne, in the water below, goes blue in front of her whole watch. A week earlier, on Saturday 16 March, Maeve held a strong partial form under a clear sky for the first time, as long as she liked, in the gravity hall with Emeric counting: three years of trading her lightning for his training.

On Saturday morning, in Kaldo's line room, through Ferra's eyes, Ilenne rings. Her voice shows nothing, the merfolk way. She says what happened. Then she asks: "Is it yours?" It is not her question; it is her people's, and the gold eyes are why. Emeric says no. He also says he cannot prove it. There is no carcass, and the mark that would say where a beast was made sits behind its eyes. He does not dress it up.

On Monday 25 March, through Emeric's eyes, he goes over what Kaldo can and cannot do. It can send the Register of Drifters south on the next hull, with shut cases, so that if Aumere's watch ever kills one, the head can come to Kaldo sealed and Tess can read its mark with an Aumere delver watching. It can send Imani's rules for anyone who touches the flesh, and keep the sets open day and night. It cannot fight Aumere's war, because the old treaty, Kaldo's own Arms Act and Aumere's law all say so, and it cannot answer the question with anything but the king's word until a mark is read. The light sand table in the line room becomes the far board: one pin at the Rim's harbour, the date, and Ilenne's name. Ferra starts a new sheet for the gap between attacks and puts one dot on it. One dot isn't a line, she says. Then we wait for the second, he says, and neither of them likes saying it.

Chapter 98: The Pass Vote

On Tuesday 2 April, in the castle kitchen, through Runa's eyes, she turns fourteen. Ferra makes the day a holiday from lessons, which Runa thinks is a trick. Ward gives her a proper whetstone and shows her how not to ruin a blade with it. Mochi gets a candle and does not approve. Privately, she asks Emeric how old you have to be for the press that forms a core. Eighteen, he says; it is Imani's rule, and he will not argue with Imani on her behalf. She takes it better than he expected.

On Saturday 6 April, in the old toll hall at Pryor's Gate, through Agatha Vell's eyes, the Cocan March votes. The question is what becomes of the three things Pryor's charter did that nobody owns now that hereditary rule has ended: the pensions paid to the widows of pass guards, the charter chest kept against a failed winter, and the pass toll that paid for both. Should a public toll, kept by the March's elected council, go on paying for the pensions and the chest as March law, or should both pass into the kingdom's common funds and the toll end? The March votes to keep them, about sixty-one in every hundred, on about seven in ten of the roll. Agatha campaigned for it. She stands where Pryor paid her pension in the rain and knows the irony: he was right that the pensions mattered and wrong that only his charter could keep them. Now the March keeps them by vote, in a public book. She remembers her husband's name without the clerk's book, the way Pryor did, and does not let herself enjoy the comparison.

On Sunday, through Ferra's eyes, Emeric hears the count by phone. He dislikes it, a toll on the only land road to Ostrel, for one region, by a vote the rest of the kingdom did not get, and he says nothing in public. Kaldo News asks for a comment and gets none, because it is the March's choice, not his. Ferra watches him not say it, and pays him the compliment of not mentioning it.

In March Runa cleared Stilled, holding stasis through Ward's footwork drill and a controlled blow to the shoulder, and Ferra wrote the third row. On her birthday, before Ward's whetstone, comes the second birthday problem. She gets home on time and is angry it wasn't first.

Chapter 99: The Second Fruit

On Friday 26 April, at Milford's first close, through Osric's eyes, the groves drop their second fruit, about sixteen hundred seeds across the closes, counted at the cage the same hour under two signatures, his and the Inspector of the Groves'. This time the count agrees with the vault to the seed. Lowell Tilley's close has a new picket.

On Saturday, in her office, through Ferra's eyes, Emeric asks for three rack runs, about twelve hundred seeds into the ground. Ward allows two, about eight hundred, because every new close needs a Watch post and Osric does not have the men. The Assembly's grove line, voted in December, funds two. The king loses again, to his own institutions, and plants what he is allowed. Saban's man in the north sends word of a pass road beyond the Cocan Range that is closed on feast days, with nothing on it to protect that he can see, and Ferra adds it to her page of what else they are hiding. The knights on winter watch at the closes have taken to going without their coats on mild nights, and argue about whether that is the air or the coats getting better. Ferra's margin now holds about twenty-seven Watchers.

On Tuesday 30 April, through Emeric's eyes, a hundred of the kept seeds go into Ferra's book for the old peoples' groves past the wall, by their leave, to be offered over the sets. Ferra asks whose leave first. Ilenne's, he says. Out of habit, Ferra moves the far board's sheet with its one dot beside her air column. Neither of them says why.

In April, in Ferra's office, Isa Brann finds the cause: a trace of sodium in the oxide, a fingerprint's worth, at a works that sits by the sea. The first chip off the open line, a NOR gate, goes round the room, and nobody can see it. Amara, in her golem, holds it the longest. Ferra's yield line moves for the first time, slowly.

Where the Story Stands After Chapter 99

The Mage Corps is working across the kingdom on the Year 21 budget, about a hundred and twenty more mages hired than before and fewer than the king wanted. Every district is on a quarterly ledger that calls the kingdom's service uneven, and the king has stopped promising what nobody can staff. The Highways Act is law and the first highway, Kaldo to Milford, is being cut. Brask is digging sewers instead of a power station, by its own council's vote. Television reaches about ninety sets in Kaldo's states, and Kaldo News decides what goes on them. The maglev has run its first mile, and the king has said what Kaldo will burn: nothing it can plug in or grow.

The south road has its first cultivation class, and the Church has preached against it. Corrick's appeal and his complaint are both decided the long way, and the dye works has paid. The League keeps its names with an elected auditor now, after Jago Crewe lost his job to an open ledger. The Cocan March has voted to keep its widows' pensions, its chest and a public toll. A Velmoor border lord has gone home with two nursing places, a clinic kit and no Kindling, and he will be back.

Something came out of the sea at Aumere and killed three people, and the merfolk queen asked whether it was Kaldo's. Nobody can prove it isn't. The far board holds one pin and Ferra's chart one dot. The groves have dropped about sixteen hundred seeds, eight hundred will go into the ground, and a hundred wait for the old peoples' leave, Ilenne's first. The Watchers number about twenty-seven, the young are unchanged, the mother unseen, the count stands at day 949 with five sales, and the switch holds.

This batch turns the book outward. The king who won the argument at home now has to raise a whole kingdom without promising more than its people can carry, and the first news from the wider world arrives as a question he cannot yet answer.
