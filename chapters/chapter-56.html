Book 2, Chapter 17: Fixed Points

Taurre let her read the stones on the Tuesday morning, and Ferra knew before she'd turned the third slate over that nobody had ever read them the way she was going to.

They were in the back room of the watch house, on the Rim side, away from the arched windows: a long low room cut back into the rock, cool and dim, with shelves down both sides like the bench's and a single table under a lamp. On the shelves, in rows, the watch's readings. Not tally-stones like the bench's; slates, thin grey ones the size of a page, each with a date cut at its head in the watch's marks and under the date, in columns, the readings off the fixed stones round the ring. Every stone. Every week. Going back, Ilenne said, further than the watch house, to the house before it.

Ferra had the word book open beside her on the table, and Ilenne on her other side, and Taurre across from them with his own slate and his colour level; and she'd asked him for one thing only, through Ilenne, at half past seven: the slates for the stones near the seal, and the slates for the stable wall, from the turn of the year to the morning of the collapse. Side by side. In order.

He'd considered her for a while when Ilenne put it over. Then he'd fetched them himself.

Now she sat with the two piles in front of her, the structure's on the left and the stable wall's on the right, and read them as she'd have read two ledgers that were supposed to agree.

The marks weren't hard. She'd learned the watch's numbers on Monday night from Ilenne with the word book, the way she'd learned Ilenne's colours in the word room last summer, a column at a time, and after an hour she could read a slate faster than Ilenne could put it into Kaldo. Each column was a stone. Each row a reading: the height of the stone's pin against the water at a set tide, and against a mark cut in the stone beside it. If the stone moved, the pin moved against the mark.

And each reading, alone, looked like nothing.

That was the first thing she saw, and she saw it at once, and understood why six years of the watch hadn't. The pins near the seal moved. Of course they moved. Everything in the caldera moved. The water went up and down with the gas and the heat and the currents, by the week, by the season; the stable wall's pins moved too. The structure's pins went up a hair one week and down a hair the next and up again, inside the same small band, and anybody reading one slate on its own would have looked at the band and said: the season. The caldera breathing. As it always did.

So she didn't read them on their own.

She took the first slate from the left pile and the first from the right and laid them side by side, and took each structure reading and the stable wall's reading for the same day and the same tide, and wrote the difference in her book. Not the reading. The difference. One column of figures, in her own hand, under a ruled line. Then the next week. Then the next.

The difference didn't breathe.

The stable wall and the structure went up and down together through January and February and March, by the same hair, in the same week, because they were in the same water and the same water moved them both. So the difference between them sat still. A column of nothing, near enough, week after week. Then, in her column, in the middle of April, it started to move. A hair. Then a hair more. Then the same again the week after, and never back. The structure's pins were going somewhere the stable wall's weren't. Slowly. Each week inside the band, so that no single slate showed it, and every week a little further along the same way.

She ran her pencil down the column and stopped at the first week it moved. She looked at the date cut at the slate's head and put it into Kaldo's calendar with the word book, twice, because she wasn't going to be wrong about this.

Then she took out the convoy's log.

She'd brought it from the K Amara on Monday in her satchel. The fleet's own book, Sorrel's positions and hers, noon and dusk, every day since the yard. She found the week. She found the day. She put her finger on it.

The convoy had been nowhere near the thick water. It had been about a thousand miles off, the wrong side of the wall, out over the last of Ostrellin's own sea; and the Challenger had been slung under the K Endeavor's keel in her cradle with her hatch shut and Ned asleep in her, where she'd been since Kaldo.

Ferra turned her book round on the table, with the column of differences in it and her finger on the line where it started to move, and the convoy's log open beside it at the same day, and pushed both across to Taurre.

She kept quiet. So did Ilenne.

Taurre bent over the book, and Ferra watched him read her figures in a language he didn't have, and then stop reading and simply look at the shape of the column: a line of nothing, and then a line that walked away. He didn't need the words. It was as plain as a slope.

His colour moved.

It went over him from the throat, slowly, and it wasn't the flicker or the warm or the slate; it was a thing Ferra hadn't seen on him or anybody, a long low darkening, grey into deep grey, the colour of a stone going under water. He held it. He didn't put it away. He let it sit on him in front of the two of them, and said something quietly.

"He says nobody laid them out," said Ilenne. "Side by side. Like that. He says six years nobody laid them out." She listened. "He says he didn't."



The house's first answer came the same afternoon, and Ferra had expected it.

It came by way of the dry hall, where the Rim's house had a man sitting for exactly this kind of thing, and it reached the watch house by runner at four: that the watch's stones were the watch's; that the watch had stood witness at the door for the land man not a fortnight since; and that a finding the watch made for the land man on its own stones, read by the land man's own woman, was what anybody would expect.

"It's true," said Ferra.

Ilenne looked at her.

"It is," said Ferra. "He stood at the door. I read the slates. If somebody brought me this at home with Taurre's name on it and mine beside it, I'd say exactly the same." She closed her book. "So it needs somebody else."

Taurre had already thought of it. He'd sent for the man at three, before the runner came; Ferra saw that when the man arrived, because he came in with his slate under his arm and his coat buttoned and the look of somebody who'd been told why on the way.

It was the grey-haired one. The one from the long table on the Saturday who'd written on his slate when Emeric said he'd been wrong about the clicks, and again when he said the gill packs would fail. A watchkeeper. Older than Taurre. His name was Olleth, Ilenne said, and his family was the Rim's, an old craft family of the kiln islands; not the Rim's house, but the Rim's, born up here and Sael to the bone, with no reason in the world to do a land man a kindness.

He didn't look at Ferra's book. That was the first thing he did that she liked. Taurre offered it to him and he put it aside without opening it, and went to the shelves himself, and took down the slates himself, the same ones she'd taken, and the convoy's log, which Taurre had sent to be copied fair by a watch clerk in the watch's own marks; and he sat down at the far end of the table with all of it and his own pencil and his own slate, and did it again, from the beginning, alone.

It took him until eight in the evening. Ferra sat at the other end of the table the whole time and didn't watch him. She read the watch's older slates, the year before, and the year before that, to see what the stones did when nothing was wrong; and she ate the fish cake Ilenne brought her; and she didn't once look at the other end of the table, because she wanted it said afterwards, by anyone who asked, that she hadn't.

At eight he put his pencil down.

He said a short thing to Taurre. Taurre answered. Then Olleth took his slate and walked the length of the table and put it down in front of Taurre beside Taurre's own, and signed it: a mark at the foot, cut slowly with the point of the stylus, his family's. Then he looked at Ferra, for the first time all day, and inclined his head the depth you gave somebody who'd been right about your own water, and went out.

"He says the same," said Ilenne. "Mid-April. The same week." She let out a breath. "He says he'd have liked to find something else. He says he looked."



Taurre wrote the finding on the Wednesday morning, at the table in the back room, and Ferra watched him do it because he asked her to stay.

He cut it in the watch's marks on a slate of his own, slowly, and Ilenne put each line into Kaldo as he cut it, and Ferra put the Kaldo in her book, line under line, so there'd be a copy in a language the land could read.

The failure at the seal began before the convoy came. The stones near the structure moved away from the stable wall from the middle of April, and the land's ships were then about a thousand miles off, outside the thick water, and the land ship under the sea was slung in her cradle under one of them. The land ship did not start it.

He stopped there. Ferra waited. She thought he'd finished; it was all the stones said that mattered, and it was enough. She was reaching for her pencil to close the line when he began to cut again.

"He says another," said Ilenne slowly.

Whether the land ship's running gear, on the stable inner wall on the afternoon of the sixteenth, hastened the end of a thing already failing, the stones do not say.

Taurre put the stylus down.

Ferra read the line in her own book, twice. Then she looked up at Taurre.

"Ask him why," she said. "He didn't have to write that. It isn't on the stones."

Ilenne asked. Taurre answered without hesitating, as if he'd known since the night before that somebody would ask.

"He says it isn't on the stones," said Ilenne. "That's why. The stones don't say she did and they don't say she didn't. So he says they don't say." She listened. "He says the house will find that out for itself in a day. He says he'd rather they found it in his finding, in his own hand, than proved he left it out."

Ferra closed her book.

She'd known a great many honest men. She'd worked for one for years and married him. She'd never in her life watched a man write the line that would be used against the people he'd just cleared, on purpose, in his own hand, because it was true, and hand it over the table to the people it would hurt.

"Thank him," she said.

"For what?"

"For both lines."



He called the seal's record that afternoon.

Ferra didn't know he could until he did it. The bench kept its own books; she'd stood in its record room on the Thursday before and been refused a test because a bar had no mark, and the books on those shelves were the bench's and the Rim's house's, and nobody else's. But after a thing happened in its water, Ilenne said, putting it over as they crossed the causeway, the watch could call any record that touched a structure there. Any. It was the watch's water. Safety was the watch's. So Taurre walked into the bench's long cool hall at two with two of his people and a slate and asked for the seal's stones, and the clerk at the end of the bench read the slate and went and got them.

Ferra went in with him. Not by the bench's leave. By his. The accused could see what was used against them, Ilenne said; that was the custom, the watch's custom, and the watch's custom was what ran in here today. The clerk looked at Ferra standing in his record room for the second time in a week and plainly wanted to say something about it and didn't.

They read them at the bench's end, on the stone table, under the clerk's eye. Six stones, one a year, and the first: the seal's making.

It was all there, as Taurre had said it would be. That was what Ferra kept thinking, reading it. It was all there, written down, every year, and nobody had hidden any of it.

The seal built six years ago, over the vent, to stop the gas: by the Rim's house, its own crafters, its own name at the head. Passed by the bench, its two marks, merfolk and smith. Signed by the Keepers. And then every year after, a stone: the seal inspected. By the house's own inspectors, men of the house, their names. The face of the seal looked at. No leak at the face. Signed. And the Keepers countersigning every year on the house's word, because the house had looked. And the bench passing it again, every year, on the same.

Six times. No leak at the face.

Of course there wasn't. Ferra sat at the stone table with the six stones in a row in front of her and saw it as plainly as she'd seen the column of differences. There'd never been a leak at the face. That was what the seal was for. It had done exactly what it was built to do: it had stopped the gas coming out of the front of the vent. And because it had, the gas had gone behind it, into the structure at its back, the held glass, and sat there, hot, year after year, where no inspector's schedule had ever said to look. Every year they'd read the face. Every year the face was clean. Every year it was signed.

Nobody had suppressed anything. Nobody had lied. Everybody had assumed. The house had looked where the house always looked, and the Keepers had trusted the house, and the bench had trusted both; and every single stone of it was true.

"It's a good record," said Ferra quietly. "It's a very good record. I've seen worse at home."

Ilenne looked at her strangely, and didn't put it over.



The Keepers accepted the finding by Thursday noon. Ressar sent word himself: a line on a slate, by a Keeper's boy, to the watch house, that the Keepers of the Stones accepted that the land ship hadn't started it. That was all it said. Ferra thought it was probably all Ressar had ever said in a message in his life.

The Rim's house didn't.

Its second answer came by the same man in the dry hall, that evening, and Ferra heard it from Ilenne at supper in the guest rooms, word for word, because Ilenne had made the runner say it twice. It was short. It didn't argue with the stones. It didn't argue with Olleth, who was a Rim man and whom the house would have been a fool to call a liar. It took Taurre's last line, the one he'd cut himself on the Wednesday morning, and read it aloud in the dry hall, and stopped there.

Whether the land ship hastened the end, the stones do not say.

And then it said what that meant, which the watch hadn't. That by the watch's own words, the land ship might have finished what the seal began. Nobody could say she hadn't. The watch had said so itself.

"It's true," said Ferra again.

She said it to her plate. She'd been saying it all week. Every answer the house had given had been true, and every one of them had worked, and this one worked best of all, because there was nothing in the world anybody could put against it. You couldn't prove a thing hadn't hurried something. Not with stones. Not with anything.



Taurre published the finding on the Friday morning, under the watch's seal, as was his right.

He had it cut fair on a large slate and set up on the pilots' hall board beside the hazards, where whoever worked the water would see it, and a copy sent Below to the court, and a copy to the Keepers, and a copy, by his own hand, to the guest rooms, in the watch's marks with Ferra's Kaldo underneath in her own pencil where he'd asked her to put it. Both lines. All of it.

And at eleven, at the table in the guest rooms, with the copy in front of them and the pumps ticking at the dome's end, Ferra said what she'd been thinking since Thursday night.

"We should add our own," she said.

Emeric looked up.

"In the dry hall," said Ferra. "On the dry. Where it doesn't bind anybody's name. A plain charge, from us. That the house blamed the Challenger in every hall in this city for a failure its own seal caused. Its own crafters built it. Its own inspectors signed it six years running and never looked behind it. And when the stones cleared her, it took the one honest line Taurre wrote and turned it into an accusation." She put her hand flat on the copy. "That's not a reading. It's what happened. It's all on the record. Theirs."

"No," said Emeric.

She'd known he'd say it. She'd known since Thursday. She went on anyway, because she'd decided to make him say why.

"The watch's seal reaches the pilots' hall," she said. "And the court, and the Keepers. That's three places. The house's version is in every kiln yard and rope-walk and fish market on Sael, and it's in the shallows, and by the end of next week it'll be wherever the winged people carry the Rim's news, which is everywhere we're going. The land ship finished it. Taurre won't go out and argue it. That's not his work. The finding sits on a board. And the house won't be quiet just because we are." She looked at him steadily. "If we don't say it, nobody does. And their version stands everywhere ours doesn't reach."

"I know," said Emeric.

"Then why not?"

He was quiet for a moment.

"Because the finding's the watch's," he said. "It speaks for itself. Taurre wrote both lines and put his seal on them and Olleth signed beside him, and the Keepers took it, and that's the cleanest thing that's happened in this city since we came. The minute we stand up in the dry hall and tell them what it means, it isn't the watch's any more. It's the land king's. It's a land king accusing one of their great houses in their own court of nearly drowning a man, and blaming our ship for it." He shook his head. "I'm not doing that. Not here."

"Even if it's true."

"Especially if it's true."

Ferra held his eyes across the table.

She thought he was right. She thought she was right too. That was the trouble. He was right that it would turn the finding into his, and she was right that the house's version would stand without it, and both of those were going to be true tomorrow and the day after and every day until Kaldo left this water; and neither of them was going to change the other's mind across a table at eleven in the morning.

"There's something else," she said. "Isn't there."

He didn't answer that.

She put it on her list. She didn't show him what.



Maeve had rigged the curtain on the second night, two blankets and a line across the far end of the guest dome beyond the last of the ribs, and announced to the room that it was for everybody's sake and she'd hear no more about it; and on the Friday night at eleven the three of them were behind it, on the pallets Ferra had pushed together on the first night without asking anyone, with the lamp turned down and the pumps ticking at the other end of the dome like a slow clock.

Ferra had her list. She had it on her knee, on its board, with the pencil, sitting up against the curved glass with the sea dark behind her, because there were three items on it she hadn't crossed off and she wasn't going to sleep until two of them were.

Emeric took the pencil out of her hand.

"Work first," said Ferra, without looking up.

"It's eleven at night."

"It's work at eleven at night."

"I'd like that in writing," said Emeric, and lay back down with the pencil behind his ear, and put his arm over his eyes.

"It is in writing," said Imani, from his other side. She was lying on her front with her chin on her folded arms and her eyes shut. "It's on her list. Item eleven. Fun, later. I've seen it."

"There's no item eleven," said Ferra.

"There's always an item eleven," said Imani. "Give her the pencil back."

He gave her the pencil back. Ferra wrote something, and crossed something out, and then sat with the pencil still and looked at the dark glass past the end of the pallets, where now and then a pale slow shape went by outside that none of them had ever asked about.

Imani opened her eyes.

"Why did you fight her so hard?" she said. "This morning. About the house." She turned her head on her arms to look at him. "They're guilty. You know they're guilty. Not of killing anybody on purpose, but of all of it, the seal and the signing and every word since. Ferra's right about the reach. You know she is. And you sat there and said no as if she'd asked you to burn their house down." She didn't move her head. "I'm only asking."

Emeric took his arm off his eyes.

He was quiet for a while. Ferra watched him being quiet. She knew the different kinds. This was the kind where he was deciding how much of something to say, and whether to say it at all, and it went on longer than she was used to.

"Do you remember the road," he said, "the first week? Riding with Ward. The men in irons."

Ferra remembered. She hadn't been on that road; she'd heard about it, years later, the way she'd heard about most things from before she worked for him. A gang of convicts on the king's road, chained at the ankle, breaking stone for the ditch, and a man on a horse over them.

"I looked at them," said Emeric, "and the first thing I thought was, who owns them."

Ferra's pencil stopped.

"Owns," she said.

"Owns."

"They were convicts," said Ferra. "They were the crown's. On the crown's road, for the crown's sentence. Nobody owned them." She heard her own voice and didn't soften it. "That's a horrible way to think about a man."

"I know."

"Why would you think it?"

"Because where I come from," said Emeric, "men owned men."

He said it to the dark glass. He said it plainly, the way he said most things he'd decided to say, and he didn't dress it up and he didn't hurry it, and he didn't make it a speech.

Where he came from, he said, people had been carried across an ocean and sold. Men and women who looked as he looked. Taken from their own country in ships and sold at the other end like cattle, and their children born owned, and their children's children, because the law said a child was what its mother was, and its mother was owned. There'd been bondage on his world in a great many shapes, as there had been everywhere people lived. Debt, and war, and prisons. But that one was different. It was for life, and it was by birth, and it was by the colour of your skin. You were born into it and you died in it and so did your sons, and nobody had to sentence you to anything.

That was all he said about it. He didn't give figures. He didn't tell them how it ended, or whether it had.

Ferra sat very still against the glass.

He'd glanced at her once while he was talking. At her hair, she thought, where it lay over her shoulder in the lamplight. She caught him doing it and he saw her catch him.

"There's an island," he said, more quietly. "On my world. Where red hair's commoner than almost anywhere else." He looked at her hair again, and away. "That's not why you're anything. It's only what made me think of it."

"Think of what?"

"Its neighbour conquered it. Took its land, gave it to its own people. Punished it for its faith. Sent thousands of it overseas to labour. And there was a famine there, a terrible one, while food was still going out of its ports on ships." He was quiet a moment. "That was brutal. It was a great wrong. And it wasn't the same as being made property by birth. And on my world, people used the one to make the other sound smaller."

Ferra found that she'd put her hand up to her own hair.

Imani hadn't moved at all. She was lying on her front with her chin on her arms and her eyes on him, and something had gone very still in her face that Ferra had seen before only once or twice, in the clinic, when a patient told her a thing that wasn't medical and she was deciding what to do with it.

"Then why doesn't it happen here?" said Ferra.

"I thought about that on the road. The first week." He rubbed his face. "I thought it was the magic. One mage does the work of a gang. So nobody needs to buy bodies. And if you crush a people, you never know which of them will be born with the power to tear your whole house down. So nobody does it. That was my guess."

"That's a bad guess," said Ferra.

He looked at her.

"It's a bad guess," she said again. She'd sat up properly now, the list forgotten on her knee. "Most people here haven't got any magic. Most people never had any. The towers kept the glyphs so that nobody but the towers could cast a thing. The mines still needed hands. The fields still needed hands. Bram's trade needed hands. If nobody here was owned, it isn't because a mage could do their work. A mage wouldn't have."

"Is a chain not a chain," said Imani, "because nobody can see it?"

They both looked at her.

"The rite," said Imani. She'd sat up too, cross-legged on the pallet, with the blanket round her shoulders. "Nine boys in ten who walk into it carried out, and the Church calls it holy so the mothers keep sending them. The glyph laws: a mark you weren't allowed to teach your own son. The road gangs. Nobody here had to buy anybody. The Church owned their sons and their learning and their backs. It just didn't need a bill of sale." She shrugged, very slightly. "I'm only saying."

Emeric was silent.

"Yes," he said. "That's right. That's better than mine." He let out a breath. "It was a guess. I made it the first week, on a road, and I never went back to it. I should have."

Ferra wrote nothing. She sat with the list on her knee and the pencil still.

"So why," said Imani, quietly, "this morning?"

He didn't answer at once. When he did, he said it once, and Ferra knew he'd say it only once, ever, to anybody.

"A man kills you," he said. "Takes your house. And his grandchildren say they had nothing to do with it. And they're right, they didn't. They're just sleeping in your bed." He looked at the dark glass past the pallets, and the sea on the other side of it. "That's us. That's Kaldo. Every one of us. We came out past the wall in eight ships to people our grandfathers' grandfathers drove off that land and killed in the shallows, and then we forgot we'd done it. My world still lives on what it took. Most of it doesn't think about it at all." He was quiet. "I'm not going to stand up in their court and accuse their great house of anything. Not because they're not guilty. Because of whose bed I'm in."

The dome was quiet.

The pumps ticked. A pale shape went past outside the glass, slowly, and on.

"So we pay rent," said Ferra.

He turned his head.

"Rent," she said. "If it's somebody else's bed. If we're in it. Then we pay rent." She heard herself say it in the accounts voice, the one she used at the counting house on quarter-day, and didn't care. "And rent means somebody agreed to let us stay."

"Then whose permission are we missing?" said Emeric.

Ferra opened her mouth.

"Start with the people whose water we put that stone in," said Imani.

The dome was very quiet.

Ferra reached for her list. She had the pencil on the board before she'd thought about it, and he caught her wrist. Not hard. He put his hand round it and held it, the way he'd held Zale's under the water at the door.

"Not item eleven," he said.

"No," said Ferra. She looked at his hand on her wrist, and then at him. "Item one."

He let go. She wrote it. At the top. Above everything else on the board, above the bench and the fish and the steel, in her smallest, most careful hand; and then she drew a line under it, and put the board face down on the floor beside the pallet, and lay down.



It was warmer after that. It came back slowly, as the cold came out of a room after a door's been shut.

Imani lay down on his other side and put her cold feet on his shins on purpose, and he made a noise, and she said it was a medical necessity, and Ferra laughed into the pillow for the first time in a week. He put an arm round each of them. Ferra lay with her face against his shoulder and her hand on his chest and felt him breathing, and felt him not sleeping, and didn't say so.

"Nobody here's said war," he said, after a while, into the dark. Very low. "Have you noticed? Not once. Not the court, not the house, not Ushar. Not one word."

"No," said Imani.

"There's a ship at the gate that goes under the sea," he said. "And knights on the K Amara who can breathe their water if they have to. And my brother's lineage said on their steps in front of their whole city, and whose son he is, and whose brother." He was quiet. "Nobody's said war. And every bit of it's the threat of one. That's why the house won't fight in the open. That's why the queen can stand up. That's why any of it works." He breathed out. "I hate that it works."

Neither of them answered that.

Imani fell asleep first, with her feet still on his shins. Ferra lay awake a while longer with her hand on his chest, and felt him lying awake under it, with the pumps ticking and the sea going past on the other side of the glass and his eyes open in the dark; and she was thinking about the list on the floor, and the line at the top of it, and how you'd begin to ask a people for permission to be somewhere when you'd already been there two thousand years.

In the morning Imani was rested and bright and ate two of the grey cakes. Ferra was rested too, mostly. Emeric came to the table looking as if he'd been up all night on a ship's watch in a gale, and Maeve took one look at him across the fish and opened her mouth.

"Don't," said Emeric.

Maeve shut it, and grinned at her plate.
