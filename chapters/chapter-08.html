Chapter 8 - What the Loop Kept

Emeric didn't call for anyone. Word found him anyway, through walls he'd told everyone not to cross. A plate left outside his door, untouched. A message from Bram, relayed through someone else, asking if he wanted to talk. A knock from Ward that lasted exactly as long as good judgment allowed and then stopped. He'd asked not to be disturbed unless something outrageously urgent required him, and the castle had taken him at his word more thoroughly than he'd expected.

His hand found the crystal before his mind decided to look for it, the same habit that had carried it through a pocket for a week straight. Mother's crystal, though he still hadn't found the nerve to say that word about it out loud to anyone. He turned it over once, twice. Not thinking. Just needing something in his hand that wasn't the memory of Tam's name on a duty roster he'd approved without reading twice. The crystal warmed. He let it.

He hadn't meant to delve. He wasn't chasing the loop he'd found last week or the branch before that, wasn't trying to be useful about any of it. He wanted the noise in his head to have somewhere else to go, and the workspace had always been good for that. Structure where a moment ago there'd been none. The closest thing to quiet his brain still seemed able to produce.

He drifted back to the loop anyway, without planning to. Whatever passed for hands in here kept circling the same structure, the way a tongue keeps finding a broken tooth, and somewhere on the third or fourth pass he noticed something that hadn't been there before. Or that he hadn't been steady enough to see.

The loop wasn't resetting. Every time he came back through it, some piece of what he'd touched on the last pass was still sitting where he'd left it. Unchanged. Holding its shape whether or not he was holding it. Data that persisted without him watching it. Not a branch. Not the loop itself. Something underneath both of them, sitting there and staying true with nobody looking. He had no name for it yet. He filed it anyway, out of the same reflex that sorted ledgers and mapped wells, because filing something new was easier than sitting with the thing he actually didn't want to look at. It didn't work. Not really. He surfaced an hour later no lighter than he'd gone in, Tam's name exactly where he'd left it.

Except now there was a second thing sitting next to it that hadn't been there before, something his mother's crystal had handed him without him earning it the usual way. He wasn't sure that felt like winning either.

He found Ward before Ward could find a reason not to be found. The practice yard. Alone, running drills nobody had asked him to run, well past the hour any sane man would have called a night. His forearm wasn't wrapped the way it should have been. He didn't stop when Emeric came into view. He finished the pass first, discipline being the only thing left he seemed to trust.

"You've taken every shift since."

Not a question.

"Someone has to."

"That's not what I asked."

Ward set the practice blade down with more care than it needed. "I built the rotation. I put him on that door." His voice stayed level, which was worse than if it hadn't. "You don't have to say anything about whose fault it was. I already know where I've put it."

"It wasn't yours to put anywhere."

He heard how thin that sounded the second it left his mouth. He wasn't people-sharp enough to fix it in the moment, only sharp enough to know it had landed wrong.

"That's not—" He stopped. "I mean it. But I know saying it doesn't move it."

Ward almost smiled. It didn't come anywhere near actual humor.

"No. It doesn't."

Neither of them said anything else for a while. Emeric didn't leave and Ward didn't ask him to, and eventually that was the whole conversation, two men standing in a cold yard because leaving first would have meant admitting something neither of them wanted to admit yet.

It wasn't fixed. Emeric was starting to understand that certain things didn't get fixed so much as carried differently, by more than one person at once.

There was one thing that did get fixed, or the nearest to fixed he could reach, and it wasn't for him or Ward. He counted the coin out himself, late, by the one lamp, after the castle had gone quiet enough that nobody would ask. Not a large sum and not a small one. The size a man makes a thing when he's done the arithmetic on what a house loses when it loses a grown son, and hates the number for existing, because a number is a thing you can pay and the thing that was taken is not.

He thought about carrying it up himself. He got as far as the door of the idea and stopped, and made himself be honest about why. Not the road, though the road wasn't safe. It was that a prince at the door of a woman who'd just buried her second son wasn't a comfort. It was the crown come to look at what it had cost, and she'd paid too much already to owe him the courtesy of pretending the visit was a kindness. So he didn't go. He told himself it was for her sake and knew it was at least partly for his, and let both be true, because a reason didn't stop being real for having a coward's half folded inside it.

He wrote the letter three times. The first two he burned, because they were a prince's letters, careful and shaped, full of the kind of grace a woman who'd buried her boys could smell across a room. The third he left plain, because plain was the only register that wouldn't insult her. It said:

Sela,

I'm sending this coin in the hope it helps, and for no other reason. It isn't a payment. Nothing pays for what you've lost, and I won't insult you by pretending a purse could. It's only that a house short a working hand goes short of a great many things through no fault of its own, and I have coin, and you shouldn't go without for want of my having the sense to send it.

I would have brought it myself. I didn't, because I don't know if you'd want me at your door, and I couldn't bring myself to stand there and find out. I know what my family has been to this barony, and I know what it's cost you in particular, twice now, in a fight that never once asked your leave to take what it took. You don't owe me the burden of my face on top of everything else. So I've kept it away and sent the coin instead, which is the smaller and safer thing.

If there's anything you need that coin won't answer, Rylan knows how to reach me. You don't have to ask kindly. You don't have to ask at all, if it's easier to have Rylan ask for you.

I'm sorry. I know how little that's worth from where you sit. I'm sending it anyway.

Emeric

He sealed it around the coin and gave it to Rylan, who was the only man he'd trust to put it in her hands and say nothing while she read it. Rylan took it without a word and put it inside his coat, close, the way a man carries a thing he doesn't mean to lose. At the door he stopped, and didn't turn all the way around. "She'll not thank you for it," he said. A pause. "But she'll know you meant it. She's had enough of the other kind to tell the difference." Then he went out into the cold to walk it up the hill himself, and Emeric sat with the empty place on the desk where the coin had been, no lighter than before, and hadn't expected to be.

Across town, Ferra had the pages spread over the whole table by the time he made it to Bram's, corners weighted with whatever had been in reach. A hammer. A cup. A folded rag. She didn't look up right away.

"Three more pages touched, not just the one," she said. "Same hand doing the covering, different hand underneath. Whoever wrote the original entries wasn't trying to hide anything. Whoever came back later was."

"The merchant house name. What was actually there."

"Wasn't a name at all originally. A route." She turned the page toward him and angled it into the light. Under the scraped ink, fainter, sat the ghost of a different hand. A waypoint marker, the kind used for dispatch riders, not merchants. "This wasn't a debt owed to a house. It was a debt owed to whoever ran that route."

She let it sit a second.

"Cobb runs that route."

It still wasn't the baron's name. But it was Petram's word and Ferra's ledger agreeing with each other without either of them knowing the other existed, and that was worth more than the two pieces separately.

"Not enough yet," Ferra said, reading his face before he got there himself. "Enough to know where to point next."

"That's more than I had walking in."

She almost smiled, the same way Bram did when a piece came off the anvil closer to right than he'd expected. "Everything's more than you had walking in, lately."

Word from the capital road reached Kaldo two days later, unrelated on its face. Baron Wexler had petitioned for an audience with the crown's judiciary. Unprompted. Over a matter he described only as irregularities requiring clarification, before misunderstanding hardened into accusation. Nobody had accused him of anything yet. Not on paper. Not anywhere he could point to.

Emeric read the report twice, the way he'd read the discharged debt's date twice, hoping it would come apart into something less certain on the second pass.

He knows.

Not the specifics. Not Cobb's name, not the route, not Ferra's careful work under the scraped ink. Just enough to know that something was moving toward him, close enough now to feel the draft coming off it. And instead of striking again, instead of reaching for whatever had reached Tam, he'd gone the other direction entirely, toward the one body in the kingdom that could still, on paper, decide he'd done nothing wrong at all.

A man that calculating didn't run toward the crown because he was innocent.

He ran toward it because he'd already started deciding how to survive being guilty.
