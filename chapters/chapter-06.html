Chapter 6 - Not Hollow

The Hollow Crystal hadn't left his side since he'd arrived in Kaldo. Under his pillow at night, in his coat pocket by day, close enough that he'd stopped registering the weight of it the way he'd stopped registering his own pulse. His mother had given it to him. His brother had given him the others at the banquet, an insult dressed as nostalgia. Neither had ever done a thing. Every mage who'd ever held it called it worthless, and Emeric had believed them, the way you believe a doctor about a joint that's ached your whole life. Not because you checked. Because nobody ever told you otherwise.

He didn't dream about Amara often. When he did, it was never the assassination. It was smaller. Her hands over his on a practice rune-slate, woodsmoke in the room, her voice correcting his grip without any heat in it. Tonight was one of those. Not a nightmare, just a memory his mind had decided to visit unprompted, the crystal warm against his palm where his hand had closed around it in his sleep, as it did most nights without asking him first. He didn't feel himself cross into anything. No threshold. No falling. No announcement that something had started. One moment he was somewhere that felt like his mother's room, and the next, underneath it, lay over the same frame like a second exposure on one photograph; there was structure. Lines. Logic sitting where logic had no business being inside a dream.

He came awake without remembering the transition, and the stone was still warm. It took him a full minute in the dark to understand that what had just happened hadn't been a dream at all. It was months of nights since he was a kid, exactly like this one, the stone against his skin every single time, finally adding up to something. He hadn't done anything different tonight. That was the part that mattered. Tonight was just the night the total crossed whatever line it needed to cross.

He lay there turning the stone over, feeling the faint heat coming off it, the way tech feels when it's just been used. He decided he wasn't going back in. Not tonight. He wanted to sit with what it meant that the door had been unlocked the entire time and he'd spent a month assuming it was a wall. The next night was uneventful, the same way every night in that cold room was uneventful.

He lay there running the day's problems, and when the problems ran out he missed things he'd stopped letting himself miss out loud. Televisions. Central heating. A blanket that held warmth instead of just weighing him down. Fire crystals could handle the heating easily enough if he wanted to spend the effort keeping one lit all night, which he didn't. The second his focus slipped, it went cold again, no different from holding a space heater switched on with his own hand. He'd already turned over the idea of training something to cast on its own while he slept and dropped it just as fast. That was a project for a version of him with time to burn.

Eventually his thoughts wore thin enough to let him sleep. This time he went looking for the door on purpose. He didn't wait for a memory to carry him there. He held the stone the way he'd held it every night for a month, and instead of letting his mind drift, he pointed it. A flashlight down a hallway he already knew had something in it.

*Show me what you are.*

Nothing, for what felt like a long time. Then the same layering sensation as before, structure underneath awareness, except now nothing was covering it. No memory, no dream doing the work of getting him there. Just the stone, and whatever the stone actually was, sitting in front of him with nothing in between.

It didn't look like anything his eyes would have recognized. It read the way unfamiliar code used to read, before the pattern clicked. A wall of syntax slowly resolving into shapes he almost knew. Not commands. Something underneath commands. Structure that things could be built from, waiting on somebody to build.

He found what felt like a branch. A fork, one path or the other depending on something he couldn't identify yet, a condition being checked. He pressed on it carefully, testing a door he didn't know was locked, and it held. Unmoved. Not resistance and not refusal. It simply didn't answer force, the same way a variable doesn't answer being shouted at, only being addressed correctly.

*Conditional logic.*

The thought arrived with a vertigo he hadn't braced for, half excitement and half something closer to grief. This thing thinks in if-then. It isn't a crystal. It isn't even a rune, not the way every rune anyone here had shown him was a rune. It was closer to something that had no business existing in a world like this one, and every mage who'd ever held it and called it worthless had been telling the truth about their own instruments and nothing at all about the stone.

He found a second shape near the first. Something that repeated, folding back on itself in a way that felt deliberate rather than accidental, though he had no idea what it repeated for, what it counted or waited on. He didn't touch that one. The branch had already taught him that recognizing a shape and knowing what it was for were two different things, and he had exactly one of those tonight.

So he stayed where he was and studied both from a careful distance, the way he'd have stared at documentation of a language he was three weeks into learning, cataloguing what he recognized and using none of it. Branch. Loop, probably. Somewhere under both of them, likely, whatever held state. Whatever remembered anything between one use and the next.

He didn't find that tonight. He didn't yet know that *tonight* was even the right word for how long he'd been in there. Time inside the thing didn't announce itself the way it did outside. He never decided to leave. He simply became aware, at some point, that he was lying in his bed with the crystal loose in his hand and morning light doing the same thing to the window frame it did every other morning in this house that didn't quite fit anymore.

He stayed there a while, running back through what he'd seen, or felt, or whatever the correct verb was for a sense that had no name in either language he thought in. A branch. A loop. No idea what fed either. No idea what the whole structure was for. But it was there, and it was real, and it had been sitting under his pillow every night since he arrived in this town, waiting on nothing but time. He'd just found the one tool in this entire world built by somebody who thought the way he did.

Hollow Crystal wasn't the right name anymore, and he was done pretending otherwise even inside his own head. Hollow implied nothing was there. This had been full the whole time, waiting on somebody who knew how to look. He didn't have a real name for it, nothing earned by understanding rather than metaphor. He needed something better than an insult wearing a description, if only to think clearly.

Control Rune. Not because it controlled anything he understood yet, but because everything he'd found so far, the branch and the shape that was probably a loop, felt built to be directed rather than simply drawn from, the way every other rune in this world seemed to work. A working name. It would do until it earned a better one.

Morning came with a list already half written before his feet hit the floor. He didn't tell Ward. He didn't tell Rylan. He told Bram, because Bram was the only person in Kaldo he trusted with something that sounded, said out loud, like heresy. Bram took it the way he took most things Emeric said to him now, with the stillness of a man deciding whether to be alarmed.

"You're telling me the tower, the mages, everybody, has been wrong. About a whole type of rune."

"I'm telling you I don't know yet. Something happened. I don't know what it means, or what it can do, or whether I can do it again on purpose." Emeric turned the Control Rune between his fingers, plain and grey and inert-looking, the same as it had looked every day since his mother put it in his hand. "I need somewhere quiet to try it again, awake this time. And I need you not to repeat it to anyone until I know what I'm actually holding."

"Wasn't planning on taking heresy to the priest, no." Bram's mouth barely moved. Something behind his eyes did. "You've got the same look you had over the ledgers."

"Feels the same. Bigger, maybe."

Bram didn't ask how much bigger. That was one of the things Emeric had come to rely on without meaning to, the man's refusal to ask a question he couldn't yet give the answer to. Bram gave him the back room and asked no questions about what it was for. Awake, the stone was a different animal. He held it the way he had in the dark, pointed his attention down the same hallway, and got nowhere near as far. The layering was there, faint, structure sitting under his fingers where a moment before there had been none. But the dream had dropped him into the middle of it, close enough to press on a branch and feel it hold. Awake left him at the surface, the depth visible below and no way down to it. Every time he reached for the branch and the shape he'd decided was a loop, the thing thinned and slid off him, the way a word you're sure of comes apart the second you try to spell it.

He stayed with it until his eyes ached, and got the same result every pass. Not nothing. A shallow read, steady and repeatable, enough to feel the shape was still down there. Just not enough to reach it. Two depths, then. He filed it the way he filed anything that behaved like a rule instead of an accident. Asleep, the dream carried him down, and he arrived deep with no say over the descent. Awake, he kept the say and lost the depth. The floor he could stand on by daylight sat well above the room he'd found in the dark.

It was a worse tool than he'd wanted and a better one than he'd had a month ago. Shallow and waking, he could still hold more at once than his hands could sort on their own, and that much he could use tomorrow. The deep floor could wait until he understood what it cost to stand on it. The metal problem hadn't waited politely for the Control Rune to finish being strange.

"We buy our iron out of Grayrock," Bram said. The two of them were walking the smithy yard while his apprentices banked the forge for the night. "Have for years. Little copper too, when their smiths have surplus. Nothing local worth digging for, not since before the war, so it comes down the same road the grain does."

"How reliable?"

"Reliable enough, if you're not particular about price. Grayrock knows we've got nowhere else to buy. Charges accordingly."

Emeric had expected some version of that before he asked, which didn't make it sit any better. One supplier with no competition was its own kind of fragility, the sort of thing he'd have flagged on a production line without thinking twice.

"Same road Imani's supplies come down," he said. It hadn't occurred to him until it was out of his mouth. "Anything she can't grow herself."

"Aye. And the grain, and the salt. And every crystal worth cutting a rune into, Grayrock's hills or nowhere." Bram didn't slow down. "You're starting to see the shape of it."

He was. One road in, one town at the other end of it, and everything Kaldo needed to stay alive travelling single file. He added it to the list without a solution attached, which was becoming a habit he didn't love.

"I don't like this depending on one town's goodwill. Not for something this size."

"Then you'd better hope Grayrock stays friendly, because there's nowhere else." Bram shrugged, the shrug of a man who'd made peace with a fact years before Emeric turned up to find it inconvenient. "For the farm work. What am I actually going to be building?"

"Pipe. A great deal of pipes. Fittings, joints, something better than what the town's using now, which is mostly nothing. And whatever a proper irrigation system needs once we're past hauling buckets by hand."

He'd walked the floodplain himself, twice, and the arithmetic wasn't complicated even without good instruments. Call it two thousand acres actually worked, less than half what the land could carry if there were hands enough for it, the rest lying fallow for want of people rather than want of soil. Whatever got built had to draw from a clean point upriver, well above the contaminated stretch, and run down through the worked fields in branches instead of one long spine, so a break in one line didn't take the whole system with it. No single points of failure. He'd learned that somewhere that wasn't this world, and it hadn't stopped being true because the world changed.

"That's a great deal of metal for one field."

"It's not for one field. I could grow one field myself. Magic makes that easy, almost boring. It isn't sustainable, and it isn't what this barony needs from me. It needs a system somebody else can run after I've moved on to the next fire. Pipes. Fittings. Something that doesn't need a prince standing in it to work."

Bram was quiet a moment, the particular quiet Emeric had learned meant recalculating scope rather than doubting the plan.

"That's a smith's work for months, not days. I can't do that and keep the shop running for the town's regular orders both."

"I know. That's why I'm not asking you as a favor."

"No." Bram stopped at the gate and looked back at his own forge for a second longer than the question needed. "Then you've got a smith. Whole shop, however long it takes. I'll want it in writing, and I'll want it plain, because I've watched what happens to a man in this town who works on somebody's word."

"You'll have it in writing by the end of the week."

"Then we're done arguing." He pushed the gate open. "Come inside. It's cold, and I've been meaning to ask you something anyway."

Ferra was at the table when Emeric raised it, which he hadn't planned and didn't mind. She had a ledger open in front of her, the household kind, not the town's, and she didn't look up when he sat down across from Bram. He'd learned by now that she heard everything said in that house whether she looked up or not.

"Bram." He didn't dress it up, same as the farming pitch weeks earlier. "The smithing's settled. What I'm asking now is separate. I want to hire Ferra. Not to help you. On her own terms, doing her own work."

Bram's cup paused the way it had the first time Emeric had asked him for something large. "Doing what work, exactly?"

"Records. Contracts. Whatever this turns into is going to throw off more paper than either of us can hold in our heads. Who owes who, who delivered what and when. I've already seen what she does with numbers. That's not a Smith's skill. It's its own skill, and I need it."

Ferra looked up for the first time.

"You've seen what I do with numbers," she repeated. Not quite a question.

"Your father mentioned the household accounts balance to the copper every month, and you never write anything down twice. That's not luck." He nodded at the ledger in front of her. "What's the total on that page? Don't add it. Just look at it and tell me."

She glanced down. A beat, no longer than most people take to read one line.

"Fourteen silver, six copper. Assuming the fish-monger's third entry is a five and not a three. His hand's bad this week."

She looked back up with something sharp under the flatness, daring him to check instead of just believing her. Emeric checked. It took him considerably longer than the beat she'd used. Fourteen silver, six copper.

"That's the skill," he said. "That's exactly the skill."

Bram set his cup down slower than he'd picked it up. "You're asking my permission first."

"You're her father. It matters to me that you're asked and not informed." Emeric meant it plainly, the way he'd meant most things since arriving here that hadn't landed the way he intended. This one, he thought, might land right. "But I'm not hiring you on her behalf either. If you say yes and she says no, that's still no."

Bram looked at his daughter a long moment. Whatever went between them, Emeric wasn't equipped to read all of it, and he didn't need all of it to recognize a father working out that his answer wasn't the one that mattered anymore.

"I've no objection," Bram said. "Ask her, then."

Emeric turned. "Same offer, direct. Records, contracts, order-tracking, for whatever this becomes. Paid, your terms on how. Exclusive if you want it that way, and not if you don't."

Ferra closed the ledger without hurrying, the same unhurried she'd used pouring his tea the first time they met without saying a word.

"Exclusive," she said. "I don't do half-measures on paperwork I actually respect." A pause, something almost amused under it. "You checked my math just now. Nobody's ever bothered checking it before. They believed me, or they didn't."

"I don't believe things I can't check. No offense meant by it."

"None taken. I'd have thought less of you if you'd nodded along."

Petram had said three real things since the fire, and two of them were about the weather. The third came the week after the crystal, dragged loose more by boredom than by anything Emeric had done to earn it. A name half swallowed. Something about coin moving where coin had no business going. Then nothing more, no matter how the questions came at it after that. It wasn't a confession. It wasn't even an accusation that would survive anyone determined to argue with it. It was a thread, and Emeric had learned by now that a thread is worth more than it looks like on the day you find it.

Wexler's name never came up. It didn't need to. There weren't many hands in this barony with the reach to move coin anywhere at all. The letter went to the castle steward's office two mornings later, not to Wexler directly, though both of them knew whose desk it would be on inside the hour.

Emeric wrote it three times. The first draft sounded like a request. The second sounded like an accusation. The third, the one that went out under his seal, sounded like neither: a standard inspection of barony revenue and expenditure, the sort of oversight any crown-resident royal was entitled to as a matter of course, phrased with the flat procedural boredom of a thing that happened all the time and needed no justification because it never had before.

Nothing in it asked. Nothing in it accused. It informed, the way a tax notice informs, and that was the whole point. A baron can sit on a request. Sitting on an instruction with a prince's seal on it, in front of a steward who then has to explain the delay to somebody, is a different kind of exposure. Wexler received him in the same room as before, and this time he didn't wait for Emeric to sit.

"Ledgers." He turned the letter over once without reading it again, having plainly already read it several times. "You've decided you need my books."

"Crown books. You administer them. They aren't yours." Emeric kept his voice where he'd kept it, explaining the water survey. Flat, unhurried, a fact stated rather than a position defended. "It isn't a large request. Revenue in, expenditure out, the last two years. Standard practice."

"Standard practice." Wexler set the letter down on the table between them, precise, placing a piece rather than discarding a paper. "For a prince who intends to stay resident. Is that what you're telling me you intend?"

It had stopped being a question about ledgers, and Emeric knew that much without being able to name what it had turned into instead.

"I'm telling you I'd like to understand the barony I'm living in. That's all this is."

"That's all a great many things are, right up until they aren't." Wexler studied him with the same recalculating stillness as the night in the entry hall, sharper now, a man running a total he'd already run once and not liking that it came out the same. "You'll have your ledgers, Your Highness. I've no grounds to refuse a request that isn't, in form, a request at all. You wrote it carefully. I noticed."

"I try to."

"I'm sure you do." He didn't stand this time, and Emeric registered it without knowing what to do with it, whether it meant less concern or only less need to perform concern in front of him now. "You'll find the accounts in poorer shape than you'd expect for a barony this size. I'd ask you keep whatever conclusions you draw from that to yourself, until you've understood why, rather than merely that."

"I don't draw conclusions from numbers I haven't seen."

"No," Wexler said, and something unreadable moved behind the flatness for a moment. "I don't imagine you do."

The ledgers came two days later, carried up by a page who clearly wanted no part of whatever was in them. Four bound volumes and a scatter of loose receipts that looked gathered in a hurry rather than pulled from any archive. Emeric spread them across the desk that night and started where he'd started with the parish records. Sorting before reading. Structure before content. It didn't take long to see the shape of the problem. Only long enough to be sure he wasn't imagining it.

A barony this size, this population, running the numbers Wexler's own stewards had written down, should not have been sitting this close to the margin. The tithe income was there. The expenditure was accounted for, mostly. What wasn't there was the gap between them, a difference that should have existed and didn't. Nothing stolen, nothing so clean as one missing line he could put a finger on. A slow distributed thinness spread across a hundred small entries that each looked defensible on their own.

He didn't have an answer. He had a shape, the same kind of shape the plague map had been at the start. Dots with no cause attached. He added a line to the list he'd started that first cold night at the writing desk. Six items had become nine, and none of the earlier ones had gotten any smaller in the meantime.

That was fine. That was how it always started.
