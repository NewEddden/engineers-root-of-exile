Book 2, Chapter 27: Walked Down

She'd had him.

That was what Mira kept coming back to, sitting on the gate steps at half past eleven with her back against the breakwater's stone and her rifle across her knees and the soot of the kiln yard still on her hands. She'd had the flash. On the breakwater at dusk, with Zale going down between her and Arthur and the sound still coming back off the islands, she'd seen it: a single orange wink on a roof above the big kiln yard, low, at the parapet's edge, four hundred yards off, a little left of the yard's chimney. She'd marked it. She'd said it. Roof, above the kiln yard, flash, four hundred. And she'd had her rifle off her back and her eye to the sight and the parapet in it before Zale had stopped moving.

Arthur had put his hand on her barrel and pushed it down. "Him first," he'd said; and they'd got Zale down the forty steps between them with Ilenne, to the water door and the wet stair, and let him go into the pool on Ilenne's hand. When they came back up onto the landing the yard over the island had gone white, and Linnet was on the set with Ferra's words: a zone over the big kiln yard, the hatch shut, people on the stair.

Mira had had the rifle up again at the roof before Linnet finished. Arthur had put his hand on her barrel a second time.

"The stair," he'd said.

She'd looked at him.

"People on the yard stair," he'd said. "Go."

And she'd gone. People first. That was the rule, it had always been the rule, she'd taught it to half the knights' deck herself; you didn't chase the man who'd done it while the people he'd done it to were dying. She'd gone, and Bren with her, at a run, along the breakwater and through the arch into the dark yard, and spent the next hour carrying furnace hands out of the smoke in the sheds and up off the stair, where some fool had gone back down for an old man. She'd carried the old man. He'd been heavy, and he'd been sick on her.

And the roof above the kiln yard had gone dark, and stayed dark, and somewhere on it or off it a man with Kaldo's lost rifle had walked away.

She'd hated it. She'd done it. She'd do it again. She sat on the gate steps and hated it some more.

Arthur came down the steps from the hull with Bren behind him and stood over her.

"Taurre's shut the channels," he said. "Every boat on the Rim's tied up. The watch has the water. The gate's closed. Nobody's left the ring since the zone went up."

"So he's still here."

"He's still here. On the Rim, somewhere. With the rifle." Arthur looked out at the dark mass of the islands, the lamps along the street, the patch over the kiln yard where its own lamps had come back after the zone closed, dimmer than the rest. "And nobody on this rock can feel it. It's steel and powder. There's not a stone in it."

Mira stood up.

"Then we go and look," she said.



They went back to the roof after midnight, the three of them, by the streets, with Mira in front and Arthur and Bren behind her with their own visors down.

She'd put the stealth helm on. She hadn't worn it since the wall. It was the grey one with the big visor and the two short antennas behind the ears, and when she brought the visor down the night went grey and flat and close, every edge drawn in a faint pale line: the grids. The light-stone grids in the glass took what little light there was, the lamps and the stars and the glow off the sea, and drew the world with it. Under the grids, if she asked for it, the coarse layer: the heat. Warm things a dull red, cold things black. She'd drilled with it all winter in the yard at home and on the K Amara's aprons on the way out, and she'd never once used it in earnest.

The zone was gone. It had closed from its edges at about twenty past eight, Ferra had said, and the yard's lamps were coming back on; there was nothing in the air any more to kill the grids.

The house was a tall narrow one on the island's spine above the kiln yard, three floors of black stone and a flat roof with a parapet, the kind the Rim built where the island was too steep for anything wide. A rope-maker's, by the coils in the doorway. Nobody answered when Arthur knocked. Nobody was in. They went up the outside stair at the back, which every house on the Rim had for the roof, where people dried things.

The roof was flat and warm. Mira felt it through her boots before she'd seen it: warm stone. All the Rim's roofs were. The vents ran up through the rock under the islands and the Rim built its houses over them on purpose and laid its roofs with the vent-stone so that kelp dried on them overnight. She brought the coarse layer up, and the whole roof came up a dull smudged red under her, and the next roof along, and the one after; the whole spine of the island glowing faintly in her visor like the back of a stove.

She put it down again. No use.

The parapet's corner was where she'd seen the flash. A little left of the chimney. She went to it slowly, in the grids, looking at the roof before she put her feet on it.

He'd made a hide.

Not much of one. A drying frame, the kind every roof had, a lattice of thin wooden slats on legs for laying kelp on, pushed up against the parapet's inner face at the corner with a length of old sail thrown over it. A low tent. Room under it for a man lying down. She lifted the sail with the muzzle of her rifle.

Empty. A folded sack where a man had lain on his belly. Two loose bricks set side by side on the parapet's top in front of it, with a gap between them the width of a hand: a rest for a barrel. And on the roof, under the edge of the sack, small and bright, a casing.

She knelt and picked it up with two fingers. It was a big one. As long as her hand. Brass, Kaldo brass, the dull yellow of the Works' own drawing, with the base stamped round the primer in tiny hard letters she'd seen ten thousand times on the range: the lot, the year, the mark.

"Ours," said Bren, behind her, in a whisper.

"Ours."

She put her bare palm flat on the sack where he'd lain.

Cool. Not cold. She brought the coarse layer up again and looked at the sack, and at the vent-stone round it, and at the bricks, and saw what she'd hoped: the stone under the sack was warmer than the stone round it. Not the vent's warmth. His. A long man-shaped patch, faint, fading, where a body had lain on that roof for hours and left its heat in it the way a bed keeps the shape of a sleeper.

She watched it in the visor. She'd learned this on the K Amara's apron in the winter, lying on the steel at night and getting up and having Arthur time how long it took her outline to go. Steel went fast. Stone was slow. Warm stone was slower.

"Two hours," she said. "Not more. He lay here till after the zone closed." She sat back on her heels. "He waited. He watched it all. The fire and the ship and the yard. Then he went."

"Which way?" said Arthur.

She found it in the grids. Not footprints; the roof was stone. A drying frame along the next roof knocked a hand's width out of line with the others. Soot on the parapet's top two roofs along, smudged, where a hand had gone on it. A bundle of kelp on the third roof pushed off its rack onto the stone and not put back. Down. He'd gone along the roofs toward the harbour, and at the end of the row where the houses stopped, he'd gone down.

The water stairs. A narrow flight cut in the rock between two houses, going down to the inner harbour in the dark.

They followed it.



It took them the rest of the night.

The Rim was a maze in the dark, and he knew it and they didn't. The water stairs at the bottom of the row came out on a ledge along the harbour, and the ledge went under an arch, and the arch came out on a causeway, and the causeway went to the next island, and every turn of it there was another stair up or down and another row of roofs and another passage between houses no wider than a man's shoulders. He'd been through all of it. She found him three times in four hours: a wet footprint drying on a ledge in the grids, going; a rope on a rack moved; a dog in a doorway on the third island that wouldn't stop looking at the end of the lane. Each time, behind.

The coarse layer was useless. Every roof was warm, every wall over every vent, every step where the stone came up out of the rock; the whole Rim lay under her visor like a banked fire, with bodies in it everywhere, false ones, warm patches of stone the shape of anything she wanted them to be. She stopped looking at it after the first hour. She used the grids, and her eyes, and Arthur's.

And nothing in any of it could feel the gun.

That was the thing that kept going round in her. On the Rim, everything had a stone in it. Everything hummed. A merman's charm, a lamp, a door, a pump. If he'd had a wand or a staff or a stone round his neck, Bren could have stood on a corner and pointed. The rifle had nothing. Steel and powder and Kaldo brass. A thing made, on purpose, by Bram's own people, to work where stones didn't. And it worked here, now, in the hands of the man who'd taken it off a seabed, exactly as well.

At a little after four the sky over the eastern islands began to go grey.

They were on the fourth island, on a long flat roof over a smokehouse, and Mira was standing at its parapet looking down into the lane, and something in her neck told her to move.

She moved.

The round went past her head. She heard it: not the crack, not yet, the long tearing rip of a big round going past close in the air, a sheet torn in half beside her ear; and then the crack, from a long way off, three hundred yards, from the dark across the inner harbour, and the thump of the brake under it. The same sound. Hers. Kaldo's.

She was flat behind the parapet. So was Arthur. Bren was flat on top of Arthur, which Arthur said something about.

"Where," said Arthur.

"Across the water. The roofs on the far side, low. I didn't see." She was angry with herself. She'd been looking down. "He's been watching us."

"For how long?"

"Long enough to know when the light'd let him shoot." She lay there with her cheek on the warm stone. "He missed."

"He missed by a foot."

"He missed," said Mira.

She came up to the parapet's edge slowly with the grids up and the visor turned across the harbour. Across the water the roofs of the next island stood black against the greying sky. Nothing moved on them. And then, at their far end, where the island ran out in a long low spit toward the outer reef, something did: a shape going over a parapet and down, quick, low, toward the flat ground at the end of the spit.

Toward the kelp racks.



The kelp racks were the Rim's fields. Ilenne had shown them the first week: long rows of wooden frames on the flat tops of the low islands and the spits, chest-high, strung with lines, with the kelp hung over the lines to dry in the sun and the wind, rows and rows of it, brown and black and glossy. At first light they stood out in long dark ranks across the open ground at the end of the spit with the sea grey behind them, and between them, the bare ground, sand and stone and puddles.

Open ground.

He'd run out of roofs.

She saw him at the far side of the racks as she came off the causeway onto the spit, at the run, with Arthur and Bren behind her: a man, tall, in a dark coat, going between the rows with something long and black in his hands, two hundred yards off. Going for the water on the far side of the spit. A boat, perhaps. A way off the Rim nobody'd closed.

She stopped. Knelt. Put the rifle up.

He was in the sight. Between the racks, going away, full length.

She fired.

And the air round him went white.

Not a flash; a shell. She saw it in the grey dawn quite plainly, a curved skin of light round his whole body, there for an instant where her round met it and gone; and the round didn't go through. It stopped. It stopped in the skin of light a foot from his back, and the man went down. Flat. Off his feet, forward, sprawling between the racks, as if somebody had swung a door into him.

A shield. He was a mage.

She'd known it the instant the light went white, and she knew what it meant, because Ward had taught her and she'd taught it to the knights' deck after him. A shield stopped what hit it. It didn't stop the push. A round that couldn't go into a man still hit him; the whole of what it carried went into the shield and through the shield into him, and that was a man knocked down. And the air here was thick. Thicker than anywhere at home. A mage's shield at home would have broken on the first round, or the second. Here it drank from the air the whole time he held it. It would fill again.

How fast?

He was getting up. Getting his knees under him between the racks, with the rifle still in his hands, turning.

She got up too.

She walked.

Straight at him, down the row between the racks, not running, the rifle at her shoulder and her eye to the sight, walking the way Ward had made them walk on the range at home the whole of one winter: heel and toe, knees soft, the muzzle not moving. A hundred and eighty yards. She fired as she walked.

White. The skin of light. He went down again.

A hundred and sixty. He was up on one knee. He'd let go of the rifle with one hand and his free hand came up at her, open, palm out, and she saw the glyph come on the air in front of it, bright, quick, a cast, and she knew what was coming and that she was walking straight into it.

She'd been hurt before. Nineteen stitches, in the winter, at home, for coming in on someone as if she knew what he had. She'd lain in her bed for a week after with the stitches pulling and decided that she was never going to be hurt again for thinking she knew what somebody had.

She stepped left.

One step, as the glyph flared, as the thing came off his hand. It went past her. She felt it go: a hot hard rush of air, the side of her face burning with it, and behind her a rack of kelp went over with a crash and the kelp on it smoking. She kept walking.

She fired. White. He went back on his heels, half down.

And he still had the rifle.

That was what she saw, coming down the row at a hundred and forty yards in the grey light with the smoke of the kelp behind her: the rifle in his other hand. He'd never let go of it. Not when he went down the first time, not the second, not when he cast. He had it by the fore-end and he was dragging it round, the long black barrel of it with its slotted fore-end and its blocky brake, round toward her, trying to get it up, trying to get his other hand back to the grip. A dragon rifle at a hundred and forty yards. If it came up and he got a round off, the round would go through her plate and her and the rack behind her and the next rack. His shield would hold against her and hers wouldn't hold against that. She didn't have one.

She shot him in the head.

White. His head snapped back. The shield took it. He went down flat again, on his back, and the muzzle went wide.

He moved. His hand on the rifle moved, dragging.

She shot him in the head.

White. Down.

A hundred yards. Ninety. She walked, and every time his body moved she put a round into his head, and every time the shield took it and every time the shield went white a little dimmer, and every time the push of it knocked his head back against the ground before he could get his feet under him or his thoughts together or the muzzle round. He couldn't think. She could see that, through the sight. He was trying to cast again, and every time he started the next round came and slammed his skull back against the sand and the glyph went out half drawn. He couldn't get up. He couldn't do anything but hold on to the rifle and drag it, and she wouldn't let him. That was all. She wouldn't let him.

The magazine ran dry at eighty yards. Her hand changed it without asking her. Right hip, smooth base plate. Ball. She'd fired ball the whole way. She thought about the plate rounds, the black-tipped ones with the hard cores, on the right behind the ball; she didn't go for them. Ball would do it. Ball was doing it.

The shield paid. She could see it pay. Every round she put into it took something out of it and the thick air put something back, and the air was slower than she was. White, and white, and dimmer, and dimmer, the skin of light coming up round him each time thinner and greyer and slower to come.

Fifty yards.

He got the rifle halfway up. She saw the brake come round, black, toward her. She saw his finger find the trigger guard.

She fired.

The shield came up grey and thin, like a skin of ice on a puddle, and the round went into it, and through it.

He lay still.

She kept walking. Forty yards. Thirty. Twenty. The rifle at her shoulder, the sight on him the whole way. He didn't move. At ten yards she could see that he wasn't going to.

She stood over him.

A merman on his legs, younger than she'd thought, thin, in a dark coat with the Rim's shell buttons, lying on his back between two racks of kelp in the sand with the sea grey behind him. A plain face. He'd have walked past her on the market stairs and she'd never have looked twice. His eyes were open. His hands were still on the rifle.

She put her own rifle on her back. She bent down and took the dragon rifle out of his hands. His fingers didn't want to let go. She opened them, one at a time, and took it.

It was heavy. Heavier than she remembered, from the range. Wet. Salt-crusted, rough under her palms, gone dull grey at every edge and joint, with a smell of the sea floor on it. It had been on the sea floor since May and someone had brought it up and dried it and loaded it and lain on a roof with it and put a round through Zale.

Arthur came up beside her. Then Bren, breathing hard.

The sea went on breaking on the far side of the spit.

"Twelve," said Arthur at last. He'd been counting. "Twelve rounds."

"Twelve," said Mira.

She looked down at the man. Then at the rifle in her hands. Then out at the sea, where the sun was coming up behind the eastern islands red and flat, and the light was coming along the kelp racks toward them, row by row.



The watch came for him at a quarter to six. Two boats, and an officer with a slate who knelt by the man and looked at his face, and looked up at the three land knights, and wrote; and a name, after he'd asked the boatmen: Pirro. A mage of the Rim. Of no house that anyone would say.

The officer looked at the rifle in Mira's hands. Then he said, in careful Kaldo, that the land would bring it, and him, to the quay. Now.

They brought her in on the second boat, with the rifle across her knees, round the inner harbour in the early light to the pilots' quay with the watch officer in the stern; and she sat in the bow and looked at nothing.

Maeve was on the quay.

She was sitting on the same upturned crate at the end of it by the stack of empties, with her chin on her fists, as she'd been sitting on Monday. Mira didn't know how long she'd been there, or how Maeve had known to be.

The boat came alongside. Mira went up the water stair with the dragon rifle in her arms and stood on the quay.

Maeve got up off the crate. She looked at Mira's face, and at the rifle, and at the soot and the salt and the kelp-smoke on her, and the burn down the side of her face where the cast had gone past. She looked at all of it.

She didn't ask anything.

She came and stood beside Mira on the quay, close, with her shoulder an inch from Mira's arm, and stayed there.



Ferra came down from the pilots' hall at six with her book.

She hadn't slept either. Mira could see that. She had the revolver on her hip and her collar done up wrong and her face was grey and composed and absolutely steady, and she came along the quay to where Mira stood with the rifle and stopped in front of it.

"Give it here," she said. "No. Hold it. Turn it over."

Mira turned it over.

The serial was on the receiver's left side, under the bolt, stamped deep into the steel: a letter and five figures. Salt had filled the stamping, white. Ferra bent close and rubbed the salt out of it with her thumb, slowly, figure by figure, and read it, and opened her book.

She turned back a long way. To the first days. Mira could see the pages going by, the small square hand, the columns. Ferra found the page and ran her pencil down it, and stopped.

"K Amara," she said. "Dragon rifle, the second of two. Lost with its case at nineteen minutes to six on the morning of Sunday the third of May, in the water, when the dragon came down on her. Box full. Ten rounds." She read the serial off the page. Then off the steel. They were the same. "Declared to the watch's hazard log at Aumere on Wednesday the sixth of May, by me, at the pilots' hall. Posted on the board."

"Posted," said Mira.

"On the board. Outside the pilots' hall. Where everybody reads it." Ferra closed the book on her pencil. "Hazards are for whoever works the water. That's their rule. I put up that we'd lost a gun on the bottom, and where, and roughly what it was. Because that was honest, and because it was their rule, and because if anybody fouled a net on it I wanted them to know what they'd got." She looked at the rifle. "Somebody read the board."

"And that's the only reason we can prove whose it was," said Ferra. "The log. My entry, in their record, on their slate, a month before tonight, with the serial in it. Without that, it's a land gun, found on a Rim roof, that fired at the queen's son-in-law. With it, it's ours, lost honestly and declared honestly, and taken off the sea floor by someone who read the board." She was quiet a moment. "Our honesty's how they found it. And it's what'll prove it."

She held out her hand for the rifle at last, and Mira gave it to her, and Ferra took it in both arms, all its weight, without changing her face.



Bram had it on hull four's bench by eight, under the big lamp, in the workshop, with Ferra and Mira and Dagny and a watch officer standing round it and the doors shut.

He didn't touch it for a while. He looked at it.

"The salt's in everything," he said. "Every joint. The bolt's half seized. The springs'll be gone. The bore'll be pitted all the way down, you'll see it when I get a light in." He bent close to the action. "Somebody's dried it and oiled it on the outside, badly. Not on the inside. They didn't know how to get at the inside." He was quiet for a moment. "Or they did."

He took a glass out of his apron and looked at the pins.

The pins that held the action together, through the receiver's sides, little steel rods with their ends flush with the steel. He looked at each one through the glass, turning the rifle slowly under the lamp.

"These have been out," he said.

Ferra looked at him.

"Fresh marks." He pointed with a pencil, not touching. "There. And there. A drift, a small one, and a hammer, by somebody who didn't have the right drift and used something near it. You can see where it slipped. The salt's over everything else and it's not in these marks. These are new. Since it came up." He straightened. "Somebody's had it apart. The action. All of it, I'd guess, when I get in there. Had it apart, and looked, and put it back together."

"To see how it worked," said Mira.

"To see how it was made," said Bram. "There's a difference."

He drew the box out of its well, carefully, against the salt, and laid it on the bench and pressed the top round down with his thumb and counted, and looked at Ferra.

"Eight."

"It went in full," said Ferra. "Ten."

"One into Zale," said Mira. "One at me, at four this morning."

Eight, in the box. Two fired. Ferra put down the time of the second and the roof it came from, which Mira gave her.

The watch officer hadn't spoken. He did now, carefully: the rifle was a thing used in a killing in the queen's water. It would go on the watch's record. It would be held by the watch, as it was, until the court said otherwise.

"It's ours," said Mira.

"It is," said Ferra, "and he's right." She turned to a clean page. "It goes to Taurre. As evidence. And I'll sign for it, as Kaldo's declared property, lost on the third of May, declared on the sixth, recovered on the sixth of June, held by the watch." She wrote it out, in full, and turned the book round, and held out her pencil to the watch officer, and he made his mark beside her name.

Bram put the rifle in a case. Not its own; that was on the sea floor, or in somebody's house. A long box from the workshop, lined with sacking. He laid it in, and shut the lid, and the watch officer picked it up.

"I want it back," said Bram, to nobody. "I want it back with all its pins and I want to know who had them out."
