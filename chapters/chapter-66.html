Chapter 66 - The Same Sin

He did not sleep the night after Ward told him, and not for the reason he expected. He had expected to lie awake on the hatred, plotting against it, fitting the avenger into his defenses the way he fitted every new threat, and he did some of that, in the first hour, the strategist's part of him laying Aldane's true shape alongside the wall and the guard and the walled lawful road and asking what changed now that the enemy could not be traded with. But the strategist ran out of work quickly, because the honest answer was that little changed in the near term, an avenger and a rival both wanted him dead and one was merely harder to talk down than the other, and once he had filed that he expected to sleep, and instead he lay in the dark and found that a different part of him had gotten hold of the story and would not give it back.

The part of him that would not let go was not thinking about the duke's hatred. It was thinking about the hallway. A man who read a woman off her surface and found her beneath him. A man who took her for a lesser thing than she was because she wore no mark of what she was, and who, on the strength of that misreading, decided she was owed to him, and moved on her, and kept moving when she made her refusal plain, because in his arithmetic the refusal of a lesser thing did not count, was not a real refusal, was only an obstacle to be pushed through by a man of standing exercising what standing was for. Ward had told it as the origin of an enemy. Emeric lay in the dark and could not keep it in that shape, because the shape kept sliding, in his own memory, onto a different hallway, or not a hallway, a hall, a bright loud hall full of witnesses and wine, and a woman there too, and a man there too, and the man was not Aldane.

He had not thought about the diplomat in a long time, not really thought about her, only used her, the incident with her, as the pivot of his own story, the drunken folly that had bought him his exile and therefore, in the accounting he had made of his life, his salvation, the worst night of the prince's ruin turned into the door through which everything good had come. He had told himself the story that way so many times that the woman in it had gone flat, a plot mechanism, the visiting princess whose rank he had failed to know and whose rebuff he had pushed against in front of the court until the pushing became an incident and the incident became a sentence. He had never once, in all the tellings, stood in the woman's place in it. He had stood in his own, the fool who ruined himself and stumbled into grace. Tonight, with Ward's hallway laid over his hall, he stood, for the first time, where she had stood, and did not like the view.

He had not known her rank. Neither had Aldane known his mother's. He had taken her for a thing she was not, a lesser thing, a body at a banquet he was entitled to press because he was a prince and pressing was what princes did with pretty women who ought to be flattered. Aldane had taken his mother for a lesser thing on the same logic exactly, the logic of a man who reads worth off marks and finds none and concludes there is none to find. She had rebuffed him, the diplomat, plainly, and he had kept on, because in his own drunk arithmetic her refusal had not counted, had been an obstacle and not an answer, a thing a man of standing pushed through. That was the sentence Ward had used about the duke, near enough, he moved on her the way a man collects a thing he considers his by right, and when she did not grant the honor he kept on. Emeric lay in the dark and could not find the daylight between the two men. He looked for it, the way he looked for the flaw in any structure, wanting to find the beam that held his own case up above the duke's, and there was no beam. The only difference he could name was that his mother had been a mage and a delver and had put her man down with her own hands, and the diplomat had not been, and had needed the court and her rank and a scandal to do what a queen had done in a hallway with a look, and that difference was not a difference in the two men at all. It was only a difference in the two women's power to punish. The sin was identical. The sin was exactly identical. What differed was who had been able to make the sinner pay.

He turned it and turned it and it did not soften. He was not, he told himself, that man now, and it was true, the prince who had pushed against a woman's plain no in a loud hall was a man whose whole life he had inherited and remade, the trauma-drift wastrel he had climbed up out of the wreckage of. But he had done the thing. Whatever he was now, the hands that had done it were the hands on this body, and the memory of it was in this skull, inherited and his, and he could not disown it by pointing at the future-brain that had not been driving that night, because the future-brain now owned the record and the record said he had committed, in a bright hall, the same sin the duke had committed in a dark hallway against his own mother, and had been lucky, only lucky, that his victim had lacked the queen's means to answer it in kind.

The strategist tried, once, near dawn, to make it useful, to file it as leverage, a thing that let him understand Aldane from the inside and therefore predict him. Emeric let that thought stand for a moment and then found he did not want it, because to make his mother's assault into a strategic aid to fighting the man who committed it, by way of his own matching guilt, was a colder thing than he was willing to be even alone in the dark, and he had spent two years drawing lines against exactly the coldness that would find that thought useful. So he did not file it as leverage. He let it stay what it was, which was a fact about himself he had managed not to look at for two years and could not, now, unlook.

It changed nothing he could act on and everything about how he could carry the fight. He had wanted, since Ward's telling, to hate Aldane cleanly, the way you are permitted to hate a man who laid hands on your mother, and the clean hatred had felt, for a few hours, like a simple thing he was owed. He could not have it. Every time he reached for the clean hatred his own hall rose up under the duke's hallway and fouled it, because a man cannot cleanly hate another for a sin he has himself committed and been spared the price of, and Emeric had committed it and been spared, spared into a barony and a power and a life, while the woman the duke had wronged lay dead and unavenged. He would fight Aldane. He had no choice about that, an avenger left alive was a death sentence pending. But he would fight him carrying the knowledge that the thing that made the duke a monster was a thing Emeric had done too, on a smaller stage, with better luck, and that the only judge who had ever put the duke's sin down with her own two hands and no help from anyone was the same woman whose blood was the reason the duke wanted Emeric dead. He did not say any of it aloud. There was no one to say it to, and no words for it that would not make it smaller, and some things a man is only permitted to know in the dark and never to speak, because speaking them is a way of setting them down and this was not a thing he had any right to set down. So he kept it, and slept an hour before the light, and said nothing to anyone, then or after.
