Chapter 66 - Too Personal for Status

The thing that would not sit right was the heat of it. Emeric had by now taken the measure of a good many men who wished him ill, and enmity, he had found, came in grades a man could read the way he read the grade of a hill, and most of it was cold. Corbin's people were cold, a succession player's clean arithmetic, remove the piece, take the ground, nothing personal in it because to a man like that nothing ever was. The org, whatever it was, was colder still, factors and handlers and initials, a machine that wanted him gone the way a mill wants a jam cleared, without malice because without feeling. Cold enemies he understood. Cold enemies could be planned against, because a cold enemy wanted a thing and would trade for it and stop when the trade went bad. What he could not fit into that clean picture was Aldane, because Aldane was not cold. Aldane, on the one occasion Emeric had stood close enough to read him, at the banquet years gone and again through every proxy since, ran hot in a way that no status grievance accounted for, and Emeric had spent long enough building things that when a reading did not account for the heat, he stopped trusting the reading and went looking for the missing term.

The public reason was legible and insufficient. Aldane was old blood, the kind that measured a man's worth by how many generations his name had held its land, and Emeric was a jumped-up exile who had turned a poverty into a power without any of the slow blessing of time that old blood believed such power required, and to a man of Aldane's cast that was an offense against the order of the world, a status inversion, a thing that should not be allowed to happen and had happened anyway on his watch. That was real and Emeric did not doubt it. It was also not enough. Status grievance was a cold thing, a sneer, a refusal to seat a man at the high table, a slow patient obstruction dressed in courtesy. It did not burn. What Emeric had felt off the duke, and off every letter and every proxy the duke had aimed at him, was not a sneer. It was hatred with a fire under it, personal in a register that status could not reach, the hatred of a man avenging something and not merely defending a rank, and a man does not avenge an inversion of precedence. He avenges a wound.

So he went to Ward, because Ward had been at court longer than Emeric had been alive in either life, and because Ward, alone among the men close to him, had the particular stillness of someone who carried things he did not volunteer. He put it plainly, the way Ward preferred. The duke hates my line past all reason of status. I do not think this began with me. Do you know where it began.

Ward was quiet for long enough that Emeric knew the answer was yes before the man spoke, because a no came fast and a yes that had been kept came slow, and this one came very slow. When Ward did speak he did not tell it as gossip and he did not soften it. He told it the way a guard reports a thing he witnessed, flatly, in order, because he had witnessed it, and Emeric understood as the telling began that he had asked the right man by accident and had walked, without meaning to, straight into the center of the thing.

It had been years ago, when Emeric was a small boy and Ward was young and stood guard on the queen. Aldane had come to the capital on some business of his house, and had crossed the queen in a hallway of the palace, in the wing that led toward the boy's study, and had not known her. That was the root of it, that first and fatal not-knowing. Amara did not go about the palace hung with the marks of her rank, she was a mage and a delver and had never needed the world to announce her, and Aldane, arrogant with the arrogance of a man who read worth off surfaces, had taken the woman in the hallway for some lesser noble of the household, a body beneath his rank and therefore, in the arithmetic that governed such men, a body owed to him if he cared to take it. He had not pursued her the way a man pursues a woman he desires. He had moved on her the way a man collects a thing he considers his by right of standing, in the register of you should be honored, and when she did not grant him the honor he laid hands on her to drag her, and that, Ward said, was the last free choice the duke made in that hallway.

"He put his hands on her," Ward said, "and I moved to kill him." He said it without heat, a fact of the record. "I knew what it was to draw on a duke. I knew it was my life for it and I had decided my life was a fair price and I was already moving. I did not get there. She did not need me to get there. She handled him herself, with her own hands, before my blade cleared, and she did it so completely that the thing was over before it had properly started, a great old duke of the oldest blood in the kingdom put down in a palace hallway by a woman he had taken for a servant, and put down not by her guard, which he might have survived in his pride, but by her, which he could not." Ward paused. "Armand saw it too. He was mid-tutorship of you then, he had come to fetch you or bring you, I forget which, and he stood in the hall and did not move, and I have never faulted him for not moving, because he was a mage and he knew what I did not fully know yet, that she was in no danger, that the danger in that hallway was entirely the duke's. He watched her handle it and he let her, because there was nothing to do and he knew there was nothing to do. We both carry it. We have neither of us spoken of it in twenty years. You are the first to ask."

Emeric sat with it, and the missing term dropped into the reading and the heat made sense at last, all of it, the whole disproportion of the man's hatred snapping into a shape that fit. Aldane did not hate a status inversion. Aldane had been unmanned, once, absolutely, by a woman of this line, in front of a guard and a mage, in a way his pride could never absorb and could never avenge because the queen was beyond his reach and then, dying young, beyond reach forever. The hatred had had nowhere to go for twenty years. It had waited, banked, the way such things wait in a man who cannot forgive and cannot strike, and then her son had come up out of an exile that should have finished him and become a power, and the hatred had found, at last, a place to land. It was not about the crystal. It had never been about the crystal, or the wall, or the inversion, or any of the legible reasons. It was about a hallway, and a woman who had put him down with her own hands, and the fact that her blood was walking the world in success while she lay in the ground, and Emeric, reading it whole for the first time, felt the danger of it change character entirely. A cold enemy stops when the trade goes bad. A man avenging a twenty-year wound on the only body left to avenge it on does not stop, because there is no trade. There is only the wound, and the only thing that closes it is Emeric in the ground beside his mother.

He thanked Ward, and Ward inclined his head, and neither of them said the rest of it, which did not need saying between them. Later he would go to Armand and hear the same hallway told from the mage's side, the standing still, the knowing, the small precise recollection of a man who watched a thing he understood too well to interrupt, and it would match Ward's telling in every load-bearing part, which was how Emeric knew it was true and not a comfort assembled to explain a mystery. But that was later. For now he sat with Ward's flat account and understood that he had been fighting the duke as a rival and had, until this hour, misread the entire war, because you fight a rival for a thing and you fight an avenger for your life, and he had brought a rival's tools to a fight that had, at its burning center, nothing to do with him at all and everything to do with a woman twenty years dead who had, in a palace hallway, needed no one's help.
