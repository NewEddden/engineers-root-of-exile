Chapter 16 - The Foundry Rises

The old foundry had been the town's smithy for as long as anyone alive could remember, and moving it out to the river cost Kaldo its smith for the better part of a month.

Emeric had accounted for the machines. He had not accounted for the silence. Bram's forge in town went cold, the anvil crated, the hearth swept out, and for twenty-six days a man who wanted a hinge repaired or a share re-pointed had to wait, or ride, or do without. Emeric noted the delay as a line in a schedule. He did not, at the time, note the way it sat with people.

The new foundry rose fast. Eagerness, need, and the plain efficiency of building where the water already ran carried it up in a season that would have taken the old crews a year. It stood two stories of coursed stone under a steep, many-planed roof, and out of that roof climbed three tall round chimneys, each one married to a furnace below.

Three. Bram had argued for one.

"We need one now," Bram had said, standing in the mud where the footings were still wet. "One good furnace runs the whole town's iron and then some."

"We need one now," Emeric agreed. "We need three by the time the fields come in; the barracks want armor, and every farm inside a day's ride wants a cast plow-head instead of a forged one. Building the shell for three costs a fraction more than building the shell for one. Adding a furnace to a foundry that was framed for it is a week. Adding a furnace to a foundry that was not is another season and another roof."

Bram had looked at the three cold hearths for a long moment. "You're building for a town we don't have yet."

"I'm building for the town we're about to be."

It was, Emeric thought, unarguable. He was right about the stone, right about the framing, right about the week versus the season. He was so completely right about the building that it did not occur to him to ask what it would take to feed three furnaces once they were lit.

The wheel went in against the wall nearest the river, open-spoked and mounted vertically, taking the current straight off the race they'd cut. It was ordinary work. Any millwright in the empire could have set it, and that was the point. The river turned the wheel whether or not a single crystal in the barony held charge, and if the season ran dry Emeric had bedded a small water stone at the intake to lean on the flow when it thinned, a lift under the baseline rather than the baseline itself. Lose the stone and the foundry slowed. It did not stop.

From the wheel, a line ran up to the roof ridge where a timber crane frame carried a hoist. The same rotation that drove the works below could be thrown onto the hoist drum to haul a loaded skip up to the second floor, where the charging doors opened onto the tops of the furnaces. That was the reason for the height, and the reason for the crane, and Emeric was proud of both.

The furnaces themselves were the part he could not have bought or borrowed anywhere in Kaldo.

A blast furnace is not a bigger forge. Emeric had explained it to Bram twice and drawn it three times before the shape of the idea took. Each furnace was a tall stone shaft, nearly twice a man's height inside, narrowing and then widening again in a profile Bram had called wrong on sight and beautiful once it ran. You charged it from the top, in courses: a measure of ore, a measure of charcoal, a measure of crushed limestone, then ore again, the whole column stacked and descending as it burned. Air went in at the bottom through clay- lined nozzles, the blast driven hard and steady off the wheel's bellows instead of a boy's arms, and that steady blast was the whole trick. The rising heat cooked the descending charge on its way down. By the time the ore reached the bottom, it was not softened. It was liquid.

That was the line Bram had not believed. Every furnace he had ever worked gave up a bloom, a hot spongy mass of iron and slag you hauled out and beat the slag from by hand, hour after hour. Emeric's furnace never made a bloom. It made a pool, iron running molten at the base of the stack, slag floating clear on top of it, both drawn off separately through their own tap holes. Iron you could pour. Iron you could cast straight into a mould.

The heat to hold that took more than charcoal. Set into the hearth of each furnace was a fire stone, industrial-cut, tuned to give a long, even burn rather than a burst, and it did the one thing charcoal alone could never do reliably in Kaldo: it held the bottom of the stack at the temperature where iron stayed liquid long enough to work. A carved reader watched the heat and fed it back up the control tube so the stone answered the furnace instead of a guess. Push it, ease it, hold the curve. Emeric had built the whole governor around a single rule he'd learned the expensive way months ago at the threshing floor: never trust a fixed number when you can read the real one.

He was careful, too, to build it so the magic could fail without the work failing with it. Drain the fire stone, or drop the foundry into a dead patch, and the furnace did not die. It fell back to what a very good charcoal furnace could do on its own, slower, cooler, coarser iron, more of it wasted. Worse. Not nothing. The mundane furnace underneath the mana one still stood, and Emeric had insisted on that the way he insisted on everything now, because the barony could not afford a heart that stopped the moment a crystal did.

Ward walked the finished shell once and said only that he did not like how much of Kaldo's worth now sat under one roof by the water, out past the wall. He'd had the corners built up with a parapet and merlons, more keep than workshop, and had put it about that the foundry was watched at night. Emeric had let him. It cost stone, and it cost nothing he cared about, and Ward was rarely wrong about the things Emeric was blind to.

They lit the first furnace on a grey morning with half the town finding a reason to be near the river.

The charge went up the hoist and down the throat. The blast came on. For an hour the stack roared and did nothing anyone could see, and Bram stood with his arms crossed and his jaw set in the particular way that meant he had bet against something and was waiting to be proved right.

Then the trouble Emeric had not designed out arrived.

They needed to charge the second course, and charging meant throwing the wheel's power onto the hoist to lift the next skip. The instant the hoist drum took the load, the wheel slowed. And the bellows ran off the same wheel. The blast sagged, right as the furnace was hungriest, and the reader on the control tube showed the bottom of the stack shedding heat in a slow, wrong slide.

Emeric saw it in the numbers before he saw it anywhere else. One wheel. Two mouths. Hoist a heavy load, and you starve the blast to feed the crane, and you starve it at the worst possible moment, because you only hoist when the furnace is ready to eat again.

"Hold the skip," he said. "Don't hoist while she's drawing hard. We charge between blasts, not across them."

It worked, in the sense that it stopped the immediate ruin. It also meant the whole rhythm of the furnace was now hostage to a man watching a dial and calling when the crane was allowed to move, and it meant the throughput Emeric had built three furnaces to reach was, for now, impossible, because you could not run the hoist and the blast at full at the same time off one wheel. The real fix was a heavy wheel on the drive to carry the works through the dips, a flywheel to buffer the load. And a flywheel that size had to be cast. In the foundry. That was not yet reliably cast. He wrote it down. He deferred it. He was getting very good at deferring things.

They lost part of that first heat to the sag, a skimming of scrapped iron and a lining that would need patching sooner than it should. Bram cursed and skimmed and re-charged and did not say I told you so, which from Bram was its own verdict.

And then, past noon, they tapped it.

The clay plug came out of the bottom, and the iron came with it, running white-gold down the sand channel into the moulds, into the plow-head forms lined up waiting, and the whole crowd made a single sound Emeric felt in his teeth. Bram crouched at the channel with the light of it on his face and watched his three generations of bloom-and-beat metallurgy become a thing that simply poured, and said nothing at all for a long time.

It was, by every measure Emeric owned, a win. The furnace ran. The iron poured. The moulds filled. He had brought a piece of a world eight hundred years ahead down onto a riverbank in Kaldo, and it had worked on the first real day.

He let himself have it for most of an afternoon.

Saban found him at dusk, and Saban did not come out to the river for good news.

"The seam's thinning," the merchant said, without preamble, because he'd learned Emeric preferred it that way. "Old workings. What's left near the surface is near gone. There's more ore in that hill, but it's deep, and it's poor, and getting at it means more men, more timber, more time, for worse rock." He nodded at the furnaces, at the three chimneys still breathing heat into the dark. "You built three mouths. I can barely feed one of them past the stockpile, and the stockpile's a month, maybe less at the rate that thing eats."

"There's ore in Duvrenne," Emeric said. It wasn't a question.

"There's ore in Duvrenne, and in half the baronies between here and it, and I can have it moving up the road inside a fortnight." Saban let that sit, because he was honest, and honesty meant not letting a lord hear only the half he wanted. "At Duvrenne prices. Plus the carting. Plus every hand that touches it on the way. I can find you all the ore you can burn, my lord. I can't find it cheap, and I can't find it free, and you already know which of those two we haven't got."

So it came down to the same place the mine did. The same place the furnaces did. Not a supply problem he could out-think with a taller stack or a cleverer blast. A money problem wearing a supply problem's coat.

Ferra was waiting for him back at the hall with the ledgers open, and she did not soften it either.

"We're out," she said. "Not low. Out. The relocation, the furnaces, the fire stones, the seed and the tools and the last of the field works, it all went out this season, and none of it has come back yet. Payroll's due at the turn of the month. The colliers want paying for the charcoal. Saban's carters will want paying the day the ore leaves Duvrenne, not the day it arrives. And there is nothing behind any of it." She turned a page she did not need to turn. "The fields are the only thing that pays in. And the fields aren't in."

There it was, all of it, folded down into a single number that wasn't there. The foundry stood. The furnaces ran. The fields were sown and worked and wanting nothing but weather and weeks. Every coin that had bought that readiness was spent, and the only thing that could put a single coin back was a harvest that had not happened yet, from a system he had built and scaled and never once tested at the size the whole barony was now balanced on.

"If the harvest comes in," Ferra said, "we're fine. Better than fine. If it comes short, we can't make payroll, we can't buy Saban's ore, and the furnaces you just lit go cold with nothing to feed them."

"And if it fails," Emeric said.

Ferra didn't answer that one. She didn't have to.

He found Bram still at the foundry that night, sitting on an upturned crate by the tap channel where the last iron had long since gone hard in the moulds. Emeric sat down beside him, expecting to share the win. The furnace had run. Surely Bram was where he was.

"Good first day," Emeric said.

Bram was quiet for a while. When he spoke, he didn't look up from the cold iron.

"It's the best thing I've ever put my hands to, and I'll say that to anyone." He turned a cold plow-head over with the toe of his boot. "But you asked me once why I fought you on the third furnace. It's because half this town watched you close my shop for a month and pour everything we have into three fires, and when that iron ran today they didn't cheer. They counted. They've got the same seed in the same ground we do, and they know to the bushel what happens to Kaldo if it comes up short." He looked over. "You read that furnace to the degree, my lord. You didn't read one face in that crowd."

And he hadn't. He had watched the blast sag and named the cause in a single breath, and he had watched a whole town all season and called their fear excitement, because excitement was what he would have felt, and he was not standing where they stood. The sound they had made when the iron poured was not triumph. It was the sound of people who had staked everything they owned and just watched the wheel begin to turn, not yet knowing where it would stop.

That was the shape of it, laid flat. The barony had spent its last on him, and he had turned that last into stone and fire and sown fields, into everything a place needed to stop being a plague-scar on the map and start being something more. And none of it, not one course of stone, would keep a single family fed through the winter if the ground came up short. He could out-build the old world. He could not out-build a bad harvest, and he could not buy his way through the wait, because there was nothing left to buy it with. For once Emeric had made a thing that worked exactly as designed, and it had left him nothing to do but sit by the cold iron and count the weeks, and hope the fields were kinder than the ledger.
