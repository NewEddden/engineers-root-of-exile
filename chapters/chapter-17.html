Book 2, Batch 17: The Reckoning

Chapters 117–129 | Monday 11 January – Saturday 13 March, Year 18

The fleet is home. The crews have had one night with their families under Ward's oath, every diary is under Ferra's wax, and six guests wait aboard the K Discovery at the castle quay: Neris of the Rim, Runa, Duncan Hale of the Westgone, Gisela of the Sabir, Grau the werewolf physician and Siv of the Murr. Seventeen Church prisoners sit in the castle cells. Maeve carries lightning scars and a storm dragon nobody knew about. Emeric owes his brother the truth about the leash he set on him in the winter, and his mother the thought he could not say on an open line. The Church's pulpits already know the fleet is back.

This is the batch where Kaldo is told and has to live with it. The king tells his city what its own ancestors did first, then that the other peoples are alive, and brings the guests up the steps by name. A stadium hears the dead read, including twenty-eight whose names nobody has, and then hears a band and a singer for the first time. Emeric confesses to Corbin's face. The town makes a long joke of a ten-year-old with a tail. And the reckoning comes in the ordinary way a commonweal does it: courts, a ledger read aloud, a vote the king loses, a riot outside the chamber, a censure, a law that takes every private gun.

Underneath it the Church's hand keeps working. A sealed order is read with a scripture key Kaldo printed, a witness is poisoned in the king's own cells, and Corbin sits up all night on an ordinary landing to keep the next one alive. A movement that calls itself Avarran's Heirs nails up its first pamphlet. The batch ends in a museum room that opens with no speeches, and the first thing on its wall is what the humans' own side did.

Chapter 117: The King's Word

At dawn on Monday 11 January, in the records hall's back room, Harrold takes the regent's seal off its chain and puts it on the table between them and makes no speech. The rest, he says, is in the book: the Shell, the stadium, the prisons, the exception for the Isle's oil, the oil reserve burned, Declan. Saban notes that Emeric goes to the exception's page first and says nothing about it yet. Harrold says he will answer for it at the Assembly.

In the forenoon, in the castle's court room, the magistrate Keller holds the remand hearing. The seventeen are brought up from the cells in cuffs, the charge is read, attacking the crown's fleet in Randillia's sky, their names are taken, and counsel is assigned at the crown's cost. Keller asks the officer his name. He does not give it, and Keller writes "declined" and moves on. Counsel for the seventeen asks what became of their crewmates who went into the sea after the princess was already free. Keller asks the crown for a written account of the beams under the king's own name, the way the king's knights write theirs. Saban watches Emeric hear it and not argue. That night Emeric writes the whole of it, what he told Ferra on the K Discovery, not sorry, and why, and signs it, and Saban carries it to Keller's clerk himself before dawn. Keller reserves, in open court, the question whether a court of the crown can judge the crown's own act in its own sky. The casters go to Stillwater on remand, the only place built to hold a mage long without draining him, and the deckhands stay in the castle cells. In Saban's head the reasons for telling the city now are plain: the guests cannot be hidden; eight hundred and thirty families have had their sons and daughters home for a night under an oath that was never written for this; the pulpits have preached necromancy since June and the trap since the week before; and Harrold told a lord in the capital to ask the king when he came home. Saban's head start ran out on 4 January.

About eleven, Emeric stands on the records hall steps over Lantern Square, the square full and the trolleys stopped. He tells it in order. First, what their own side did: the war, the Thinning, the Leaving Terms signed at swordpoint, and the humans' own sons given to the rite by the thousand, as the Sea Hold's ledgers record. He does not soften it and does not make it a sermon. Then that the others are alive: the merfolk of Aumere, the orcs of the Sun Shore, the dwarves of the Sea Hold and the Murr under them, the Sabir and the dry edge, the winged ones, the dragons. He has been to them, by their leave, on their terms. Then the guests walk up the steps and stand beside him, and he says their names. Runa comes first, her hand in Grau's, her tail out of her coat because she would not hide it, and he says plainly what she is: a werewolf's daughter and a human repairer's, from past the wall, under the crown's care. Then Neris of the Rim, Gisela of the Sabir court, Siv, Grau, and Duncan Hale of the Westgone, humans who left with the old peoples and never came back. Every guest has a contract, a wage, a house, a passage home whenever they ask, and the right to say what they think of Kaldo in public without losing any of it. Then: "You'll see all of it." The diaries go back this afternoon, and the room opens in March. In his head, in one line, is what he does not say: delving, the Forges, how the net works, Earth, the makers, the Cradle, the weapons.

Through Runa's ears, not everyone in the square is glad. A woman pulls her boy back from her. A carter spits. Someone at the back says "beasts," loud enough to be heard and not loud enough to be found. Someone starts clapping and stops because nobody joins, and then a lot of people join. Lord Cassel, in the square on the regency's business, writes something down. Doran, on the lower step in his cardinal's red, does not make the sign of the Eye, and Runa notices him not making it.

That afternoon, in the records hall's long room, Ferra's clerks break the wax box by box and hand every book back against the register. Arthur's tally book comes first, because his was the first issued. Mira's page for the day of the trap holds the date and nothing else, and it goes home with her. Kaldo News prints the Word whole the next morning, with the guests' names spelled as they gave them. By evening every voyager's family is reading at the kitchen table, and the lanes have the peoples by supper.

Chapter 118: Dry Thunder

That night the city fills the Grand's central stadium, Tess's short name for the complex, which Kaldo takes up. Through Tess's eyes, who knows every bolt of it: the bowl's lower tiers sit on earth banks and the upper ones on printed dustcrete and steel; the first ribs of the facade stand against the dark; there is no roof yet; the five wings round it are foundations and a first shell. The stage comes up out of its base chamber for the first time in public on the mundane screw lift, the stones in it doing nothing, on purpose. Braziers burn along the tiers. The city comes in tens of thousands, the guests in the arena with everyone else, Runa on Grau's shoulders, and Harrold, Amara in her golem body under a hood, and Corbin in the stand like anyone.

The dead are read first. Doran reads them, because the death book has been his for forty-odd years: Sir Declan, of the home knights, on the ridge above the river in June; Bryony Fenwick, fourteen, of Cinderhale, and the two who died with her. Then, after a breath long enough for people to wonder whether he has finished: "And twenty-eight from the Church's ships in January, whose names we don't have." Nobody asked him to; it is his own choice as a priest. Some of the crowd mutters. Most of it does not. Emeric, in the stand, closes his eyes for a moment, and nobody but Ferra sees.

The all-ages school's orchestra, Fisk's pupils, plays the first piece from the lip of the stage, and the lower tiers are full of their parents. Then Dry Thunder plays, the first time Kaldo has heard the band: Arthur's guitar, Mira's bass, Bren on the cask, Finn's concertina and Zale's squeezebox, Zale staying a week before the pair goes back to Aumere. Then Wren sings. Nobody knew she could. The set list and the words had gone home by picture wire in Ferra's hand, Emeric heard her singing one of the songs behind Tess's voice on a call day and asked her, she said no and then yes, and she rehearsed with the band on Sunday afternoon, the day they landed. The city finds her voice the same night it learns the others live. Rylan, her father, in the stand, does not know what to do with his hands.

Through Maeve's eyes, she sits in the stand beside her father with her scars under a high collar and watches Mira the whole set, and Mira plays to one place in the stand. Everyone round them can see it. Harrold can see it, and Zale on the stage. Ferra and Imani trade a look over Emeric's head. Emeric is watching Bren on the cask with an expression Bren will later describe to Arthur as like he had stolen a horse.

After the concert, on the players' stair under the stage, Maeve finds him, because she knows he will come down to thank the band, and says she wants to tell him something before he works it out wrong. He says he has already worked it out. He has the glare ready and means to have a word with Bren. It is not Bren. Maeve says Mira's name, and Mira comes down the stair behind her with her bass on her back at exactly the wrong moment, sees his face, and stops on the step. He says he had planned all voyage to be the overprotective brother and glare at the man, and it is a woman, and one of his favourite knights, which he will never tell Mira and says in front of her anyway. "Never tell her I said that." Mira, on the step: "Too late." Zale, coming down with the squeezebox, says he has known since the relief box, and Emeric takes it personally, out loud, for about a minute. He tells Mira that keeping her scar is metal. Maeve asks what metal means. He cannot explain it, so he says it means good, and Mira says she will take it. The next morning Bren, told by Arthur, asks for an apology for four months of being glared at. He gets one, badly.

Past midnight, in the castle, Emeric, Ferra and Imani have their own bed for the first time in nine months, after a year of bunks and one orc bed, a door that locks, and the window open on the bay because the room has been shut since April. The night is theirs, and it is a honeymoon. Afterward Ferra, face in the pillow, asks what metal means. He starts to explain, and Imani tells her it means he likes the scar, and to go to sleep.

Chapter 119: To His Face

On Tuesday 12 January Corbin is sent for and finds Harrold standing by the window of the small room off the long room, Amara in her golem body by the door, and the king at the table. Nobody tells him to sit. He sits. Ferra and Imani know it is today and are not in the room. Emeric starts with December: the check, the drain stone, the piece he set in Corbin's stone. He rambles, an engineer explaining a failure, the conditions, the burn-to-dust lock, why a piece in a kept stone could be read. He starts to justify it, Ward's view, the chair, a thousand miles, and stops himself mid-sentence because he can hear it.

Corbin had guessed. He knows what the walking feels like from inside, so he felt something set near him at the December check and guessed the rest, and in March, when the stone went to dust under the king's palm, he knew it had been there. He says his side of the cost once, plainly, not as an accusation: three months in the chair with his death on his ankle while his brother was in the next room, every hard call that winter made wondering whether that was the one; then nine months of the voyage without it and without knowing for sure. That is how he knows it was right to do to him. With the shoe on the other foot, back then, he would have done the same and worse; he would likely have killed him. Then he gives his own list, what he did, not how he feels: the yard; Augustine; what he let be done in his name; the men he did not stop. A boy in a man's body, and Kaldo taught him the rest by making him work for it.

Emeric asks him to keep Kaldo's chair under the Assembly, now that the king has a kingdom and a capital's seat to answer for. Corbin says he would be a terrible king and the job is miserable, and he will do it as penance and because someone has to. Then, mid-sentence, it dawns on him that he does not want the power either, and he laughs, which nobody in the room has ever heard him do. He says it half laughing: a family who do not want what they were born into. Their father would rather have been her husband, Zale has a boat, and Emeric never wanted the crown. Harrold, by the window, keeps his promise and says nothing. Amara does not either. Emeric gets up and hugs him. Corbin freezes, because they have never done that, then eases into it, and Emeric teases him about how bad he is at it. In the hug, or just after, Corbin tells him one memory: the stair outside Amara's tower room, and a small boy on the bottom step waiting for his brother to come down. Emeric does not say he had lost it. Amara, at the door, knows he had, and does not say either.

That evening at the family table Corbin learns that Harrold has known since March and kept the promise, and Amara too. He disagrees with all of them about what it obliged them to do: they think it obliged them to tell him to his face; he thinks it obliged them to tell him the day it burned, before the voyage, so he could run Kaldo knowing instead of guessing. He says it flat, once. Harrold says he is right. Emeric says he is right. Amara says he is not: some things a man has to carry until he can hand them over properly, and that is the promise she asked for. Nobody wins. They eat. Corbin rings Rowena that night himself. Rowena had already told Zale by set, the night they landed, that the queen is alive in a golem's body; that was hers to decide. Zale goes to the dome on Tuesday, does not know what to say to a woman he was told was dead and has known for one night to be his brother's mother made whole, and talks about Ilenne, and Amara lets him. Harrold pays an old debt the same week: he writes to Rowena properly, and she answers by the next post.

That night, in the dome where Amara's body lies, Emeric finally says what he could not say on an open line. Her golem body is the proof that a mind can outlast a body, and since the night after Saldis's cone he has been thinking about Ferra and Imani, and about how long he may live. He says it badly. Amara answers as the only person alive who has done it. It is not a body, it is a room you live in, and it does not taste bread. She would rather he spent the next hundred years making sure the two of them do not need it than the next ten building it for them. And, being his mother, she tells him to tell them he asked her. He says he will.

Chapter 120: Is It His?

From Wednesday 13 to Sunday 17 January, through Runa's ears, the town works her out. The fishwives on the quay decide the king went off for nine months and came home with a girl of ten, so either the sea ages them fast out there or he has been busy longer than he let on. The carters say whichever of the two queens she belongs to has some explaining to do, and so does he. At Maren's eating house they ask whether she looks like him, round the eyes maybe, and Maren serves Runa a second plate and says nothing. At the all-ages school on her first morning, her tail trimmed for school, the children ask why she has a tail and whether they can touch it. No. The older town still says "his highness" from the prince years, on purpose. Ferra and Imani hear the "which queen" version at a stall and answer it differently: Ferra counts the months out loud and leaves the sum hanging, and Imani explains as a doctor exactly how long a pregnancy is, at length, until the stallholder begs her to stop. Emeric, asked by a stallholder whether she is his, says, "Yes, now," which is the wrong answer to the question being asked, and that joke runs longest. By the third day Runa has worked out what "is it his" means, decides it is the funniest thing in the city, and for a week calls him "father" at the top of her voice across the market to watch the stallholders' faces fall. Both of them know it is a joke. Kaldo News's letters page the next Tuesday has a reader asking in all seriousness whether people grow up quicker past the wall, and the editor prints Imani's one-line answer.

He builds her boots and will not let anyone else adjust them, moves her mattress to the corner of the castle's south room she picked, and puts a honey jar in with her hoard. He walks her to school until she makes him stop a block short, hides behind a delivery cart, and she says "I can smell you" loud enough for the whole street. When a pot lid falls in the castle kitchen she drops and covers her ears. Her sessions go on at home: the circle, the circle alone, control drills. The squirrel September, in the old pear by the castle gate, will not come to the king for a week, because he has been gone nine months. Ferra says it is sulking and enters it in her book. Runa does not see the point of a squirrel until she smells it: it smells like him. On the Saturday he stops trying, and it comes down on its own, straight past him, and sits on Mochi on the kitchen step. Runa sleeps on Mochi, so from then on it is war over who gets the warm slime, and September usually wins by getting there first. That evening he gives it a place from the castle roof and it goes. Nine months and an ocean have not undone the bond.

On Thursday at the knights' base Emeric goes to see the juggernaut Harrold has kept alive for seven months, and asks Amara to come, because of the stone in the dead man's Gilded Eye. Is there a viewer in this one? Amara reads it again. The Eye on its plate is gold with nothing behind it; the Church sent it blind, because it was never meant to come back. Emeric does not touch it. He agrees with Harrold that it did what it was made to do and could not refuse, and is not a prisoner of anything but its making. It moves to Stillwater's secure hospital ward as Imani's patient, and the man who sat with it asks to go too. At the Motion Shop, Tess tells him what she reached while he was away before he can ask: a bench transistor that has held its gain for a year, an oxide skin on a slice, measured, and one clean room serving the long fibre; not the printed pattern or the masks, because the raid and the Shell ate her autumn.

On Wednesday Duncan asks Ferra to introduce him to the castle steward Hale properly, the Westgone way, because kin are introduced, not met. Hale, thin and formal, thirty-eight years in the house, says of course and looks at the candle. Duncan names his line back to the War of the Groves and says some of his family stayed in the old land. Hale says his grandfather kept a family book that he has never read past the second page, because nobody ever asked. Nobody can prove they are kin, both of them say so, and neither minds. Duncan asks one thing: will the steward stand witness for him the day he presses, the way a warden's kin witness his oath at home? Hale has waited eleven years and more to be asked anything. He says yes.

On the first clear night, Friday, Ferra and Imani sit in blankets beside the glass on the castle roof in the cold while Emeric answers a question he asked under the stars long ago and could not answer without a telescope: same rules is not the same sky, and the far things do not move. He finds them one at a time: a smudge that is not a star and does not twinkle, a dark split down the length of the band, and two small clouds low over the sea to the south. They are there. He is home, only very far from it. He lets each of them put an eye to the glass, Imani last, and she says something about how small it is, and she is not talking about the cloud. Nobody says anything large.

Chapter 121: Why Now

On Saturday, in his harbour office, Saban makes his changes. No public notice will give a date for anything the king or the fleet does again, because the concert's bill said the homecoming, the tenth; notices go up the morning of. More lighthouses: a heartbeat is a lighthouse, so light more of them. He asks Tess for decoy beacons, beat-only boxes with no decider and nothing to open, hung on the chain between the drums and beating in their pattern, so a listener cannot tell which beat is a door. The crews call them the dummies, and Tess has the first three on her bench by February from the old third drum's shell, at Ferra's cost of three boxes and a ride-up each. No position goes out in clear on any set again, gift sets included; Ferra's card gains a line saying so, and the twelve in the locker are re-carded. Tess asks why nobody thought of it before, and Saban says somebody did: Ferra, on the card, in July. And the radio is a door too, so Tess builds band inversion into the knights' net and the relay voice lines, a switch on every set that flips the voice's tones top to bottom. Anyone with a matching set and patience can turn it back, so it hides talk from a casual listener, not from someone holding one of Kaldo's own sets. Saban says then the sets are the secret. Emeric adds the lock: from now on every stone Kaldo writes carries the keel, the lock and the tamper response, so a wrong note shuts it rather than steers it. You cannot steer it. You can break it. So nobody gets to hold it alone. On Sunday, under the Kaldo News board, someone nails up the first pamphlet of Avarran's Heirs.

On Sunday evening, Zale's last night before the pair goes back to Aumere, the family sits at the castle table: Harrold, Amara, Corbin, Zale, Maeve in her high collar, Emeric, Ferra, Imani and Runa eating, with Waylon on the works' set from the Isle, a voice from the sideboard with a half-second's lag. Ferra has brought the sheet because Zale asked to see it: five rows, Emeric fire, Zale sea, Harrold light, Corbin stasis, Maeve storm, and Waylon's blank, with her line from the Rim beneath, nature picks the kind and need picks the moment. Harrold asks what nobody has: not which, but why now. The line has had gold eyes for two thousand years, and there is not one pair of wings in the portrait gallery. Emeric says the blood must be in it, since everyone who changed is of the line, but it cannot be all of it, or someone would have done this centuries ago. Waylon gets there over the set: every one of them ate a bonded stone in the last two years. A pause on the line. "Then I'm next." Corbin says you do not get to be next by asking. Waylon says he did not ask, he counted. Ferra ate one too, as did the six and the three knights, and she has looked: no scales, so the stone alone is not enough; Imani looks at her own forearm anyway, and Ferra sees her do it. Amara says the towers' register has no mage in four hundred years who grew a wing, so density alone is not enough either. Emeric adds the second lock: he walked a year after his stone with nothing showing, and every change since has come in thick air and a crisis. Ferra rules two new columns while he talks, the stone and the air. Harrold says no king of the line was ever given a bonded stone; the Church held the only tree, and bonding takes delving. The table goes quiet, and nobody says why. Zale asks whether that means he does not actually like the sea; Emeric says or he does, that is the other column; Amara says she married into this; Runa, asked by nobody, says her tail is not from a stone and goes back to her plate. Then Amara asks the one nobody wants: if it is blood and the stone, every child of the line carries it, and whoever gives one of them a bonded stone is choosing whether to wake a dragon. Zale, whose children will be half merfolk, puts his fork down. Nobody answers.

Then Ferra rules a third new column, the door, and makes each of them say which form came first and what is hard. Emeric has pieces, then the partial form, up a ladder he built himself, but the whole has not come. Harrold has the whole; coming back takes him till dawn, and the partial form will not come. Zale has anything in salt water and on land is sure he can't, though Emeric says it isn't the sea. Corbin has the partial form, easy in and out, and the whole nowhere near. Maeve is strong in weather and thin without it. Runa, asked by nobody, with her mouth full: wolves teach the pups, so who is teaching them? Emeric says the only people who would know live on a volcano, and one of them wanted him dead. Ferra says one of them has a set.

Chapter 122: Witness

From Monday 18 January the voyage's class reconvenes in the gravity hall at the knights' base, and now, in Kaldo's dosed air, the press with intake begins. Imani reads one room at a time and can stop anyone, Bettany at her elbow learning, Ferra keeping the register. Before the first press Imani prints the warnings, the signs of deviation and the dosing rule, on one sheet, and Kaldo News prints it the same week; the method stays in the class until there are readers to teach it, and the first relay sheets on the core go only to the knights' base and the hospital's wards. The padding knights press first, Arthur, Mira, Bren, the Crestless and the rebuilt knights, because they have had stasis without its name for years. Emeric presses at home for the first time since Aumere, toward a working core in weeks. Maeve, who drilled stasis all winter, is still behind the knights and furious about it. Ward comes, presses badly, and asks what it costs. Runa, ten, does not press; her sessions go on in the side room. That week Zale and the pair go home to Aumere low on their veins and on the water, Abloh's sea chest emptied into the museum's store and refilled with Kaldo cloth for Ilenne, and Ilenne feels him coming before he is past the Smoking Rings.

On Saturday 23 January, after three days of presses, Duncan's seed holds, small, about as much as he held when he started. Hale stands by the gravity hall's wall in his good coat for an hour without moving and understands none of it. When it is done Duncan tells him, and Hale says "Good," and then, because it is the first time in thirty years anyone has asked him to stand for anything, he asks Duncan to dinner. Duncan's log, with the rage written in it, has to wait for the First City trip, since there is no courier door in Kaldo and no hull going past the wall. He tells Emeric so and asks whether he wants to read it before it goes. Emeric says no; the terms were unopened. Duncan writes one more line in front of him: what Kaldo did with its prisoners afterwards, a magistrate and counsel, not killed. An honest log has the after in it too.

From Monday 25 January a man named Gethin comes in from Cinderhale with a stump, sent by a duty clinic's note from September, to the hospital's sterile ward. Imani assesses tissue, nerves, circulation and joint, and regrows the two fingers over repeated sessions, fed and watched through the hard stages with a sterile ward between, and then physiotherapy. "That's not a forge injury." "That's what happened." "Then whatever you call a forge in Cinderhale, stop putting your hand inside it." When he asks whether she will refuse him next time, she says that if he brings her the same injury again she will mend it again, and she would prefer that he stop giving her the work. The bill is two coppers on the sliding scale, and the public subsidy he never asks about pays the rest. She tells the king nothing. Liora, Imani's student in the sterile ward, tells Gethin in Kaldo to use both hands until the grip is his: her first clean sentence in any language, nothing through the nose. Imani says nothing to anyone, and it goes in her diary under Gethin's line.

On Monday 18 January Ferra goes to Abloh's shop about the fleet's dress coats and finds the castle laundress's basket on his cutting table. The line has been home a week and the basket is all backs: Emeric's, Corbin's, Maeve's, and two of Harrold's from before he learned to undress first. Abloh, eight days off Zale's ship, has laid them side by side, and every one is torn from the same two points along the same two lines. A coat has a vent so a man can sit a horse, he says, and nobody ever cut one for wings because nobody had a customer. He chalks two lines down a shirt back and laps them. Ferra reads him the count from her book, twenty-three shirts and four coats since June for the king alone, and says the vent is cheaper. Emeric, sent for, adds the closure: thin strips of magnet steel in both edges of the lap, because a button would hold against the wing and tear, and a dead zone would kill anything cast. Magnets don't care. At the first fitting, on Thursday 28 January, the wing comes out through the lap and goes back, and the lap shuts by itself with a small click. Then Abloh says all of it. The half form stands up in the shop, over seven feet, and the shirt goes at both side seams, the trousers at the seat where the tail comes, and both boots split. Abloh fetches a stool, measures him at height, and tells him to come back in a month.

On Monday 1 February Ferra makes the first entry under Kaldo's own sky: the Well's January draw, by weight. Under it goes a second line she had not planned. The class's intake comes out of the same dosed air, so cores pressed in the city are the Band's too, and she enters the class's share as an estimate. No dragons' observer has come to read the column, and she keeps it anyway. In the first week of February the hospital prints Imani's blood groups, and Kaldo News prints them too: the same groups in every people aboard, Grau, Runa, Neris, Siv and Gisela beside Kaldo's own, and a human can take a werewolf's blood. Nobody on the page knows why. When the Heirs' pamphlet went up, Emeric named the pattern privately to Ferra and Imani and then to the six at the long table: a real grief, a story about blood laid over it, a scapegoat, a purity that never existed, and fear of losing work. "I've read where this goes." He is not the only one answering it: Saban already has the printer's name, Imani has the blood sheet, and Ward wants to know who is paying. On Friday 5 February Gethin carries Corrick's third refusal of a royal summons to the castle door himself, because it is on his way, hands it over with the new hand, and paraphrases the doctor to the king's face: she told him to stop giving her work, and Corrick says the same about summonses. Emeric does not know what the doctor said or why the man is grinning, and Imani tells him nothing that night either. The same Friday, in the ward, Gethin's new fingers close round a hospital cup, and he waits a moment before he trusts his grip. Nobody tells him his hatred of the crown has been cured, because it hasn't.

In the line room that morning, after Ferra has read the month's debt to Sohra's set, Emeric takes the handset and asks her in orc, for his father, whether she would teach a man who came out whole how to come back, and how to be smaller. A long hiss on the line. She will ask her grandmother. Harrold, in the doorway, pretends he came for something else.

He keeps the handset and asks Sohra one thing more: how her people keep their clothes. She doesn't follow the question, and then she does. They don't put the scale down. That night he tries it a piece at a time, which only he can, scale from hip to knee and nothing else changed, and it holds. Ferra looks at it and says no. A king can't open a school in his own skin. The cut stays ordered.

On Sunday 31 January, through Runa's eyes, she asks Emeric in the Kaldo words she has whether he'll stop loving her if she still means to kill them. He says no. She asks whether they can fight now. Not until Imani says. She likes only one of the answers. In her first week, after a nightmare, he didn't ask about it; he salted a cut of meat at two in the morning and set it down a yard from her, the way he did at the shore camp.

On Monday 1 February, through Imani's eyes, the bite case closes and Imani clears Runa for the knights' yard on three conditions: short sessions, no blows to the head, and Imani's word stops any session without anyone's leave, the king's included. She says the third one in front of Runa so Runa knows it exists. The first Saturday there is no fighting. Emeric asks Runa to show him how she moves through ferns, and he's bad at it.

Chapter 123: The Order

From Monday 18 January, in her office, Ferra looks again at the officer's sealed order, whose length and seal have been in her book since the trap. At home she sees what she could not on a deck: the groups are in threes, and the first numbers never go higher than the number of books in the Book of the Light. It is a book cipher keyed to scripture. Doran, asked, brings the edition Kaldo printed, which he proofread line by line years ago, and the page numbers fall into place by Tuesday night, because the Church bought Kaldo's printing like everyone else and its officers carry it.

On Wednesday 20 January, in Keller's room, with Saban, Ferra, Ward, Arthur and the crown's counsel for the seventeen present, because it is evidence, the order is read. It is dated 28 December in Ostrel and addressed to the officer by rank, with no name. Watch the chain for the fleet's passage, expected by the notice in the square at Kaldo for the homecoming on the tenth. If the king's party comes within reach, take one of the line alive, the sister if she flies, as surety for the relay locks. Do not engage the king; hold him if he comes, and leave if he cannot be held. No man of ours is to be taken alive by Kaldo. Counsel reads line four twice and asks for a recess, because it is why the officer cast at his own men falling, and Arthur's account and Bren's burn are the rest of it. Line one is Kaldo's own error: Kaldo told the Church the week by billing a concert. Saban says so in the room, in front of Keller, before anyone else can. What the order does not prove is who in Ostrel wrote it. The seal is the Church's, and the hand is a clerk's. Corrick declines the first royal summons to the castle, politely, because he does not accept that a royal summons is simply a friendly invitation. Emeric takes offence and says so to Ferra.

On Thursday a deckhand among the seventeen, no caster, the man who fell past Bren's reach and was caught by the next knight, sends word through his counsel that he will tell Keller what the hulls were there for. Counsel asks for him to be moved to a witness's lodging, and it is approved for Monday. On Friday night he dies in his cell. On Saturday morning Imani does the death herself, because a patient is a patient and the dead too: poison, in the evening meal. The kitchen hand who carried the tray that night is gone, paid, the watch finds, by a voice in an alley and a purse, and out of the city on the Saturday carts before anyone looked. The pulpits preach the third drum as the dragon king burning three ships of the faithful out of the sky. Kaldo's answer is a trial in open court, which takes longer than a sermon.

At the long table on Saturday 23 January, a man has died in the king's cells, in a city that built two prisons on the rule that the punishment is losing your freedom and nothing gets added to it. Corbin says, "Give it to me." He holds Kaldo's chair, he knows what the Church's hand looks like when it works through people who do not know they are its hand, and if the king does it, it is the king guarding his own case. The king gives it to him without being asked twice. Then word comes through counsel that the deckhand Bren carried off the falling deck will speak now, because the first one was going to and look what happened, and because a knight carried him while his own officer was casting at him.

Chapter 124: The Reckoning

On Tuesday 26 January the Assembly sits, and Ferra reads the voyage's ledger whole as Chancellor: what came back, what was spent, what was promised. That includes the gift sets given at the stops, with twelve home; the bills paid abroad, the guano levy and bond, the Murr's terms, the Sabir Compact's cursed share, the nodule deal; and the promises, Ilenne's price, the grove's seed, the inspection agreement, Duncan's terms and the guests' contracts. She says aloud for the first time why the guests' housing and the First Room cost nothing while the museum's shop sells Neris's picture book at a price: necessities free, rarities priced. She reads her own wrong line into the record. At the Murr's stop her ledger counted the fans as a gift, and a Murr witness showed her they had paid for passage in maps. A member asks why she is confessing a fan, and she says because the next ledger will be read by people who were not there. She puts the column on the record: Kaldo's own sky, and why, since the offer under Saldis's truce sign said wherever we can and that has to mean home. A member asks what the dragons will do with it. Ferra says read it. The yr17 works are closed too: the Shell's eight spires and the stadium's bowl went up on the crown's works line under Harrold's seal, and the museum's own line is still this Assembly's to vote.

On Thursday 28 January, through Harrold's eyes, the regent answers for every paper he signed: the Shell, the stadium, the prisons, the juggernaut kept alive, the Velmoor contract, a nephew's exception and its revocation, and the exception for the Isle's works under the king's own clause. When the warm rock and a second beat were found, Tess asked for a pause, and he signed a limited exception because the relief run and the Answer Tower needed oil after the raid burned the reserve, and he wrote the date and the reason. He does not apologise. It was lawful, it was defensible, and he would sign it again on the same facts. A member asks whether the king would have. Emeric, from the crown's bench, says he wrote the clause so that someone could. That is all. Tess reports on the raid, what broke and what is mended. Imani reports on foreign contact, the health check of 9 January, a finding not a guarantee, the quarantine both ways, the seed hand's crates cleared from Milford's shed and planted in its glass houses, and her blood sheet. Saban reports who got the first foreign goods, the museum's store, Bram's bench and the Works Town, not the port merchants, who are disappointed and say so through a member.

On Tuesday 2 February the guests' contracts and rights are read into Kaldo's law as they were signed, including the right to criticise Kaldo in public without losing their places, with Neris in the gallery by that right and Duncan's terms the same, his letters unopened. Then comes the seat, the capital's formal move to Kaldo put to the Assembly as law. Lord Cassel answers for the court families, not stupidly: a capital that loses its seat sulks, and its families have a real grievance. Emeric's counter-offer is a learning city, a second Polytechnic in the Circle's yard with the Circle's mages teaching, the state house and archive in the old castle, and the ring tram, the spine line and the funicular ordered. It goes to committee. Bryda and Paxton work inside the Assembly's business, and the mill lord who objected to the king's face at the crowning votes against everything and is outvoted. Corrick declines the second summons. Lord Cassel says he will take the counter-offer home and argue it, which is more than anyone in the chamber expected.

Chapter 125: Unpaid

On Tuesday 9 February, the budget sitting, the lines are read one by one. The inspection agreement with the winged ones, the Nestwardens' mark on every guano lot Kaldo buys, is ratified, because it binds Kaldo's buying and costs nobody in the chamber anything. The museum's own line passes about thirty-one to eleven, since every member has a son or a neighbour in the fleet. The Sky Yard's engine bench and long shed pass, and a member asks what a shed that long is for. Bram, in the gallery, says engines. Sorrel, beside him, says nothing loudly. The clean room is funded as defence and telephone, smaller sets for the relay net first, with Vantic arguing the numbers as a witness and losing gracefully. An engineer from the Isle works presents his finding, warm rock, the beat faster, the shard cutter's warning filed in November as insufficient to halt, and asks for a proper survey of the hollow before the shaft goes deeper. It loses to the raid repairs and the terraces and is deferred to the next budget. Harrold does not speak. Tess does, and loses.

Then Emeric puts the winged ones' larger restoration fund himself: the minimum Kaldo paid abroad was the binding one, and the larger fund is what the bill was really for. It loses to the unfinished workers' terraces, about twenty-four to eighteen, because members with people in damp rooms in the Works Town vote for walls they can see. He loses. He says so aloud, accepts it, and does not pay it privately; he will bring it back next budget. Neris, in the gallery, by her signed right: "You'll paint their cliffs and not pay for them." Emeric agrees with her out loud from the crown's bench, and the clerks write it down.

That week, through Armand's eyes, the winged ones' letter is framed whole in Tobias Halloran's workroom, with its commentary attached and Armand's own correction in his register, marked UNPAID with their consent, because they wanted it read. Beside it stands a box, which Ferra counts every Friday, entering every coin by name. Halloran, about sixty, is a chapel painter from Randillia City found by Saban's network, who has stopped going to church because he says he has never seen one. He cross-examines voyagers over three diaries like a magistrate, asking how tall, against what, and where the sun was, and paints only what three agree on. Ottoline sits Neris first, in her own Rim dress, her colour painted to her correction. Ottoline and Tess finish the Motion Shop's card-reading loom, and its first cloth is Neris's bead pattern, the first picture the wire ever brought home. Tess and Wren's dome model leaks on the bench twice. The collection comes out of hull four's after hold into the museum's store with every tag copied out: seller, price, diary number. Liora checks the winged hall, since each people's hall is checked by one of them, and reads the commentary aloud in her own tongue for Halloran's caption, including the sentence that closes her sky. She is not in it, and says so. Armand says, "No. Nor should you be." From then on she is the hall's checker as well as Imani's student.

On Tuesday 16 February, through Ward's eyes, the Heirs petition the Assembly to bar the guests from citizen's cards. When their speaker quotes Avarran, Emeric says that Avarran married two elves, sisters, and that the pelt bounty was paid by a war cult he never signed for. A member asks how he knows. He says from the peoples' own records, the Sea Hold's and the orcs', and that the museum will show them. Grau, in the gallery, confirms the werewolves' half, and Emeric has to stop where the records stop. Outside in the civic ring's square a crowd of Heirs meets a crowd come to stand by the guests. Stones are thrown, a cart is overturned, a bin is set on fire. The home knights under Osric and the watch under Saban's word hold the chamber's doors, and the first baton rounds ever fired in Kaldo break the front of the crowd without killing anyone, though three people from the crowd go to Imani's ward with broken ribs and one with a cracked skull. The vote is held under guard. The petition fails, and the Assembly passes nothing that bars a people from the card. Some citizens see knights protecting the chamber; others see knights holding a door against their own city. Both halves are on Kaldo News's front page in the morning.

Chapter 126: The Quiet House

The poisoned tray came through the court's lodging account, so the leak lies somewhere between Keller's court and the castle kitchen. In the second week of February Corbin has Ferra write three lodging orders, each naming a different house, to the three offices that by law must know where a crown's witness sleeps: Keller's lodging clerk, the castle steward's office and the watch's duty room. Ferra keeps the list of which office got which house in her own hand and nowhere else. Hale's office gets a house too, and Hale, told why, says of course and does not look at the candle. The witness goes into the house told to the lodging clerk, because that is the office the tray came through, and Corbin bets on it. The witness is told so plainly and given the choice, and stays, because Bren carried him.

On the night of Saturday 13 February, in an ordinary house on a lane off the ring street, two up and two down, the witness lies in the back room upstairs. Corbin sits in a chair on the landing, two home knights wait in the kitchen, and Tess is in the attic with the night glass's heat layer on the lane and the backs. The stair is rigged with a pressure plate under the fourth tread, wired to a bulb in the attic and a bell in the kitchen, mundane, with no stone, because a stone can be felt. Hours pass with nothing: a cart, a dog, the dread of being looked at. At about two, Tess's glass shows one warm shape coming over the back wall. The fourth tread does not creak; the bulb lights. The man comes up the stair fast with a knife, and Corbin takes him on the landing by strength alone, with no cast, and does not kill him, though the man cuts him across the forearm and Corbin is angry enough to. Killing him would have killed the only lead. Imani heals the forearm by Tuesday.

On Sunday, through Ferra's eyes, the attacker knows very little: a voice, a purse, a chalk mark on a door to tell him which house. The house he came to was the one told to the lodging clerk. The clerk, taken quietly that afternoon, knows a little more: a man who paid for lodgings' addresses, a false name, and a church he was seen leaving that is not Doran's. The organizer is not proved. It goes into Keller's file and Saban's book as a lead, not a name. Emeric hears about it on Sunday morning from Ferra's list, after it is over, and does not say good until he has been told everything. Then he says it to Corbin, and Corbin says don't.

From Monday 15 to Friday 26 February the seventeen are tried in Keller's court, before a jury and three judges, with counsel at the crown's cost and no delving of any prisoner. The witness testifies on the Monday, and the pulpits' account is contradicted in open court by one of their own. Counsel asks the king, in his written deposition, whether he could have caught the hulls' crews and chose not to. He answers yes, in writing, under his name, and it goes into the record. The officer is found guilty of the attack and of the attempted killing of his own falling crew, and gets twenty-five years at Stillwater. The casters are guilty of the attack under orders and get eight years each. The deckhands are acquitted as men who sailed where they were told, and are offered passage home or a place at Turnstone's open house while they decide; two ask to stay. Some of Kaldo sees a brother who sat up all night on a landing and took a man alive. Others see armed knights guarding a politically useful witness the week before a vote on guns, and the Heirs' broadsheet says so.

Chapter 127: The Tea Caddy

On Saturday 20 February Corrick's written objection is read into the record of the arms law's public hearing: why should the king's own armed employees be trusted with what ordinary citizens may not own, and a law that gathers every gun into the crown's armouries makes a future king hard to remove. Corbin's printed answer appears in Kaldo News on Monday, because it is his Act and Corrick has refused every summons, so the answer is printed rather than private: the register kept with the district clerk, never with the armoury that issues the guns; the Assembly's line-by-line vote on every arms budget; and the circuit for any man refused or singled out. It does not say trust me, and it does not say the tags cannot be beaten. It says the safeguards are works in progress. At Cinderhale, Maudie pins both letters side by side on the League's wall, and Corrick's reply is one line in pencil underneath: none of it is safe while those bodies still answer to a crown. After reading all three refusals side by side, Emeric sees that each one declines the summons, not the talk, and tells Ferra he thinks the man would come if he were not sent for. Ferra says that is half of it, and does not give him the other half.

On Tuesday 23 February Corbin's Private Arms Act passes narrowly, about twenty-two to twenty, amended against him. It bans every private gun, short and long, copied or original, with no hunting exemption; previously lawful guns are registered and surrendered with a valued buyback and a right of appeal; command of every private watch passes at once from the lords to interim public reeves and magistrates; small towns' watches run under a temporary public licence for up to two years, armed from crown stock on tracked issue; the safeguards from his printed answer are written in; and deliberately and repeatedly financing a prohibited private command after notice carries up to thirty years. Corbin wanted it faster and harder and lost on both. A member says the vote is being held a week after knights guarded a witness, which is true, and it passes anyway. Ferra writes down that somebody will scam the buyback before anyone has.

In the same sitting the Assembly's committee reads its finding against the Chancellor's office: "I counted them as wages." Her model read the money houses' warden rolls and Dunholt's sales as employment and industry, and she defended those numbers on the council call in July. She is formally censured and loses her personal power to halt emergency spending for a year. The office's stay passes to Ilsa, the acting deputy who has sat at her desk since before the voyage, under the Assembly's emergency committee. The king's own minute is read too, the registration he ordered, the audit, the observers refused, the Crestless kept on the voyage. The Assembly cannot censure a king, and one member says so out loud, which goes into the record. That night Ferra is angry. Her model was valid and incomplete, she would rather have been wrong than incomplete, and she is not sorry for the numbers. Emeric says it was his decision on her numbers and he made it wrong. She says she knows. Imani makes them both eat.

On Wednesday Harrold, in the castle yard, says that a register is only as true as the man who walks past the names, and he will walk past them. On Monday 1 March he rides south with a clerk and two home knights to inspect the new registers in the south road's towns himself. The same Wednesday, Vantic comes into the king's study without an appointment, sets Bram's grandmother's tea caddy on Emeric's desk, the one painted with a shepherdess, and leaves. Nobody says anything. He opens it after she has gone. Inside is the hundred-mark note from the old mint's bad year, the paper that bought a loaf in the end, a promise from a mint with its books shut. He understands which instrument he was looking at, and that it was the wrong one.

Chapter 128: A Hand's Width

On the night of Tuesday 2 March someone chalks BEAST across Neris's door in the Port Town lodging and puts a stone through her window. She cleans the door herself before anyone comes, and keeps the stone. The watch has the man by Thursday: a journeyman carter of twenty-two, frightened that the Works Town will hire Sea Hold iron-workers. On Monday 8 March Keller's bench hears it as a crime against a person, threat and damage, not as a creed: a fine, the window paid for by his own hand, and bound over a year. Neris, by her right, speaks at the bench. She does not want him in a cell; she wants him to come and see the room on Saturday. He says no. At the bench's suggestion he is offered work at the Works Town beside the iron-workers he was afraid of. The first guns are handed in at Wayford's magistrate the same Monday.

On Wednesday 3 March, in the gravity hall, through Emeric's eyes, Armand's first core holds. He has drilled the inward stasis since the first morning in the Band, as slowly as every mage, and pressed since January. The seed is small. Imani reads him before and after, and his body's own figure has not moved. That evening, in his room in the keep, he has the brown calf book open, his record of every child he read for the tower at ten, and he has worked it out for himself: a core is not the body, so the total he once said everyone lands at is not a ceiling. Where you start decides how fast you go, not where you stop. He turns to the children who read below the tower's mark and were told they would be what they read for life, and he is delighted, out loud, in his dry way; his hands, which shook on an evening like this years ago, are still. Emeric tells him it is storage, not strength in a fight, and Armand says that makes a second column. When Emeric says anyone can do it, Armand gives him his own old line, that one man, once, is a story, and starts reading the whole class every week, body and core apart, with a third column for whether a core left alone shrinks. He copies out the names from under the mark and holds the letters until Kaldo has the readers to take them.

On Friday 5 March, in the gravity hall, Arthur's core, locked in the first week of the class and fed for six weeks, carries him. He has spent February learning the one gravity cast core flight needs, slowly, because gravity is not his base, and on the Friday he lifts a hand's width off the hall floor for about four seconds on his own core, with no suit, and comes down on his heels laughing. It is the first core flight in Kaldo by anyone but the king, a hop and not a flight; a core that can carry a grown body for real is years of work. Mira asks for the next turn and does not get off the floor. Ward writes it down. That evening on the harbour wall the Great Answer Tower is proved: Emeric has cut the Aperture into the cap's shard stone that week and Tess has cut the revolver's ring, and after the cylinder is turned dry through every chamber the lightning chamber fires once at a shielded hulk past the bay mouth and goes through its shield. Ward asks what one shot costs in liquid mana and writes the figure down. On Monday 8 March the first citizen's bands are fitted. Armand writes the street name, no fire in the street, before the first is fitted, and Kaldo News prints it. The first bands go on duty staff, healers, teachers and children, each carrying the Shell's key. Runa, under the crown's care and not a citizen, gets a yellow visitor's band and is furious it is not the same colour as the other children's. Ferra tells her directly that the colour is only the law's slowness. At the Works Town, Bram's electric arc furnace runs the first Aumere nodules into a nickel-copper-cobalt alloy, and the stack is dust-free because Imani made it so. Imani begins her dose study for the merfolk's thin-water trip, with Neris's leave and Neris's own blood, two hours a day in a dosed room. The voyage's cargo reaches the benches: the Rim's ash cement on Bram's test slab, sea silk on Abloh's table, the cloudstone on the station bench, storm honey in Imani's stores. Maeve has been home to the capital for a fortnight in February and came back early, saying the court is too quiet. Harrold comes back from the south on Friday 12 March, saying only that Cinderhale's register is honest and its officer still walks free.

On Sunday 6 March, Lantern Night, the first since the fleet sailed, the names go onto lanterns at the river wall: Declan; Bryony Fenwick and the two with her; the burned relief crew; the two drowned after the trap. Emeric writes Declan. Runa cannot write yet. Ferra offers to write Veya and Orren for her, and Runa says no, she will write them herself next year. For the first time the count is read by field: the year's graduates field by field, in the year the king was away and the University kept building; Sela's teachers' college's first class standing together; and the night school's adult readers last, who get the loudest cheer. Fennick's display goes up, and his own review is second-best. Emeric sees the dome's glow under the lanterns and thinks of Ferra's vault, four years in a box, and does not say it yet.

On the river wall Ferra tells Emeric what came back on Monday's read while the king was on the train south: one word from Saldis, carried by Sohra. Teach. Sohra's terms are her own. She will teach the ones who came out whole, because that is the only way she knows, and coming back is the first thing; the ones who started smaller learn the other way, like him, and he can teach those. The first lesson is the first of April.

Chapter 129: The First Room

On Saturday 13 March the first finished hall of the Peoples and Civilizations wing of the Grand opens beside the stadium, outside the walls by the campus on its tram line. The doors open at eight with no speeches. The walls, in the order a visitor meets them: at the door, the name-it-first wall, the war, the Thinning, the Leaving Terms, and the humans' own conscripts given to the rite by the thousand, a page of the Sea Hold's ledgers copied large in the dwarves' hand, columns of names nobody on the human side wrote down. Diary pages under glass, each with its writer's name and book number: a knight's drawing of the orc treaty-house, Sorrel's straight aurora, and Imani's times with her leave. Nothing about the Isle. The first cases of the collection, every tag copied out, Neris's beads, a Guest Fire bowl, Sea Hold iron, and where a cloudstone block would go, Neris's line about glass that is mostly air, because the one block is Bram's. Halloran's first canvas, the ring at Aumere from the wall water, from a dozen diaries. Ottoline's first figure, Neris, in her own Rim dress, her colour as she corrected it; Neris checked Aumere's corner and struck one line, which stays out. The colour wall, Ilenne's colours beside Kaldo's hand signs, where by the end of the first morning children are doing the bow. Kaldo's own record panel: Ward's crossing and the skiff family, the lost rifle that shot Zale, Corvan's shoulders and Imani's objection, Ferra's corrected entry with the Murr's maps, and the vote the king lost. The winged ones' letter, framed, UNPAID, with the box beside it. Ivo looks at the ring plate for a long time, then asks Bram, not Emeric, how big a pour the Sentinel Forge can spin. Bram says he will find out. On a table by the door lies the first issue of the University's Transactions, every result signed by whoever did it, Tess's transistor paper first.

The capital comes on the morning trains: Lord Cassel's people, the Circle's mages, and a party from the cathedral close who walk the whole room in silence and leave by the side door. Outside, about two hundred Heirs stand with the broadsheet and placards, with the watch at the doors this time rather than the knights, because Corbin asked for it that way after the riot, and nobody is stopped from going in. Honor Linden goes in. She is about fifty, a copy clerk at the records hall who has copied every ordinance Kaldo has passed for nine years, a widow whose only son died on the Isle of Shards in the succession war, and who signed the Heirs' broadsheet in February with her own name. She pays nothing and walks to the name-it-first wall to be angry at it, and stands in front of the Sea Hold's ledger page: the dwarves' columns of human boys sent to the rite by their own kings, names kept by the people the war was against, because nobody on her side wrote them down. She stands there a long time. She leaves without speaking to anyone. At the next week's meeting of the Heirs she tells them she will not call the guests beasts again, and that the room is telling the truth about one thing at least, and half of them leave her and the other half stay and are angrier. The capital's pulpit preaches the next day that Kaldo has built a temple to the things below. Doran's pulpit reads from the death book and does not mention the room.

At ten, like anyone, the king comes and pays nothing. He walks the room once with Ferra and Imani in the crowd and stops at Kaldo's own record panel, at the line about the vote he lost. A boy of about eight asks him who lost it. He says he did. The boy says that's stupid, he's the king. Ferra writes that down.

Where the Story Stands After Chapter 129

Kaldo has been told since 11 January, and the First Room has been open since 13 March, free every day, its first wall naming what the humans' own side did. The guests live in the city under signed rights and use them: Neris speaks at the bench and in the gallery, Runa goes to school in a yellow band and has retired "father" as a joke for now, and Duncan has a locked core, a steward who stood witness, and a log that waits for a hull north. Emeric has confessed to Corbin, who had guessed, laughed, hugged him badly, and given back the memory of the stair. Corbin keeps Kaldo's chair, has taken a man alive on a landing, and has his Private Arms Act passed narrowly and amended against him. Ferra is censured and Ilsa holds the stay to February next year. The king lost a vote on the winged ones' fund and did not pay round it. Amara told him a golem body is a room that does not taste bread.

The Church's hand is inside the city. Its order, keyed to scripture Kaldo printed, named the sister as the target and dated the fleet's passage by Kaldo's own notice. A witness was poisoned in the king's cells, and the organizer behind the clerk and the knife is a lead, not a name. Saban has stopped dating notices, hung three decoy beacons on the chain, taken positions off every open set and put a scrambler and a lock on everything. The trial is done, and the king's written yes, that he could have caught the falling crews and chose not to, is on the record. Avarran's Heirs have shrunk without vanishing: Honor Linden turned halfway after standing in front of the dwarves' ledger, and half her people went with her.

The family has worked out that it took blood, a bonded stone and thick air to wake the line's dragons, that no king was ever given a stone, and that Waylon is next. Amara has asked who gets to decide for the children, and nobody has answered. The press with intake has begun at home, Arthur has lifted a hand's width on his own core, and the first citizen's bands are on. On the Isle, under Harrold's defended exception, the survey of the hollow was deferred, an engineer's warning and Tess's vote were outvoted, and the shaft is still going down for oil.

This batch is about a city choosing to be told the truth and paying for it in public: in court, in the ledger, in a vote the king loses and a censure his chancellor takes. Kaldo meets its first organised hatred with evidence, open trials and a room, and it does not fully win, but the one argument it does not lose is the record itself.
