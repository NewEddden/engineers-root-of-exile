Book 2, Chapter 7: The Bill

He wasn't breathing properly when she got him onto the table, and for the first hour Imani didn't think about anything else.

It wasn't that he'd stopped. He was breathing. It was that he was breathing like a man who'd been held under, in great dragging heaves with nothing at the top of them, and between the heaves his lips went grey, and under her sight the whole of him was doing a thing she'd seen in drowned children pulled out late: everything drawn in to the middle, to the heart and the head, and the rest of him let go cold to save them. He'd gone minutes without air. She didn't know how many. Nobody did. Somewhere out over the cloud, inside that fire, he'd stopped breathing and gone on fighting, and his body had paid it out of whatever it kept for drowning, and now the bill had come.

"Mask," she said. Bettany had it on him before she'd finished. "Bag. Squeeze when he pulls. Not before. With him."

Bettany squeezed with him. Imani put both hands flat on his chest, one over the heart and one low on the right side where she could feel the ribs gone, and opened her sight all the way, which she never did on a patient, and looked.

Two ribs, the seventh and eighth on the left, broken clean, the ends not through anything. Good. The left arm, a crack in the long bone above the wrist, nothing out of place. Bruising everywhere, deep, in the muscle, in the gut wall, round the kidneys, the kind you got from being hit by something huge through something soft. She could see where the softness had been. He'd padded himself. The bruises had edges, as if each blow had landed on a cushion and the cushion had spread it; without the cushion, she thought, looking at the size of them, he'd have come back in pieces, if he'd come back.

And bleeding. Small, slow, in a dozen places, the way bruised gut bleeds. Nothing she couldn't stop. She stopped it. She went in with the water in him, a thread at a time, and closed what was open, and it took her forty minutes, standing, with her hands on him, and when she straightened up her back cracked and Ferra was at the foot of the table and had been for all forty of them.

She hadn't noticed her come. Ferra had her book shut under her arm and both hands on the table's rail, and she was looking at his face. Not at Imani. At his face, the grey lips, the burned hair at his temples gone to frizz and stink. She looked as if she were doing a sum and it wasn't coming out.

"Is he..." said Ferra.

"He's here." Imani wiped her hands. "He's here. He's not going anywhere if I can help it, and I can." She heard her own voice and didn't like it. She'd meant it to be the voice she used to families. It had come out as something else.

Ferra nodded. She didn't move.

"Sit down," said Imani.

"I've the stretchers to count."

"Bettany's counted them. Fourteen burned. Two bad, the rigger and the knight, on the end beds. Twelve dressed and lying down. Nobody dead. Sit down before you fall down and I've three of you."

Ferra sat down on the stool at the foot of the table. She put the book on her knees and didn't open it. After a while she put out one hand and laid it on his ankle, through the blanket, and left it there.

They stayed like that. Imani worked. She splinted the arm. She bound the ribs, loosely, because a tight binding on a man who couldn't breathe was a way to kill him. She sat on the edge of the table with her fingers on the pulse in his neck and counted it every five minutes and wrote the count on the board, and it was fast and thin and then less fast and less thin, and at about eleven his breathing changed. The heaves came down. A breath went in all the way to the bottom and came out again, and then another, and his lips went from grey to the ordinary colour of a man who'd been badly knocked about.

Ferra made a sound. It wasn't a word. It was small and came out of her all at once and she put her hand over her mouth after it, as if it had got out without leave.

Imani didn't look at her. She looked at the board, and wrote the next count on it, and found that her own hand was shaking so much the figure came out crooked, and she rubbed it out with the side of her thumb and wrote it again.



The dragon had watched the whole morning from the after rail.

She'd stood there, in her woman's shape, with her back to the sea and her arms folded, and the blood dried black down her neck, and nobody had made her go and nobody had dared go near her but Imani, twice, to look at the cut and not touch it. The K Amara had gone back to her own station at nine, when the burned were all across, with her port side black and her after store still smoking through the shut vents. The convoy had come down out of the Band at a quarter past seven, slowly, the K Discovery last, because Sorrel had stayed up alone for the length of time it took to put a new drum over the side where the old one had stopped, with two flight knights off the K Amara hanging in the air beside her. Ward's order. Ten in the hold now. Linnet had reported the chain whole at seven forty, and Kaldo's answer had come back at seven forty-one, from Amara, one word: Status? Ferra had answered it, from the stool at the foot of the table, by sending a slip across by boat with Imani's count on it, and her own initial.

At four in the afternoon the dragon unfolded her arms and came forward along the deck.

Imani was at the end bed with the burned knight, changing a dressing. She saw the dragon stop at Emeric's table and look down at him. He was asleep now, properly asleep, breathing, with the mask off. The dragon studied him with her head on one side. Then she looked across the deck at Imani, and Imani gave the dressing to Bettany and came.

"He lives," said the dragon.

"He lives."

"Good." She said it as though it settled something on a list. "I go. To my elders. I tell them what I see." She looked down at him again. "A thief in the sky, with the face. Who beats me. And stops." Her mouth went thin. "They will not like the stopping."

"What's your name?" said Imani.

The dragon looked at her as if it were a strange thing to want.

"Sohra," she said.

"I'm Imani. I'm his doctor. If you come back, I'll stitch that."

Sohra touched the dried blood on her neck with two fingers, for the first time all day, and looked at the fingers.

"Maybe," she said. She looked past Imani, across the deck, to the stool where Ferra was sitting with her hand on his ankle, and breathed in, and her face did the thing it had done in the morning: that small honest frown. Then she went to the rail and over it, and was a woman falling, and then was not.

Nobody on the K Endeavor's deck saw her go after that. She went west, low, and fast, and the light was in their eyes.



He woke at six on the Monday morning and wanted to try it again.

Imani had known he would. She'd sat up with him from two, because Ferra had finally gone to sleep on the next bed in her clothes, and at six he'd opened his eyes and looked at the deckhead and lain still, which was a good sign, and then said "Imani," which was a better one, and then, before she'd got to the side of the table, "I want to see if I can call it."

"No."

"Carefully. Not the skin. Not the whole of it. Just the..." He moved his good hand at his own shoulder. "The back. I felt it come. I want to see if I can find where it came from. If I can find it once, cold, with nothing at me, I'll know if it's a thing I can do or a thing that does me." He was still grey. His voice was thin. He was perfectly reasonable. "I've a plan. Three steps. You can stand there with the bag."

"No," said Imani again.

He looked at her.

"You went five minutes without air yesterday," she said. "Maybe more. Nobody counted, because you were through a cloud. Your body took everything out of everything to keep your heart going. I've had my hands in you for most of a day putting things back where they go. You've two ribs broken and an arm, and blood in places I'm not going to tell you about while you're lying down." She kept her voice level, and it stayed level, mostly. "And the last thing you did before you fell on my deck was that. On your back. And then you went down like you'd been shot. That thing costs. I don't know what. I don't know how much. Nor do you." She folded her arms. "As your doctor, no. Not today. Not this week. Not on this voyage until I say, and I'll say when I've a reason to, and you'll not get round me."

He opened his mouth.

"Don't," she said. "I'm not asking."

He shut it. He lay still a moment more, and she saw him decide not to argue, and saw what it cost him, and was glad it cost him something.

"All right," he said.

"Thank you."

"I'm only saying it's three steps."

"That's my line," said Imani. "Get your own."

At eight he was well enough to be useful, which she'd been dreading. She'd come back to his table from the burns with her hands smelling of the yellow salve, and he'd watched her come, and said, without preamble, "How many of them are minor?"

"Twelve. Dressed and clean. They'll heal on their own in a fortnight, three weeks. The two on the end are mine for a long time."

"Not the two on the end. The twelve." He turned his head on the pillow. "The dose water. You've a tank of it for the knights. Bettany's got the wash spray for the deck. If you put the one in the other, fine, a mist, over a dressed burn that's clean..."

"No."

"You haven't heard what goes wrong yet."

"I know what goes wrong. I've seen mending kill a man." She'd watched it: a wound closed too fast over what was in it, and the man dead of the closing. She didn't need to say so. He'd been there.

"Then let me say it anyway. It's the rule." He said it in order, as he always did, and she stood there with the salve on her hands and let him. "It shuts a wound over whatever's in it, so the wound has to be clean first, every time, no exceptions. It can give you a lump where you don't want one, growth in the wrong place. And it doesn't always know when to stop. So: only the minor ones, only dressed and clean, never anything deep, never on anybody who's ill in himself. And you look at every one every day, and you write it all down, and the first time anything grows that shouldn't, it stops." He shut his eyes. "That's what goes wrong. That's all I've got. The thing it might do right is a fortnight off a burn."

She stood there.

He was right, as far as it went. She'd known he'd be right. That was what made her angry, mostly: that he'd lie on her table with his ribs strapped and say the true thing in order, and she'd have to stand there and look for the hole in it, and there wasn't one, except the one she couldn't say, which was that she was tired, and frightened still, and didn't want one more new thing on her deck that could go wrong in a way she hadn't seen before.

"Four," she said.

He opened his eyes.

"Four, and four not. Matched. Same place on the body, same size, same day burned. Bettany does it, not me, and not you. Bettany keeps the column. I look at every one every morning. It's logged and it's not issued. Nobody outside this deck hears the word mist from me or from Bettany or from you until I say, and if one of them so much as itches wrong, it stops." She looked at him. "That's mine. Not yours."

"It's yours," he said.

Bettany did it at ten. She mixed it in the wash spray's bottle, the knights' dose water, measured with the glass, and went down the row of the twelve with the atomizer and the book. Four she sprayed: a fine cold mist over a clean dressed burn on the back of a hand, a forearm, a neck, a calf, each one patted dry after with gauze. Four she didn't, and matched them as near as she could, hand for hand, forearm for forearm. She ruled a column in the back of Imani's register with eight lines in it, and wrote at the head of it, in her round careful print, Mist: logged, not issued.

Ferra came across at noon, woke and washed and in a clean collar, and Bettany showed her the column, because Ferra would ask, and Ferra asked what the dose water cost.

Imani told her.

Ferra did it in her head, the volume in the bottle and the water in the tank and what the tank had cost to fill at Kaldo, and said the cost of a burn's worth, and it was so small a sum that Bettany laughed.

Imani didn't laugh. She stood and looked at the figure Ferra had said and felt something go cold in her that hadn't gone cold all morning.

"That's the trouble," she said.

"What is?"

"That it's cheap. If it works, and it's cheap, everybody'll want it. On everything. And they won't wait for four and four not." She took the register from Bettany and shut it. "I'd sooner it was dear."



Ferra asked him at four, in her accounts voice, with the door of Imani's cabin shut and Imani sitting on the end of the bunk writing up the morning, and him propped on three pillows in the bunk itself because Imani had said he could be moved from the table as far as the cabin and no further.

"Can you do it at will?"

He'd been waiting for somebody to ask. "I don't know. That's what I want to find out. I think it's like the skin: it comes when something's at me that's more than I can take, and the question is whether that's the only door to it or just the first one I found." He'd had all morning to think about it, flat on his back, and it came out of him in order. "I'd start cold, nothing at me, and see if I can find it by feel. Then I'd try with a threat I know, Ward with a wall, something I can measure. Then with Imani on the bag and somebody counting my breath, because if it takes the breath like the skin does..."

He stopped, because he'd looked at her face.

Ferra was sitting on the stool by the bunk with her hands folded in her lap and her book shut, and she was looking at him with the expression of someone who has asked a question precisely and is waiting, with great patience, for the other person to notice which question it was.

"Oh," he said.

"Yes," said Ferra.

"You weren't asking for the... you weren't asking for the knowledge."

"I was asking," said Ferra, "if you could do it at will. Wings. Horns." She tilted her head a fraction. "I'm only interested in whether it's reliable."

He looked at her. He felt the ring, and through it the small bright amused thing in her that she wasn't hiding at all, and under it the other thing, the one from yesterday morning on the stool, that she was hiding, mostly, and that the joke was standing in front of.

"Where I'm from," he said slowly, "there were women like you."

"I'd hope so."

"No. I mean there were... they'd..." He could feel it going wrong and couldn't stop it. "There's a sort of thing they sold. Made. For women. In the shape of. Dragons. Sort of. Not real ones, obviously, there weren't any real ones, it was a... it was a thing you could buy. By post. People bought them. It was a whole..." He stopped. "Business."

There was a short silence in the cabin.

"In the shape of a dragon," said Ferra.

"Roughly."

"By post."

"It's not..." He shut his eyes. "I'm making it worse."

"You are," said Ferra, with interest.

Imani hadn't looked up from her board. She turned a page and went on writing, and said, in exactly the voice she used to ask a patient about his bowels:

"Were they to scale?"

He put his good arm over his face.

Ferra laughed. It came out of her helplessly, the first time since the deck, and she bent forward on the stool with her forehead nearly on her knees and laughed until she had to wipe her eyes; and Imani wrote one more line on the board and capped her pencil and looked up at last, and her mouth was perfectly straight, and her eyes weren't.

"Later," he said, from under his arm.

"No," said Imani.

"Not later," said Ferra, wiping her eyes. "Much later. Work first." She put the back of her hand against his good cheek. "You're the work."



When they'd stopped, he gave them the list. He'd been carrying it since the air over the cloud, and a list said aloud was a list somebody else could hold.

"Ilenne's word for my eyes," he said. "Suvarre. On the beach, at the start. She said it like a word everybody knew. She said there were peoples out here who'd see my eyes and..." He searched for it. "React. Her word. She wouldn't say how." He moved his hand. "And a dragon who's never seen me and knows my face. Who was told my face when she was small, so she'd know it. By her elders. Her words."

"And us," said Ferra quietly.

"And you two. 'Those two. Since my mother.'" He looked at the deckhead. "Three things. Nobody's said what any of them means. I don't think Ilenne knows. I'm sure Sohra does, and she didn't say."

"And you," said Imani. "Have you looked?"

He had. He'd gone in at three in the morning while she slept in the chair, down into the place where he kept everything, the long rooms with the shelves, and walked along them to the end, to the shelf that had always been sealed: the shelf with the things on it from before he was seven, that he knew and didn't remember. He'd stood in front of it as he'd stood a hundred times. He'd never been able to open it and he couldn't now.

"One of the spines has a title," he said. "It didn't before. It was blank. They were all blank. Now one of them has a title on it in letters I know, and I can't read it." He was quiet. "It's not that it's in another tongue. I know the letters. I look at them and they don't go into words."

Neither of them said anything for a while.

"Call your mother," said Ferra.

He called her that evening, from the cabin, on Linnet's long set by way of the K Endeavor's, through the new drum and the chain behind it. He told her about the dragon. He told her the word, and the face, and the shelf. She listened all the way through without a sound, and he could picture her at the end of the desk in the line room with her made hands flat in front of her.

"I don't know," she said when he'd finished. "I'm sorry. I've nothing. My father never spoke of dragons except as a thing that was gone, and the tower's books say they went with the old peoples, and nothing about faces." A pause. "I'll look. Armand's notes from the vault. Jerome's. I'll look tonight."

"Thank you."

"Emeric." Another pause, longer. "You stopped breathing."

"I'm breathing now."

"I know. Imani told me." His mother's voice did nothing at all, very carefully. "I wanted to hear it from you."



Ilenne named it on the Monday night, at the K Amara's table, with Ruan there for the escorts and Arthur standing at the door because Ward had sent for him.

Emeric had been allowed across. Imani had brought him herself, in the K Endeavor's boat, and sat him in a chair with a cushion behind the ribs and said he was to stay in it, and he was staying in it.

"The first fire," said Ilenne. "On the racks. Not on people, not on the ship. Only on the thing that takes." She drew a line on the table with one finger. "That is the telling. That is how a dragon says stop. Everybody knows. My people, and the others, all the others out here. You see it, you close the thing that takes, you go away, she goes away. Is finished." She looked round. "You don't know it. How can you know it? It is not on your side."

"And we put the shell up in her face," said Ward.

"The shell was up already," said Emeric.

"She doesn't know that. To her it's a wall raised against the telling." Ward said it flatly. "We'd have done the same."

"Yes." Ilenne nodded. "To her, a wall is no. And then she comes again. Harder." She looked at Ruan, who'd been sitting still since she started, with his big scarred hands on the table. "Nobody fires on the telling. Nobody. Your guns, they hold. That was right."

Ruan looked at his hands. "We held because Ward said hold."

"Then Ward was right. I'm telling you. You don't have to think in the night that your guns made her come back. Your guns did nothing. The wall did." She turned to Emeric. "And there is the other."

"Which other?"

Ilenne hesitated. Her colour moved at her throat, slowly, the grey-blue of a thing being turned over.

"To her," she said, "you are here. That is the wrong. Not only the taking. That you are here at all. Out here." She spread her hands on the table, at the sea outside, all of it. "There is an old promise. Between the dry land and... everybody. A long time ago. I know there is one. I know it says something about here and there. I don't know the words. Nobody I know knows the words. Old people know there is a promise." She looked at him. "She knows. I think she knows the words."

At the door, Arthur hadn't moved. He'd come in when Ward sent for him, in his blues, and stood with his back to the frame, and he'd listened to all of it with his face giving the room what it always gave it, which was nothing; and Emeric, who was watching him because he'd wanted to see how he took it, saw him take it. It was in his hands. They'd been loose at his sides. When Ilenne said nobody fires on the telling, they closed.

He'd taken the horn off something that had the right of it.

Later, from the K Amara's bridge deck, waiting for Imani's boat, Emeric saw him at the after rail with Mira in the last light. Arthur was talking. He talked a good while, for Arthur, with his head down, and Mira stood beside him with her arms folded on the rail and listened to all of it and didn't say anything until he'd finished, and then said something short. Emeric didn't hear what. He didn't try to.



Ward's orders went into the book that night as the fleet's, not as new ones. He was particular about that.

"It's what we did," he said. "I'm writing down what we did, so we do it again, and so the next man in my chair knows we did it on purpose." He read it out with the paper in front of him. "Escorts and every armed hull. A lone contact in the air, or on the sea, or a thing done to us that's limited and that we don't understand: you don't fire on it because it's there. You track it. You cover what can't be lost: the racks, the lift, the hospital deck, the drums. You look for a signal, and if Ilenne's on a set you put her on it. You fire at once if a life's in danger or a hull is. That's the line." He turned the page. "And on a deck. If anything lands on a Kaldo deck, check fire is called that second, and no rifle fires on that deck, whoever's on it. Knights only. Hands and spears."

"It leaves a grey," said Emeric.

"It leaves a great deal of grey. A life in danger. A hull in danger. Somebody's going to have to call which, in a hurry, and he'll call it wrong once." Ward looked up. "I'd rather he called it wrong once than fired on every fish."

Ruan cleared his throat. He hadn't spoken since Ilenne. "With respect."

Ward waited.

"She took the K Amara's shell off on her second pass," said Ruan. "Second pass. By the third she was at the hospital deck. If we'd fired on the second, she'd not have reached the third." He spoke slowly, the way he laid a gun. "Your rule says wait till a life's in danger. Out here, by the time you can see that it is, the thing that's doing it is bigger than your ship. I've four crews on two escorts and I'll have them dead waiting for a signal from something that doesn't know we're asking."

Nobody at the table said he was wrong, because he wasn't.

"Write it down," said Ward to Ferra. "His objection. As he said it." He looked back at Ruan. "I'm keeping the rule. If we'd fired on the second pass, we'd have fired on a law we didn't know we'd broken, and she'd have had every right to burn us out of the sky, and her elders with her. You're right about the cost. I'm paying it." He held Ruan's eyes. "And it stays in the book with your name on it, so that if I'm wrong, everybody knows who told me."

Ruan held his eyes a while. Then he nodded, and sat back.

When the others had gone Ward stayed, and Ferra stayed, and Emeric stayed in his chair because he'd been told to.

"Twice in ten days," said Ward.

Emeric knew what he meant.

"The ride," said Ward. "The whole convoy parked in the Band with your body on a deck, because you'd decided you could manage it. And yesterday. You went off the K Discovery after that thing alone, with an order to the rest of us to stay put, because you'd decided you could manage it. And you could. Both times." He put the paper down. "Both times, the fleet hung on a risk that only you thought you could carry. I'm saying it. I'm not asking you to change. I know you won't. I'm saying that I see it, and that I'll go on saying it, every time."

"I know."

"That's all."

It wasn't, quite. Ferra had her book open.

"The charge," she said.

They did it in a quarter of an hour, because there wasn't much to it. What they had: hull four's liquid mana, of which the K Amara would take a share to fill her starboard bank while the port was refitted; what every hull held now, which was full from yesterday's climb, except the K Amara; and Noll's slow fill from the thick air at sea level, which Ferra had been watching in pencil for two weeks and which was real. What they didn't know: whether Sohra's law was about the Band or about any drawing at all. Whether low charging, at sea level, from thick air, counted. Whether the next dragon would tell them before it burned them.

"She said the sky," said Emeric. "'You drink the sky.' Not the sea."

"She said it in a language she learned from a book," said Ferra. "I'm not hanging a fleet on her prepositions."

"Is there a way to reach the ring without drawing at all?"

"No," said Ferra.

"Then we draw low," said Ward. "From the air. Slower. Longer stops. We climb only when we must: when Ferra's mark is crossed, or for a drum, and then short, and with a watch out for anything with wings." He looked at Emeric. "And nobody, at this table or in the fleet, calls it lawful. We don't know that it is."

"Nobody calls it safe either," said Emeric.

Ferra drew a line across the page, and wrote a figure above it, and turned it round so they could see. The reserve mark. Below that line in any hull's racks, they climbed, dragon or no dragon. She initialled it.



The two days went on the K Amara.

Bram came across from hull four at first light on the Tuesday with Dagny and two crates and a face like a man who'd been told about his roof. He stood in front of the K Amara's port rack bank, or the black twisted frame where it had been, and stood over it without saying a word, and then said, "Right," and took his coat off. By noon the dead racks were off and over the side, and the new bank, hull four's one spare of that pattern, was coming up through the hatch in pieces on a tackle; and by the Wednesday dawn it was bolted down and fed and its necks were open on the air, at sea level, filling slowly from the thick air the way Noll's dials had promised a fortnight before.

The shard stone took the same two days. Armand did it, with Bram, in the shell room under the bridge deck, sitting in a chair because Imani had threatened him: the crazed stone out of its housing on a bench, two facets white with cracks like frost on a window, and beside it a dish of shard dust that Dagny had ground from the spare canister's flakes, as fine as flour, and a flask of liquid mana from hull four's tanks. It was the grown-stone recipe, run backwards and small. Dust and mana into the cracks, with the stone itself for the host; and the new grain would grow into the cracks from either side, slowly, the way a bone knits, and close them.

"It's not a filler," said Armand, when Emeric came down to look on the Tuesday afternoon, against orders. "It's stone. Same stone. When it's set you won't find the cracks with a glass." He touched the bench beside the dish. "Two days to set. I'll re-key the band after. Until then the port quarter's weak and I'd thank the dragons to stay home."

"And it won't mend one broken through," said Emeric. "Patch it at sea. Recut it at home."

"Which is what I'd have said, if you'd let me get to it," said Armand. "You can tell Ferra. I'll tell her too."

Ferra had already been told. She'd added a line to the stores at noon: shard dust, by weight, two jars, against the next one.

The fleet had its say over the same two days, and didn't say it with one voice. The twelve on the K Endeavor's deck, the dressed ones, sent a man to Emeric's chair on the Tuesday to say thank you, which embarrassed everyone. The knights on the K Amara's aprons, when he came aboard, stood up. Not to attention. They just stood up, all down the apron, the way men stand up when somebody comes into a room they hadn't thought would come; and he didn't know what to do with it, and Ward said "Sit down," and they sat. A deckhand off the K Discovery went to Sorrel on the Monday and asked to be moved to hull four. Sorrel moved him. She told Emeric about it flatly and didn't say what the man had said, and Emeric didn't ask. And the K Amara's cook sent word by the boatswain that he'd made a stew on the Sunday morning for three hundred and nobody had eaten it, on account of the dragon, and that if it happened again he'd like it known in advance.

Bren came to Imani's deck on the Tuesday evening to have his shoulder looked at, where the dragon had put him down on top of Mira. It was a bruise. Imani told him so. He sat on the end of a bed afterwards with his book open on his knee, the voyage book, the oilcloth one, and wrote in it slowly with his tongue out, and Emeric, from the chair by Imani's door, saw what he'd written when he got up, because he left it open on the bed to dry.

3 May. A draggon.

There was a drawing under it. It was very bad.

Maeve came across on the Wednesday morning in the K Amara's boat, before they lifted, and stood in front of his chair with her arms folded.

"Lightning," she said.

"Lightning?"

"I taught you lightning. In the winter. You stood there and let me teach you lightning as if you'd never so much as made a spark, and I was very patient with you, and you said thank you." Her chin went up. "And now you've got wings."

"I didn't know about the wings."

"No. But you knew about something." She looked at him, proud and fierce and fifteen kinds of hurt. "What else? What else have you been keeping from me, brother?"

He didn't answer her. He couldn't, there, with half the K Endeavor's deck in earshot; and she knew it, and stood there anyway, long enough for him to know she knew it.

"Later," he said.

"Everybody says later to you," said Maeve. "I've noticed." And went back to her boat.



Ferra read the bill out at seven on the Wednesday, the sixth, on the set to every hull, from the K Amara's bridge, before they lifted for the last run in.

She read it in the voice she kept for figures, and every hull heard it, and Emeric heard it on the K Discovery's forward deck where Imani had let him go back to, on a mattress, with a blanket, and a bucket.

"The K Amara's port rack bank, burned through on the second pass, refitted from hull four's spare. Hull four has no spare of that pattern now. Her shard stone, crazed across two facets, mended in the stone and set, and the band re-keyed. The shell holds full on all quarters as of six this morning." A breath. "One dragon rifle, the K Amara's second, lost at sea in the Band, with its case, at the position in the log. Five left with the fleet. The K Amara keeps Arthur's. The after dry store: ten tonnes of flour, peas and biscuit, burned or smoked through. That's eight days of the fleet's food, or four in every hundred of what we loaded. Stores are being moved across from hull four today, and the fleet goes to fish days on Friday, which is sooner than the sheet. Liquid mana: hull four's reserve, drawn, by the volume in the log." Another breath. "Fourteen burned. Two of them badly, the rigger and the knight, on the K Endeavor. Twelve dressed and mending. One man over the rail and caught. Nobody killed."

There was a short pause on the set, the length of a page being turned.

"Two days lost," said Ferra. "The run today brings us to the ring by evening. That's the bill."

The set clicked off. All down the line the necks closed and the hulls began to lift off the swell one after another, in the order on her sheet, and ahead, a mile off, the long smooth place on the sea drew out and turned west.

Emeric lay on the mattress and watched it go.

"That's the bill," said Sorrel, from the wheel, without turning round. "Is it?"

He thought about a spine on a sealed shelf with a word on it he couldn't read.

"That's what she can count," he said.
