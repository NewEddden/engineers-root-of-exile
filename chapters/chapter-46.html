Chapter 46 - Turned Over

The instrument that made two baronies his was three sheets of good paper and a quantity of wax, and it was, Emeric thought, reading it through for the last time before he let it out of his hands, the single most dangerous thing he had ever built, more dangerous than the wall or the furnace or the device growing slow on the bench, because unlike those it worked whether or not he was standing next to it and could not be taken away by any man who did not first take the crown. Wayford and Grayrock did not come to him. That was the whole architecture of the thing, and he had spent more care on it than on any rune. He had not marched on them and he had not bought them. Their lords had been removed for cause, the cause read and found and documented until it was not a story but a record, and the holdings had reverted, as holdings do when a lord forfeits them, and the crown, having adjudicated the forfeit, had granted the vacant ground on to the nearest capable hand, which happened, by an arrangement no line of the document admitted to, to be his. A man who seizes a barony holds it exactly as long as he can hold it. A man to whom the crown grants a forfeited barony holds it the way a wall holds, because something larger than himself is now standing behind the stone.

Keller had gone over every line of it on the crown's behalf, which was the point of Keller, a man whose signature meant the emperor's lawyers had already tried to break the thing and failed. He had found nothing to break because there was nothing, and he had said as much in the flat unimpressed way of a man who was paid to assume the worst and had, this once, been denied the pleasure. Emeric had built it to survive exactly that reading. He had built it, he understood now, to survive a reading much colder than Keller's, the reading it would get in a year or five years from a brother who wanted Kaldo and everything under it and would go over these same three sheets looking for the one loose thread that turned a lawful grant into a naked seizure. There was no thread. He had made sure there was no thread. And having made sure, he discovered that the paper had written the next hard thing for him, the way the covenant had once written a year of his life, because a grant that survives on being lawful only stays lawful if the ground under it is administered lawfully too, and the ground under it was, at that moment, full of empty chairs.

The old administrations had been cleaned out. That was the cause, read and documented, the rot in the toll-books and the grain-counts and the quiet standing arrangements by which a Wayford factor grew fat skimming a waypoint's traffic and a Grayrock overseer sold the same ore twice on paper and once in fact. It was real rot, not manufactured, and removing it was the justest thing he had done in months and also, he was aware, the most convenient, because a barony full of documented thieves was a barony the crown would let a man clean. But cleaning left holes. Every skimming factor and favor-bought overseer he had turned out had sat in a chair that now needed a body in it, and the bodies he put in those chairs would decide, more than any wax seal, whether the word for what he had just done was administration or conquest.

He ran the appointments the way he ran everything, through the pipeline, because the pipeline did not care whose feelings it hurt and that was its entire virtue. Ferra took the ledgers of every candidate and read them the way she read all ledgers, for the gap between what a man earned and what a man spent, and flagged the gaps without sentiment. Ward took the softer channel, the quiet word passed among people who knew who leaned on family and who leaned on merit, and brought back the names of the men who were good and the men who were only well-connected, which were, as ever, two lists that overlapped less than anyone hoped. Between the two of them the field narrowed to the people who could actually do the work and had not stolen while doing it, and that field, when it was finished narrowing, contained almost none of the people Emeric would have chosen with his gut.

Which was how Merrin came to be standing in front of him on a grey afternoon, hat turned over and over in two big honest hands, wanting the Wayford stewardship and not getting it.

Merrin had been one of the first. When Kaldo was still a mouth of dead ground and Emeric was still a rumor of a prince nobody had reason to believe, Merrin had run the rebuilt granary through the worst of it, honestly, to the last measured grain, when running it dishonestly would have been easy and unremarked and no one would ever have known. He had stood in the barony when standing in the barony was a bad bet. He had earned, by any accounting a decent man keeps in his chest, the right to be lifted when there was finally something to be lifted into. And Emeric had given the Wayford stewardship to a woman Merrin had never met, a Wayford local who had kept the honest set of the toll-books in a locked drawer for six years while the fat factor kept the other set in the open, and who had, when the reckoning came, simply handed her drawer to Ferra and let the numbers say what they said. She knew Wayford's traffic the way Merrin knew Kaldo's granary, which was to say completely, and Merrin did not know Wayford's traffic at all.

"I'd learn it," Merrin said. He did not raise his voice. That was the worst of it, that he did not raise his voice. "I learned the granary. I learned it from nothing. You know I did, my lord. You were there."

"I was there," Emeric said, because it was true and because the man deserved to hear it said back to him plainly, and because he had learned, in a small house across town, the weight of being told the true shape of a thing even when the true shape did not help. "You'd learn it. You'd learn it well, given three years and a barony's traffic to practice on, and Wayford does not have three years to spend being your schoolroom, and I do not have the right to make it one." He set it down as level as he could. "The woman I gave it to does not need to learn it. That is the whole of the reason, and it is the only reason, and I am not going to insult you by dressing it in a kinder one."

Merrin took that. He was not a stupid man, which made it harder, not easier. A stupid man would have argued the merit and lost the argument and gone away angry at being wrong. Merrin understood the merit perfectly, agreed with it even, Emeric could see him agreeing with it, and it changed nothing at all about the thing that was actually happening in his chest, because the thing in his chest was not an argument about competence. It was a door he had spent three lean years walking toward, closing, quietly, in his face, opened for a stranger who had done nothing but be better at a job than the man who had bled for the lord who owned it. There was no answer to that. Emeric had a dozen and every one of them was true and not one of them was worth saying, because the man was not asking to be answered. He was asking to matter, and the honest structural truth was that his mattering and his qualification had been allowed to come apart, and that Emeric had let them, on purpose, and would do it again.

Because he had built the whole thing to survive the cold reading, and the cold reading did not stop at the wax. If he staffed his lawful grant with the men who loved him rather than the men who could do the work, then in a year, when the peer-lords sent their careful auditors to walk his books under the polite fiction of due diligence, the auditors would find a barony administered by the prince's own faction, and the word for that was not administration. The word for that was the exact word he had used, in a documented and lawful way, to strip the last lord of this ground: a holding run by favor and not by fitness, a chair sold for loyalty instead of filled for merit. He would have removed a thief and installed a court. He would have made the escheat a lie in the only place a lie could not be papered over, which was the ground itself, the actual daily fact of who sat in which chair and why. Every enemy he had was waiting for precisely that, the loyalty cult, the private machine, the prince who kept his own close and called it justice, and the surest way to hand it to them was to be kind to Merrin.

So he was not kind to Merrin. He was fair, which was a colder thing and, from a lord, probably the same thing wearing work clothes. He offered the man real work, a genuine post inside Kaldo where his knowledge of the granary was worth more than any stranger's, and he watched Merrin hear the offer as what it partly was, a consolation, a smaller chair, and take it because a man with a family takes the chair he is given, and go out with his hat still turning in his hands, and Emeric filed the whole exchange under a heading and reached for the next candidate's ledger and had it half open before he let himself finish the thought he had been holding off.

He had read the load path right. He was sure of that, as sure as he had been of the wall, and being right about the wall had never once been in question. Staff the grant by merit or the grant fails at the audit, that was true, load-bearing, correct. What he had not priced, because he never priced it, was that Merrin did not live in the load path. Merrin lived in a small honest chest that had kept a granary true in the dark for a lord who now, given the first real thing to give, had given it to a stranger, and no correctness about audits reached into that chest or ever would. Emeric had assumed, the way he always assumed, that a man who understood the reason would in time be reconciled to it, because Emeric would have been, because Emeric was a man for whom a good enough reason closed an account. Merrin was not that man. Almost no one was that man. Emeric had spent, to buy a legitimacy that would read flawless on every page anyone could ever audit, a thing that appeared on no page at all, the plain early loyalty of the people who had stood in his dead ground when standing there was foolish, and he had spent it deliberately, and he had spent it, he suspected, at a rate he was not yet able to feel and would go on not feeling until the bill came due in some quiet way years from now, a name that did not defend him when it could have, a door that stayed shut on a night he needed it open.

The baronies were turned over. Lawfully, cleanly, on three sheets of good paper that would break no reading, cold or otherwise. The chairs were turned over, the thieves out and the able in, by a pipeline that did not care and was right not to. And in the ground between him and his earliest people something else had turned over too, quiet as soil, recorded in no ledger and findable by no audit, and Emeric noted it the way he noted every reading with no column to sit beside, set it in the drawer where such things went, and did not yet understand that it was the only entry on the page that would ever come back for what it was owed.
