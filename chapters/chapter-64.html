Book 2, Chapter 25: The Shutter

Linnet's voice came out of the set on the shelf by the door at nineteen minutes past seven, and Imani had her bag on her shoulder before it had finished the first sentence.

"Zale's shot. On the breakwater, at the stage head. In the side. Ilenne's taken him down the stages. Not to the station. Down. Arthur says she said her mother's bell." A breath. "Arthur says it's bad and he's alive. Mira marked the shot. A roof over the kiln yard."

Imani took the set off the shelf and clipped it on.

"Imani," she said into it. "I'm going."

"Where?"

"Down."

She was out through the wooden door before Linnet answered, with the gill pack off its hook in her other hand and the helm hanging from it by its hose.

She'd worked it out in the time it took to reach the door, as she'd work out a dose. Ilenne had taken him down the stages. The stages were slow: bell after bell, with a land man on the end of your hand, the whole of a morning to the floor. Ilenne could do them in an hour with him, perhaps, if he could brace. Imani couldn't do them at all without a pilot, and there wasn't one. But there was another way down. Bram had drawn it on Wednesday night at the table and she'd sat across from him and watched him do it: the yard stair from the big kiln yard, forty-one steps and a turn, the shutter, the chamber, the seal, and beyond the seal the wet shaft and a short run of bells straight down to the court's water. The fastest way from the Rim to the floor, for anybody on legs who could swim a shaft with a pack on her back. The furnace hands went down it every evening. The queen's guards had come up it an hour ago.

She ran.

Out through the bell, through the water door's sheet. At the foot of the forty steps the stone was wet, a long dark smear of sea water going down the wet stair toward the pool, and in it, here and there, darker spots that weren't water. She'd missed them by a minute. She didn't stop. Up the forty steps. Along the Rim's street in the last of the light with the lamps coming on and the smoke still hanging brown over the far end of the island, past people who stopped to look at a land woman running with a bag and a pack and a helm. Through the arch into the big kiln yard.

The fire was at the far end. She could see it from the arch: the racks over the flues, a long low stack of them against the back wall of the yard, black and burning, and the queen's guards and a crowd of furnace hands and buckets round it, and the smoke going up. The near end of the yard was clear. The bench's long office building on its landward side with its lamps lit. And beside it, the low door, and the bronze hatch in the floor standing open with its bar in the brackets beside it.

Nobody stopped her. Nobody was looking at the hatch. She went down it.



The stair went down steeply in the rock, lit by lamp stones in niches, dry, warm from the yard above. She counted without meaning to. Ten. Twenty. The pack banged on her hip. Twenty-five. She could smell the smoke behind her coming down the stair after her, faint.

At about thirty the lamps went out.

All of them, at once. Not dimming. Out, every one, as if a hand had been closed over them; and in the same instant the open hatch above her went white. A flare. A great flat silent glare of white light filling the square of the hatch and pouring down the stair after her, so bright that she saw her own shadow thrown down the steps ahead of her, long and black, before it faded.

Something went out of her too.

She felt it go. She couldn't have said where. The thing she used when she put her hands on someone, the warmth that went out of her palms into a body, that she never thought about any more than she thought about her eyes: gone. Not weak. Not tired. Gone, as if a door had been shut in her. She still had herself. She had her legs and her breath and the strength the stone had put in her. She didn't have anything that went outward.

She knew what it was. Every one of them did. Ward had drilled it into the whole fleet before they sailed.

A dead zone.

And below her, in the dark, at the bottom of the stair, there was a sound.

A long grinding rush of bronze in stone. Not loud. Heavy. Then a blow that came up through the steps into her feet and her knees and her teeth, a single deep boom like a door the size of a house closing; and then the sound of water, a lot of water, moving fast, and rising, and then stopping, all at once, against something.

She stood on the stair in the dark.

The flare had gone. There was a little light from the hatch above, the ordinary dusk and the red of the fire at the yard's far end, coming down the stair grey and faint. She went down the last steps toward the sound with one hand on the wall, slowly, and came to the turn, and round it, onto the landing.

The shutter was down.

It filled the stair from wall to wall and floor to ceiling, a great flat slab of bronze, a foot in front of her face. It had come down its two slots and sat on the landing's floor in a groove cut for it, and it was shut. She put her hand flat on it. It was cold. Through it, on the other side, she could hear water: the sea, filling the chamber below where the seal had stood, all the way up to the shutter's foot, and lapping there, against the bronze, very quietly, a foot away from her hand.

The seal had died. That was the water. And the shutter had done exactly what Bram had drawn it doing, and what he'd written guessed beside in letters smaller than all the others.

She thought: there's no way down.

And then, a moment after: there's no way down, and the water's on the other side of the bronze, and Zale's at the bottom of the sea with a hole in him.

Above her on the stair there was shouting.



They came down out of the flare. A dozen of them, perhaps more, the furnace hands who'd been nearest the hatch when the yard went white; she heard them before she saw them, running, crowding, shouting in their own tongue, and then they were round the turn and on the landing and on the stair above her, a press of bodies in leather aprons in the grey light, coughing.

They'd run down because down was away from it. That was all. The yard had gone white and something had been wrong with the air, and down was the way they went every evening, and they'd run. And now there was a bronze wall at the bottom with the sea behind it, and a land woman with a bag standing against it, and the smoke was coming down the stair after them.

Somebody at the back screamed something. The press on the stair surged.

"Stop," said Imani.

They couldn't understand her. They heard her. She said it again, louder, in the voice she used on the K Endeavor's deck when a man was trying to get up who shouldn't, and the ones at the front stopped and looked at her, and the press behind them stopped against their backs.

Then, far above, at the top of the stair, a heavy hollow bang.

The grey light went out.

It was dark. Not black; there was a seam of red, faint, round the edge of something at the top of the stair, a long way up. The hatch. Someone had shut the hatch.

And then a scrape, metal on metal, and a clunk. The bar.

Somebody had barred it from above.



She did what she could do, which was work.

That was all there was. Her sight was gone and her hands were only hands, and she had a bag with a lamp in it that ran on a battery and not on a stone, Tess's, which she'd sworn at for its weight every day since the wall. She got it out and lit it. It lit. White, small, ordinary. The furnace hands on the stair turned their faces to it and away.

The smoke was coming down. It was in the stair now, thin, sour, from the yard; it had come down before the hatch shut and it hung under the low ceiling and crept down the steps. Smoke rose. She knew that as she knew which way blood ran. The best air on this stair was at the bottom of it. Here. At the shutter, with the cold sea a foot behind the bronze cooling it.

"Down," she said. "Sit down. Low. Here. All of you." She showed them with her hands, palms down, pressing. "Sit."

They sat. Not all at once; the first two, who'd been watching her, and then the next, and then the rest, crammed onto the landing and the bottom steps in the lamplight, low, with their backs to the rock and their aprons over their mouths, coughing. A boy near the top kept standing until the man next to him pulled him down by his apron.

She went among them on her knees.

Burns first. Three had them, from the flare or from the racks before it: hands and forearms, one man's whole left side of his face reddened and blistering. She had dressings. She had the jar of the salve Bettany made and the Rim had laughed at. She dressed them by the battery lamp, quick and close, with her own hands, and the old feeling of reaching for the warmth in her palms came every time, and every time there wasn't any, and every time she let it go and used the dressing.

Airways next. A heavy old man near the shutter was breathing badly, wheezing, his lips gone dark. Not burned. He'd breathed the yard's smoke, or he'd breathed it all his life and tonight was one night too many. She sat him up against the bronze where the air was coolest and put his head forward and held it there and talked to him, and he looked at her as if she were mad, and breathed a little better.

A man with a gash on his scalp from the stair. A woman with a twisted knee. A boy who'd done nothing to himself but was crying, quietly, with his hands over his face.

She knelt beside him. He was perhaps eighteen, perhaps less, narrow, in a furnace hand's apron too big for him, with soot all over his face and the tears making lines through it.

"Hurt?" she said. One of the station's words.

He shook his head.

"Name?"

He stared at her.

"Dauvre," he said.

He said something else. Then he made a motion with his hands, rowing, and pointed up, and out, toward where the sea would be beyond the rock; and then, with his hand, a woman's height, and touched his own chest.

"Your mother," said Imani. "In the boats."

He didn't understand the words. He understood her face. He nodded.

"Watch," he said, in Kaldo, carefully. It must have been one of the dozen words of it anyone on the Rim had. "Watch. Boat."

"Your mother's on the watch's boats." She put her hand on his shoulder, flat, the station's word for here, I'm here. "She's outside. She's on the water. She's all right." She didn't know that. She said it anyway. "We'll get out."

He looked at her hand on his shoulder.

The smoke was thicker. She could taste it at the back of her throat. Above them, a long way up in the dark, round the edge of the barred hatch, the seam of red light was getting brighter.



Ferra had been in the bench's office when the yard went white.

She'd been there since four, at the long table in the clerks' room on the landward side of the yard, with the bench's two senior clerks and their slates and Bram's steel on a cloth between them and her book open, for the third time that week, arguing about whether a bar with Kaldo's mark on it could be tested by a bench that didn't know the mark. The clerks had been courteous. They'd been courteous for three days. She'd been about to get somewhere, she thought.

She'd heard the shot. Everyone in the yard had: a flat enormous crack from somewhere above them on the island's spine, and the deep thump under it that she knew from the range at home and had never thought to hear here. The clerks had looked up. She'd gone cold. Two minutes after it Linnet's voice had come out of the field set on her belt, small, for anyone of Kaldo's who was listening: Zale shot on the breakwater, Ilenne had taken him down. And Imani's, answering: going. Down. Ferra had stood at the clerks' window, and a minute later she'd seen Imani come in through the yard's arch at a run with her bag and a gill pack and go straight across the near end of the yard to the open hatch and down it, and she'd known exactly where Imani was going and why, and there'd been nothing in the world she could do about it but stand there.

Then the yard went white.

The flare came in through every window at once.

White. Flat, enormous, silent, filling the room, throwing the clerks' shadows across the far wall and the shelves; and the lamp stones in their brackets along the walls went out with it, all of them, at the same instant. The two clerks were on their feet. One of them dropped his slate.

And Ferra felt it go out of her.

Not her strength. Her hands on the table were her hands, and her legs were her legs, and when she stood up they did what she told them as they always did. It was the yard. For three days she'd sat in that room with the hum of the yard's furnace stones coming through the floor, the low steady note of thirty banks of fire stones working, so constant that she'd stopped hearing it on the first afternoon. She heard it stop. All at once, in the same instant as the lamps, a silence under everything, as if the building had let out its breath.

She stood very still for about the space of a breath.

A dead zone. Over the yard.

Then she did three things, in order, and she knew while she was doing them that she'd put them in that order in her head a long time ago, on the voyage, at night, without ever being asked to.

The first was the people.

She went out of the office into the yard. It was full of noise and smoke and running. The fire at the racks was still burning; fire did, in a zone; it was only fire. The ordinary dusk light was still there, and the red of the flames, and by them she could see the yard: furnace hands running from the far end in every direction, some toward the arch, some toward the hatch, the queen's guards at the racks with their bidents, shouting, and the big furnaces along the yard's side with their stone-fed fires gone dead and dark and smoking. Nobody knew which way to go. A crowd at the arch was pushing in as another crowd was trying to push out.

And behind the kilns, at the gap in the wall where the yard backed onto the small yards behind it, a woman was standing on an upturned crate with her arms over her head, shouting.

Neris.

Ferra knew her. Everyone in Kaldo's party knew her by now: the woman who ran the little yard behind the kilns with the three small furnaces and the long trough, whom Bram went to see every afternoon at four and came back from talking about her trough. Neris was on her crate in the gap in the wall, shouting and waving both arms, the same motion over and over: this way. Here. Through here.

Ferra went to her across the yard through the smoke and the running.

"Where?" she said, at the foot of the crate.

Neris understood her well enough. She pointed down. Behind her, in her own small yard, a black doorway in the rock at the back of her kilns, and steps going down into it. The kiln cellars. Ferra had heard Bram talk about them. The Rim's kilns sat over cellars cut in the rock where the ash was raked and the firewood was kept dry, and the cellars ran from one yard to the next under the walls, and came out, Neris was saying with her hands, there, and there, and there: on the far side of the island. At the water stairs. Away from the arch, and the crush, and the smoke.

"Show them," said Ferra.

Neris showed them. She got down off her crate and went to the gap and started taking furnace hands by the shoulders as they came past and turning them, and pushing them through the gap toward the black doorway; and Ferra stood in the gap beside her and did the same, with her book in one hand and her other hand on people's backs, and after a minute two of the queen's guards came across from the racks and saw what they were doing and started doing it too. The crowd at the arch thinned. People went down into the cellars in a stream and didn't come back.

On her way back across the yard to the office she passed the hatch.

It was shut. It had been open when Imani went down it, a few minutes ago. Now it was down, flat, and the bar was across it in its brackets; and two men in working coats were walking away from it toward the furnaces, not fast, not looking back. She saw them, and saw the bar, and understood it, and put it in the order with the rest. There were people to move first and a call to make, and she made the call.

The second thing was Ward.

She took the field set off her belt. It was a plain thing, a box and a battery and a coil, no stone in it anywhere; the field sets had been built that way on purpose, because the layer rule said so. She keyed it in the dark at the cellar mouth with the smoke going over her head.

"Linnet. Ferra."

"Linnet." Loud and clear. The set didn't care about the zone. "Ferra, where are you, we've lost..."

"Listen. For Ward. Now." She said it in the order she'd decided. "Dead zone over the big kiln yard by the geyser. About six minutes after the shot. The yard's lamps are dead and the furnaces are dead. The fire's still burning. The yard stair's hatch is shut and the shutter at the bottom has dropped; I heard it. There are people on that stair." She stopped. "I don't know how many. I think Imani's on it. She went down it after Zale."

Linnet was quiet for about a second.

"Dead zone, big kiln yard," she said. "Ten past the shot. Hatch shut, shutter down, people on the stair, Imani maybe. For Ward." Her voice didn't shake. "I'm sending it."



The third thing was the books.

She went back into the bench's office. The two clerks were still there in the dark, among the shelves, with the white of the flare gone and the red of the fire coming in through the windows. They'd been about to leave. She could see it on them. And on the shelves behind them, in their long rows, the bench's books: the tally-stones and the slates and the bound hides, the record of every bar and casting and test that bench had passed in a hundred years. And the furnace's casting books, the yard's own, in their own case at the end, which Ferra had been refused a look at on Thursday.

A fire in the yard. A dead zone. And nobody in the yard who'd be in any hurry to carry out the bench's books, because the bench was the house's bench, and the house's men had set the fire.

Ferra didn't know that. She thought it, clearly, standing in the dark office, and didn't let herself think it again.

"Carry them," she said.

The clerks stared at her.

"Carry them," said Ferra, and took the nearest armful of slates off the nearest shelf herself, and put them into the senior clerk's arms, and turned him by the shoulder toward the door. "Out. Neris's yard. The cellars. Out." She took a second armful and gave it to the other one. "If the fire comes in here, these are gone, and nobody in the world will ever know what this bench passed or why. Carry them."

They carried them. She didn't know how much they understood. They understood the slates in their arms and the fire at the window. They went, and came back, and went again; and when the shelves were nearly bare Ferra took the furnace's casting books in their case into her own arms, to carry them out herself.

She came out of the office with the casting books' case in her arms for Neris, and the hatch was in front of her across the cobbles: shut, flat, with the bar across it, and nobody near it now.

She stopped.

Then she put the casting books down on the cobbles and went to it.

There were people under it. Furnace hands. She'd seen a dozen of them go down it when the yard went white, running. And the shutter at the bottom was down, and the smoke had gone down the stair after them before somebody shut the hatch on it, and now the stair was a closed box of rock with people in it and smoke in it and no air coming in, and no way out at the bottom, and none at the top.

And Imani. She didn't know. She thought so.

She got her hands on the bar.

It was heavy, a bronze bar as long as she was tall and thick as her wrist, sitting in two deep brackets bolted into the stone either side of the hatch. Barred from above. Whoever had done it had done it properly. She got her shoulder under one end and lifted, and her strength was her own, all of it, the zone couldn't touch that; and the bar came up out of its first bracket with a screech, and she walked it round, slowly, with the end on her shoulder, to get the other end out.

She had it half off when they came back.

Two men, out of the smoke at the far side of the furnaces, walking fast. Rim men on their legs in working coats, ordinary. They each had a spear: not a bident, a long plain fisherman's spear with an iron head. She saw them see her. She saw them see the bar on her shoulder and the hatch, and understood in the same instant why they'd come back. A stair that breathed was a stair people came up. Somebody had wanted that stair shut and kept shut, and these two were the somebody, and they'd come back to make sure.

The bar was on her shoulder. One end was still in its bracket.

The first man came at her.

He didn't hesitate. He came round the side of the hatch fast, low, with the spear held two-handed and the iron head at her middle, and he was going to put it through her and she knew it.

She let the bar go.

It fell off her shoulder with a crash onto the hatch, and the end still in its bracket jumped out with the jolt, and the whole bar rolled off the bronze onto the cobbles; and her right hand was at her left hip, and the revolver came out of its holster into her hand as she'd been made to do it over and over on the range at home, cross-draw, thumb on the hammer as it came up; and she didn't think about it. She didn't decide. Her body did what it had been taught. The spear's head was four feet from her and coming.

She fired.

The sound was enormous. Bigger than anything. It went off the furnace walls and the rock and came back at her. The man jerked, and kept coming, and she fired again, and he staggered and still came, the spear's head dropping and lunging at her middle as he fell forward onto it; and she fired a third time, into him, as he came, a yard off. He stopped at the edge of the hatch with the spear still in his hands and his face very surprised. Then he went down, sideways, onto the bronze, and the spear clattered away across it.

The second man was there.

He'd stopped at the sound. He'd stopped dead, three yards off, with his spear up, staring at her and at the gun and at the man on the hatch; and then he came on. She saw him decide to. She swung the revolver toward him and her hand wasn't steady any more, and she knew it wasn't, and the spear was coming.

The hatch moved.

It came up under the dead man and the fallen bar, one edge, a hand's width, heaving, as something underneath it shoved up hard; the bar slid off it with a clang; and the hatch came up and over and back with a crash, and the dead man rolled off it onto the cobbles, and smoke came pouring up out of the square black hole in a column, grey, thick; and out of the smoke came Imani.

She came up the last steps on her hands and feet, coughing, with soot on her face and her bag on her back and her revolver in her hand, and she saw the second man with the spear, and she didn't stop. She came up out of the hatch onto one knee on its lip, and fired twice.

The man went down on his side with a shout, holding his hip.

The spear rolled away.

And Imani put her revolver down on the cobbles beside her, carefully, and went across to him on her knees through the smoke, and put her hands on him.



They came up out of the stair behind her. Ferra stood by the hatch with the revolver hanging in her hand and watched them come: the furnace hands, one after another, out of the smoke, coughing, blackened, a man with his face dressed in white, a woman holding her knee, a boy with soot on his face. Up the stair into the yard. Out into the air, such as it was. The smoke came up with them and thinned and went off sideways over the furnaces, and the stair below breathed.

Thirteen. She counted them. She didn't mean to. Imani said there was one more below, an old man sitting against the shutter who couldn't climb, and that somebody would have to go down for him.

Imani was kneeling over the second man in the smoke at the edge of the hatch with her bag open beside her, and her hands were on his hip, pressing, and she was talking to him. In Kaldo. Steadily, the way she talked to anybody she had her hands on, whatever they'd done: you're all right, lie still, it's the hip, it's not your belly, lie still. He was lying still. He was grey and staring up at her and holding his breath against the pain and doing as she said.

Her healing was gone. Ferra knew that. Imani was doing it with a dressing and her two hands and her weight on the wound, as anyone could, as a farm wife would. It would have to be enough until they were out of the zone. She looked up once at Ferra and down again.

"Name," said Imani to the man. One of the station's words. "Name."

The man looked at her. His lips moved.

"Keddrin," he said.

"Keddrin," said Imani. "Lie still, Keddrin."

The first man lay on the cobbles on his back a yard from the hatch with his eyes open and the fire's light on his face. Ferra didn't go to him. Imani had looked at him once, as she came up out of the hatch, before the second man; Ferra had seen her look, and look away, and go to the one who was alive.

Ferra stood there.

Her thumb had found the cylinder. She didn't remember putting it there. It was going round the chambers by itself, telling beads. One, spent, the case still warm. Two, spent. Three, spent. Four, five, six, live. She took her thumb off it.

She put it back on and counted it again. One, two, three. Spent. Four, five, six.

Three.

Neither of them said anything about it.

The yard was still silent under the noise. That was the thing she kept noticing, a tongue going to a broken tooth: no hum. Thirty banks of stones under her feet, dead, and Imani ten feet away kneeling in the smoke with her hands on a man's hip and her hair coming down, doing it the slow way.

And then, very far off, from the west, there was a sound.

It was low at first. So low that Ferra thought it was in her own ears, from the shots. Then it wasn't. It came in over the sea from the west, from a long way out, past the ring and the gate and the open water beyond, a long rising roar: deep, steady, getting louder, getting higher, like a waterfall coming toward you. Like nothing on the Rim. People in the yard were stopping and turning their heads to it, the furnace hands and the queen's guards and the clerks with their arms full of slates, all of them looking west into the dark over the furnaces where the sound was coming from, and none of them knowing what it was.

Ferra knew what it was.

She'd heard it once in her life, in the yard at home, on the test stand, the day they first lit the K Amara's cans with the doors open and the whole of the Works came out to listen.

The burn. The K Amara's cans, lit, every one of them, the afterburners, the thing nobody on the Rim had ever heard of, because nobody had ever had any reason to use it here. Thirty miles out at the Grey Mark she'd have come up off her anchor and lit them, and you could hear her from here. You could hear her coming.

Ferra stood by the open hatch with the revolver in her hand and three empty chambers under her thumb and listened to it come, and knew exactly what it meant.

The stone was coming in.

And Emeric had said, in the queen's dry hall, in front of the whole court, that it wouldn't. That if it did, it was his.
