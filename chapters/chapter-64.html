Chapter 64 - A Name That Points Up

It took Ferra the better part of a month to walk the money backward, and she walked it the only way it could be walked, which was slowly and sideways and never in a straight line, because the men who had built the trail had built it to defeat exactly the straight look. A factor did not keep a ledger that said this fortune went from Renner to a surveyor at the request of the following person. A factor kept a ledger that said an account received a sum and an account disbursed a sum, and the two accounts wore initials, and the initials answered to standing arrangements that lived in the factor's head and nowhere on paper, and the whole apparatus existed to ensure that if the box were ever opened, as this box had been opened, it would yield a chain of hands that went in a circle. Ferra did not try to break the circle by force. She had learned from watching Emeric read a hillside that you do not fight the shape of a thing, you read the shape and let it tell you what made it, and the shape of this money told her something the money itself never said, which was that the same shape had touched Kaldo before.

The factor who had moved Renner's fee was not the wool-broker who had taken Halloran's letters. She checked that first and made sure of it, because the easy error was to see one method and assume one man, and Emeric had drilled into her that a method is cheaper to copy than a man is to place, and two thieves using the same lock does not make them the same thief. Different name, different house of business, different corner of the capital's paper trade. But the two factors, the one from years ago and the one from last season, cleared their accounts through the same handful of standing arrangements, drew on the same short list of unnamed backers, moved in the same small closed water the way two fish caught a year apart in the same pond are not the same fish but are, unmistakably, the same pond. She could not name the pond. She could name that it was one pond.

And once, at the far edge of the trail, where the initials thinned and the standing arrangements narrowed toward whatever they narrowed toward, a name surfaced that was a name and not an initial, a handler, a man who took instructions from above and passed money and questions below and was himself neither the top nor the bottom of anything. Marrow. The name meant nothing to Ferra and she brought it to Emeric flat, without weight, because she did not know it carried any, and Emeric took it and turned it over and found that it meant nothing to him either, which was itself the meaning. A man who surfaced this deep in a trail this well-built, and whom Emeric had never heard of, was not a principal. A principal would have taken care never to surface at all, the way the hand that mattered in Halloran's letters had stayed off the page entirely. A name you can reach is a name someone above decided was reachable, a layer of the thing that its makers had accepted might one day be found, precisely because finding it told you nothing you could climb from. Marrow was a rung. The ladder went up past him into dark, and the dark was built to stay dark.

"He points up," Emeric said. "That is all he does. He is placed there to point up and to be a wall while he does it, so that a man who reaches him spends the rest of his life reaching the next thing and the next and never touches the top, because there are always more rungs than there is life. I have met the type. I have, God help me, built the type, I have men in my own house whose whole value is that they know one layer up and one layer down and nothing sideways, so that catching them catches a rung and not a ladder. This is my own machine, turned around. I am looking at the back of my own machine."

What he could not do, and he sat with the not-doing until it was solid in him, was tell whether the ladder Marrow stood on was Aldane's ladder or the older one. He had two enemies certain, the duke whose questions Halloran had carried, quiet and close and personal, a man of the court with a court's reasons, and behind everything, older than Kaldo, the patient backed hand that had reached for a gifted boy and might be reaching still. He had walked the survey money back expecting it to name the one or the other, to close the frightening question the dead man's box had opened, are these two hands or one. It named neither. It named a handler on a rung, standing at a place where the duke's local grievance and the old buried threat both could have put him, and Emeric stood at the bottom of the ladder looking up into dark that could have been either sky and could, worst of all, have been both, the two of them not separate ladders at all but one ladder he was seeing from below and reading as two because he wanted, badly, for his enemies to be countable.

That was the wanting he had to put down, and he knew it, and putting it down was harder than any survey. A man fighting one enemy can plan. He can read the enemy's weight and grade and build against it, the way he had read Aldane and walled the lawful road. A man fighting two enemies who might be one, or one who might be two, cannot plan, because every move he makes against the near threat might be feeding the far one, and every wall he raises against the far one might leave the near one an open gate. He had come to Kaldo to get off the board and had spent two years telling himself the board could not reach him here, and the survey in the dead man's box, and the factor, and the pond, and the handler named Marrow pointing up into buildable dark, were all one long letter telling him he had never been off the board at all. He had merely been, for two years, a piece the players left alone while they finished other business, and the crystal, the great windfall, the thing that made everything possible, had also been the thing that turned the players back toward him, because a piece that is winning is a piece the game notices.

He did not go to the crown with Marrow. There was nothing to take, a handler's name off a factor's edge, a survey years old, a shape that matched a shape, none of it a thing a magistrate could hold, all of it the same walled road he had walked with Halloran and found ended exactly where he had built its end. He filed the name instead, in the locked book, next to the wool-broker and the shape of the questions, and he understood that filing it was all he could do and that filing it changed the war he thought he was in. He had believed he was defending a barony against a jealous duke. He was, he now saw, standing at the foot of something taller than a duke, holding one rung, unable to prove the rung led anywhere, unable to disbelieve that it led everywhere, and forced, for now, to fight the near enemy he could see while the far one, if it was two, or the whole of it, if it was one, watched him do it from a height he could not yet measure.
