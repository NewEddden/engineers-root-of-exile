Book 2, Chapter 13: Sael

Bram had built the seismograph in hull four's workshop on the run west, in the evenings, out of a cracked flywheel, a length of piano wire, two clock springs Tess had packed for him without being asked and a drum off a broken barometer, and by the Wednesday morning it had been sitting in a crate on the K Endeavor's deck for nine days with nowhere to stand.

That was the trouble with a seismograph. It was no use on a ship. A ship moved; that was what ships were for. You wanted rock under it, old rock, the oldest you could find, rock that hadn't shifted since the world was made, and you wanted it on a foot that wouldn't ring or creep, and you wanted it where nobody would kick it. He'd said all that to Emeric on the run west and Emeric had said, find it a place, then, and Bram had said he would. He'd been saying it for nine days.

So on the Wednesday he went to find it a place.

He went up out of the hard rooms at eight with the sky going pale over the breakwater, and Ilenne with him to talk for him, because there was nobody else; and Zale, because Ilenne was going; and Emeric behind them all, because, he said, he'd been in a dry room under the sea for six days and wanted to see a street. Armand came too. Armand said he was going to buy things. Bram didn't ask what.

The city they walked out into was called Sael.

Ilenne said it on the landing, the first time any of them had heard it. They'd been calling it the Rim for a fortnight because that was what she'd always called it, and that was what the court called it, and it turned out the court's word was the court's. The people who lived on it called it Sael. It meant the dry, Ilenne said, or the dry place, the part that was out of the water; and when Bram asked whether that was a compliment she thought about it for a while and said it depended who was saying it.

It was a working town. Bram knew it at once, the way you know a yard by the noise of it from the gate. Nobody here had built anything for show.

Sael was a ring of black islands joined by causeways, and on every island there was something being made. Kilns, long low brick ones, three and four together, with smoke going up from them straight into the still morning, and the smell of hot clay and something sharper behind it. A rope-walk, a quarter of a mile of it, along the inside of the breakwater, with men and women walking backwards down it on their legs spinning a thick pale cord out of something that wasn't hemp, and a child at the far end turning the wheel. Long racks of kelp drying in the sun on the flat tops of the islands, rows and rows of it, brown and black and shiny, like laundry hung out by somebody who did a great deal of laundry. Boat-sheds. A dry-dock cut into the rock with a hull in it that wasn't a hull, a long sled-shaped thing of bent wood and hide that a dozen people were sewing. And behind the biggest of the kiln yards, coming up out of the rock in a pipe as thick as a man, steam.

"Geyser," said Emeric, at his elbow.

"I can see it's a geyser."

"They've capped it. Look. They've piped it to the kilns." Emeric was looking at it with his checking face, the one for a page he'd already read. "That's the heat for the whole yard. They don't burn anything."

Bram looked at the pipe. It was clay, glazed black, in sections, with collars of some grey stuff at the joints, and it ran from the capped vent across the yard to the kiln mouths in a line that somebody had laid with a string. Every joint was clean. Not one of them wept.

"What's that at the joints?"

"I don't know," said Emeric.

Bram turned and looked at him.

"I don't," said Emeric. "Ask."

Bram asked. Ilenne asked for him, of a man on his legs at the nearest kiln with a rake, who looked at the three land men and the queen's daughter with no great interest, and said something short. It was ash, Ilenne said. Ash from the mountain, burned and ground with lime and sea. It set in the water. It set harder in the water than in the air.

Bram took his notebook out and put it in, and underlined harder, and thought he'd be asking a great deal more about it before they left.

The crafters were everywhere, and none of them stopped work. They were merfolk on their legs, as Ilenne was on her legs, and they walked and stood and knelt and carried just as anybody did; and they looked at the land people going past, and then went back to what they were doing, and that was all. Bram liked them for it at once. Ilenne said they were Sael-born. She said it in a careful voice that Bram had heard Ferra use for a man who was more important than his coat, and he asked her what it meant.

"Born up here," said Ilenne. "Not Below. The great houses live Below. Up here is the work." She hesitated. "At court it's a little bit an insult."

"Is it, up here?"

Ilenne looked at the man with the rake, who was looking at them.

"No," she said. "Up here, nobody cares what they say at court."



The glassmakers' yard was on the next island along, and it was the first place in Sael that stopped work when they came in.

They'd seen the Challenger. That was why. They'd seen her at the gate on the Thursday with her nose out of the water, and two of them had come down to the steps that afternoon in a boat and sat in it ten yards off her bow and looked at her glass for an hour without a word, Ilenne said; she'd heard about it from her mother's people. They knew what it was. So when the land men came in under the yard's arch, the glassmakers knew who they were too, and they put down their pipes.

There were women among them. That was the first thing Bram noticed. The yard's head was a woman, broad and short, with burn scars up both forearms to the elbow in little shining patches, and a leather apron, and grey hair cropped close. She came across the yard to them wiping her hands, and looked at Bram, and then at the others, and then back at Bram, having decided which of them she'd be talking to.

Bram told her what he wanted. A foot. For a machine that listened to the rock. Something that sat on stone and didn't ring and didn't creep, that he could bed the machine's frame in. He said it plainly and Ilenne said it after him, and the woman listened with her head on one side.

Then she asked about the Challenger.

She asked politely. She said, through Ilenne, that they'd seen the land ship's nose. Three domes, one inside the other, cast and cooled over a long time. She said they could see it had been done with great care. She said it was very good for land glass.

Then she said what it was. And she said it kindly, which was worse.

"She says it's held glass," said Ilenne. "What ours is. But not held." She listened. "She says, like a child's drawing of a house. All the right parts. The house won't stand. She says it's the start of the right thing. She says whoever made it was close."

Bram stood in the yard with his hands in his coat pockets.

He hadn't made it. Tess had made it, in the shed on the Cradle, over the best part of a season, with the furnace going night and day and Bram not allowed in after the first fortnight because he fussed. Tess had stayed home. Tess had said, the night before they sailed, sitting on the end of her bench with her stick across her knees, that she'd have given a year of her life to stand in their glass yard for one afternoon and ask them things, and since she couldn't, he was going to; and she'd given him a notebook.

He took it out now. It was a small one, cloth-bound, with her name on the front in her square capitals and the first nine pages full. Questions. Numbered. In order. Some of them went on for half a page. Some of them were one line.

"I didn't make it," he said. "My girl did. She's at home. She sent these." He held the notebook up. "I'm to ask every one. She said if I left any out she'd know."

Ilenne put it over. The woman looked at the notebook. Then she pulled up a stool from beside the nearest furnace, and sat on it, and pointed at a second stool, and Bram sat down.

He asked every one.

It took the morning. He read them out in Tess's order and Ilenne put each into her own speech, and the woman answered, and called others over when she didn't know or when somebody knew better, until there were six of them round the two stools in the heat of the furnace. How much dust in the glass and how fine. Whether the dust went in the melt or after. How they keyed it. How long they cooled it, and in what, and whether they turned it. What made it go green. What made the old panes go brown. Why the ribs were stone and not glass. Some of the questions Bram didn't understand himself, and said so, and the woman seemed to like that.

And some of them were the stupid ones.

Tess had marked them. She'd put a small star beside each one in the margin and written at the top of the first page, The starred ones are stupid. Ask them anyway. You learn most from the stupid ones because nobody bothers to answer them. So he asked them. Does it ring if you tap it. Is it warm or cold to touch. Can you see through it at night. What do you call it when it breaks.

They answered the stupid ones best.

They answered them with their hands. Does it ring: one of the younger men fetched an offcut of held glass the size of a book and gave Bram a little hammer, and Bram tapped it, and it didn't ring; it went tk, dead, like tapping a stone. Ordinary glass sang. Held glass didn't. The dust took the singing out of it. Is it warm or cold: the woman put his hand flat on it and it was warmer than it should have been. What do you call it when it breaks. That one took a while. Then the woman said a word, and the others looked at the floor, and Ilenne didn't put it into Kaldo; she said, it's the word the Keeper said on the stair. You don't say it in a dry room. This isn't a dry room, so she said it.

Bram wrote down everything, in Tess's notebook, under each question, in his own big slanting hand. By noon he'd filled four more pages and his hand ached.

"And the foot," he said, at the end, when there was nothing left in the notebook to ask.

The woman looked at him. Then she said something to the young man with the hammer, and he went away into the back of the yard and came back carrying a thing in both arms: a block of held glass, flat on top and bottom, a foot and a half across and four inches thick, greenish, with the fine grain in it like sand caught in ice. A dome's footing stone, Ilenne said, for one of the small hard rooms. It had a flaw in one corner and they'd never set it. It wouldn't ring. It wouldn't creep. It would sit on rock till the end of the world.

"What does she want for it?"

Ilenne asked. The woman said something and the others laughed.

"She says the answer to the last question," said Ilenne. "Your girl's. She asks what the girl will do with all this."

Bram thought about it.

"Make it better," he said. "And then send her a letter saying how."

The woman listened to that put into her own speech, and nodded slowly. A fair price; she'd been expecting worse.

Armand had been buying things the whole time.

Bram hadn't noticed at first. He'd been too busy. But when he came out of the furnace shed into the sun with his four pages and his aching hand, Armand was standing at a trestle at the yard's edge where somebody's wife sold the yard's small work to whoever came, and had three things laid out in front of him on a cloth, and his little notebook open, and was writing.

An offcut of held glass, the size of his palm, with the grain showing. A float, a ball of thin green glass in a net of cord, the kind the fishermen used. And a child's toy: a string of glass beads on a stick, coloured, blue and green and a clear one, that slid up and down and clicked.

He was writing them up one by one, as he'd written up every piece in the collection at home since Emeric gave him the keys to it. Bram looked over his shoulder. Held glass, offcut. The glassmakers' yard on the second island west of the gate, Sael. From the yard. Two fingers of Kaldo iron. Armand's book. And under it, the float, the same way. And under that, the toy.

The woman at the trestle was watching him write with an expression Bram had seen before, on Bram's own face, at the castle, when Armand wrote things.

She said something to Ilenne. Ilenne put it over, with a straight face.

"She asks what a dry man wants with a toy."

Armand finished the line he was writing before he looked up.

"Tell her," he said, "that it's for a child who's never seen the sea. So that she knows another child played with it."

Ilenne told her. The woman looked at Armand for a while. Then she looked at the toy, and back at him, and said something else that made Ilenne's mouth twitch.

"She says you're very young to be so serious. She says you're thirty. Perhaps."

"Tell her I'm past ninety."

Ilenne told her. The woman laughed out loud, and said something to the man beside her, who laughed too, and she flapped a hand at Armand as you'd flap it at a boy who'd told a lie so big it was a kind of compliment. She didn't believe a word of it. Then she reached under the trestle and brought out another toy, the same as the first but with red beads, and put it down on the cloth beside the other one, and pushed both across to him, and wouldn't take any iron for it.

Armand wrote that one up too. He wrote it last, at the bottom, in a hand Bram didn't see him use often. Given. She didn't believe my age.



The salvage quay was on the inside of the ring, on the island next to the pilots' hall, and they came to it at two in the afternoon because Bram wanted to see the rock there and Ilenne said the quay was built straight on the caldera's wall.

It was. It was a long flat shelf of black stone at the water's edge with cranes on it, wooden ones, and heaps of things the sea had given back: anchors, chain gone to rust, a figurehead with no face, timbers, a pile of green-crusted metal. Merfolk on their legs were sorting the heaps with long rakes and arguing. Out on the water beside the quay a man sat in a small boat with a line going down from it into the deep.

Bram was looking at the rock. He was on his knees on the quay's edge with his hand flat on it, feeling for anything, a hum, a give, the way you'd feel a beam for rot. It was good rock. It was very old. But it was the inside wall, and the vents were on the inside, and the water here was warm; and he was thinking that he didn't want it on the inside, when the line from the boat came tight and the water beside the quay went up.

Something came out of it. A back. A long grey-brown back, smooth and wet, like a seal's back but the size of a hay cart, rolling up out of the green water and going down again, and then a head on a thick neck, blunt, whiskered, with small dark eyes and no ears that Bram could see, and round the neck and under the forelegs a harness of hide and cord with the line going up from it to the boat. It blew. The breath went over the quay like a wet wind and smelled of fish and the bottom of the sea. Then it put its forelegs on the edge of the quay, which bowed under it, and dropped something out of its mouth onto the stone.

A bronze. Old. Green. A bell, Bram thought, or the half of one, a great cracked green-black thing the width of a barrel, crusted all over with shell.

The man in the boat was talking to it.

He'd stood up in the boat and was leaning out with one hand on the line, and he was talking. Not to the people on the quay. To the animal. In a voice that went up and down and clicked at the back, a voice that wasn't quite speech, low and steady, the voice you'd use to a horse that had done well; and the animal turned its blunt head toward him and blew again, softly, and stayed where it was with its forelegs on the quay while the sorters ran up with their rakes and dragged the bronze back from the edge.

"Oddrin," said Ilenne. "That's his name. He takes things up for money." She watched the animal with an odd expression. "He talks to it. Some of us can. It goes where nobody goes. Down the wall, a long way. Further than the guards."

Bram looked at the animal, and at the man in the boat talking it down off the quay into the water again, gently, and the line going slack as it went.

He thought: I'd like one of those.

He thought it very clearly. Not for anything. Just to have. Something that went down where nobody could and brought back what it found and you talked to it. He thought about Tess's face if he came home with one. He thought about the K Endeavor's cook's face. He let himself enjoy both for a moment and then he got up off his knees.

The pilots' hall was next to the quay, a long low building of black stone with its doors open, and on the outside wall beside the doors there was a board. A long board of slate, painted over in rows of marks, white and yellow, some fresh, some faded; and people coming off the quay stopped in front of it and read it, the way men stop at a notice on a harbour wall to see what's closed.

"What's that?"

"The watch's," said Ilenne. "What's dangerous in the water. Where. So whoever works the water knows."

Bram looked at it. He couldn't read a mark of it. It was rows of little cut shapes like the ones on Ressar's stones, and it meant nothing to him at all, and he wasn't interested in it. He'd seen what he'd come to see. The rock here was the wrong rock.

"Not the inside," he said to Emeric. "It's too near the vents. I'd be listening to the kilns."

"Where, then?"

"Outside. On the old wall. Where the watch keeps its marks." He looked across the ring at the long black curve of the breakwater on the far side, where Ilenne had pointed out the watch's fixed stones on Monday, the ones they read the water's height off. "That's the oldest rock in the place. The stable wall, they call it. That's where I want it."

"That's Taurre's," said Ilenne.

"Then I'll ask Taurre," said Bram.



The smiths were in the foreign quarter, which was two streets on the north island behind a wall, and Bram would have walked past them if Emeric hadn't stopped.

There were two of them. They sat in the open front of a stone house under a hide awning, at a table, with a ledger and a set of scales and a tray of small bright things that turned out to be nails, and they weren't merfolk. Bram knew that before he'd properly looked. They were short, shorter than he was by a head, and broad, broader than he was, which was saying something; and they had beards, both of them, long and thick and plaited, one red-brown and one iron-grey, with rings worked into the plaits; and they had hands. Bram looked at their hands. They had hands like his.

They looked at him.

They looked at his beard, which he'd let go on the run west because there'd been nobody to tell him not to. They looked at his hands, which were on the edge of their table, the knuckles scarred and the nails broken, a smith's hands, a mason's, a man's who'd worked iron all his life. They looked at the top of his head, and then at the bottom of him, and then up again, measuring. And then they went quiet.

It wasn't a quiet Bram knew. It wasn't hostile. It was something else, a stillness in the two of them all at once that went on and on, as if he'd said something in their own language that they hadn't expected a land man to know. The red-brown one put down his pen.

Bram looked round for help.

Emeric was behind him. Emeric was looking at the two smiths over Bram's shoulder, and something was going across his face that Bram had never seen there. Not surprise. Recognition. A private, delighted, slightly appalled recognition, as if he'd opened a book he'd read as a boy and found it was true; and then he'd put it away, and his face was ordinary again, and he caught Bram looking and gave nothing back at all.

"What?" said Bram, low.

"Nothing," said Emeric. "They like your beard."

That wasn't it. Bram knew it wasn't it. He'd known Emeric long enough to tell when he was being told a true thing that wasn't the thing. But the iron-grey one had begun to speak, slowly, in the merfolk tongue with a heavy rolling sound in it, and Ilenne was putting it over, and it was a question.

"He asks," said Ilenne, "what the iron cost."

"What iron?"

"The iron your Ferra pays with. At the gate. The bars." Ilenne listened. "He says they hear. Everybody hears. A land woman pays a pilot in iron bars like bread. He asks where you get iron like that. What it costs you."

Bram knew what it cost. He knew it to the shilling and the ton. He knew how many cartloads of coal and ore went into the yards at Grayrock and how many men, and what Ferra paid them, and how much came out the other end as bar, and how long it took, and that it was less, every month, than it had been the month before. He could have told them in about four sentences. He looked at their faces.

He kept it behind his teeth. So did Emeric, behind him. So did Zale, or Armand, or Ilenne, who knew perfectly well what the smiths were really asking, because the whole Rim was asking it: not what the iron cost, but how much of it there was, and what it would do to the price of everything theirs.

The silence went on for a while. Then the red-brown smith picked up his pen again, and nodded. The silence had been an answer, and they'd taken it.



The foot came over on the evening boat on the Wednesday, wrapped in kelp sacking, with the young glassmaker sitting beside it holding it steady, and Bram had the seismograph out of its crate on the K Endeavor's deck and bolted to it by the time the light went, and on the Thursday morning at seven he took the whole thing across the ring in a watch boat to the breakwater's far side to ask Taurre.

Taurre was there already. He was on the breakwater's crown where the fixed stones were: a row of square-cut black blocks set into the oldest part of the wall at intervals, each with a mark cut in its top and a bronze pin in the mark, that the watch read the water and the wall by. He stood by the nearest of them with his slate and his colour level and watched Bram's boat come in.

Bram told him what it was. He told it plainly, and Ilenne put it over, and he showed him: the frame, the heavy wheel hung on its wire, the arm and the pen, the drum on its clock that turned once an hour, the paper on the drum. A thing that listened to the rock and wrote down what it heard, so you could see it afterwards. He told him that it needed old rock, the oldest, and he'd looked all over Sael, and this was the oldest. He told him it wouldn't hurt the stones. He'd bed it next to them, not on them.

Taurre listened to all of it without a word.

Then he said his terms. He said them as he said everything, in his clerk's voice, off the slate.

"He says these are the watch's stones," said Ilenne. "The watch's places. What stands on them is his to say. He says it can stand." She listened. "He says the watch reads it too. Every day. Not only you. He says it comes off when he says. Not when you say. He says if it shakes his stones, it goes in the sea."

"Tell him that's fair," said Bram. "Tell him if it shakes his stones I'll throw it in myself."

Ilenne told him. Taurre's colour did nothing at all. He wrote on his slate, a line and a mark, and stepped back.

So Bram set it up. He set it up on the old black rock at the breakwater's crown beside the third fixed stone, with Zale holding the glass foot steady and Emeric levelling it with a spirit level and Ilenne keeping the watch's people off it; he bedded the foot in a mortar of the grey ash stuff, which the watch's people brought him in a bucket without being asked, and let it set, which it did, faster than anything he'd ever used at home, under a wet cloth; and he hung the wheel, and wound the clock, and put the pen to the paper.

The drum began to turn.

It turned very slowly. Once an hour. The pen drew a line round it, thin and black, and the line was almost straight. Almost. If you looked closely there was the faintest tremble in it, a fur on the ink, the sea against the breakwater and the kilns across the ring and the whole caldera breathing. It was the first time anybody had ever written down what the rock under Sael was doing. Bram stood back and looked at it with his hands in his pockets.

The watch's people didn't look at it. There were four of them on the breakwater's crown, at their stones, with their slates, doing their morning reading. Not one of them looked at the drum. Every one of them, Bram noticed, had moved round to the side of their own stone that faced it.

"Don't let anybody touch it," said Bram, to Taurre, through Ilenne. "Not even you. Especially not you. If it wants touching, send for me."

Ilenne put it over. And Taurre considered him, the land man with the beard and the smith's hands telling the head of the watch not to touch a thing on the watch's own stones; and for the first time since the gate, at the throat, very briefly, Bram saw his colour go warm.



Neris was behind the kilns.

Bram wouldn't have found her if Emeric hadn't asked about the soap.

They'd washed in the hard rooms with what the Keepers left them, which was a grey cake in a shell that smelled of nothing much and, Bram had noticed on the first morning and every morning after, lathered in the salt water from the jug. Lathered. Properly, a thick white foam, like good soap in soft water at home. And Bram knew enough about soap from the castle's laundry house, which Ferra had made him rebuild twice, to know that soap didn't do that in sea water. Sea water killed soap. It turned it to scum on your hands.

He'd said so to Emeric on the Tuesday, and Emeric had gone very thoughtful, and then asked Ilenne who made it.

So on the Thursday, once the drum was turning, they went round behind the biggest kiln yard to a smaller one, a yard of its own with a broken wall and three little kilns and a long trough of something steaming and a smell that took Bram by the back of the throat; and in the middle of it a merfolk woman on her legs in a stained apron and gloves of grey rough skin up to her elbows, stirring the trough with a paddle.

She looked up when they came in and didn't stop stirring.

She was young, Bram thought, or youngish, with a broad face and her hair tied up in a cloth, and burns on her arms above the gloves like the glassmaker's but fewer; and her colour went slate when she saw who was in her yard, at once and all over, and she held it, and kept stirring.

"Neris," said Ilenne. "She makes the soap. For the Keepers. For the halls." She said something to the woman, politely, and the woman answered without taking her eyes off the land men. "She asks what we want."

"Tell her I'd like to know how she makes it lather," said Emeric. "In the sea."

Ilenne told her. Neris laughed. It wasn't a pleasant laugh. She said something short and went on stirring, and Ilenne hesitated before she put it over.

"She says everybody would. She says they can buy it from the guild. With the guild's mark on." Ilenne looked at the woman. "She says the guild's mark like a thing she'd spit on."

Emeric didn't press. Bram watched him not press. He walked over to the trough, slowly, and looked into it without putting his hand near it, and then at the kilns, and the heaps beside them: a heap of grey-white ash, a heap of burned shell, a row of covered jars. And at the end of the yard, on a rack, in the sun, a row of long dried black things the shape of sausages.

"Ask her one thing," he said. "Only one. Her lye. How she makes the lye."

Neris looked at him when Ilenne asked her that. For the first time she stopped stirring.

Then she put the paddle down across the trough and took him to the heaps and showed him. She didn't explain much; she did it. The kelp ash from the drying racks, burned again in her own kiln till it went white. The shell, burned and slaked and burned. The two together in a jar with rain water from a cistern, not sea. Left. Drawn off from the top. She held up a jar of it to the light, clear, faintly yellow, and said a word for it, and Ilenne said lye.

Emeric nodded.

That was all he did. He didn't say well done, or clever, or anything at all. He looked at the jar and nodded, as you'd nod at a man who'd shown you how he set a joint and set it right; and Bram, who had known him a long time, saw that he knew exactly why her soap lathered in the sea, and that he wasn't going to say so, and that he'd decided that before he walked into the yard.

And then Neris did something nobody had asked her to.

She took a small shallow dish off a shelf, clay, glazed, the size of a saucer, and poured a little of the lye into it, and held it up, and said something to Ilenne with her eyes on Emeric.

"She says it does another thing," said Ilenne slowly. "In the kiln cellars. Under. Where the bad air sits. The air that makes your head hurt and puts the lamp out." She listened. "She says you put a dish of it down there. Open. Leave it a day. And the air is better. It drinks the bad air." Ilenne frowned. "She says she doesn't know why. She says she found it because her cellar always made her sick and then one week it didn't, and she'd left a dish down there."

Emeric looked at the dish.

Bram looked at Emeric. He'd seen his face do that before, once or twice over the years, and he'd never got used to it: the face of a man who has just been shown something he knew from a book, done with a saucer, by somebody who'd never seen the book.

"Tell her thank you," said Emeric.

Ilenne told her. Neris put the dish back on the shelf.

Then she picked up the paddle and went back to the trough, and said one more thing without turning round, to the steam, and Ilenne didn't put it into Kaldo at once.

"What did she say?"

"Something about the guild." Ilenne was looking at the woman's back. "That they shut the door on her. Her own. And a man, one of them, who sells her soap with the guild's mark on, as his." She paused. "She says if you want the soap, buy it from him. He'll be glad of the iron."

Neris stirred the trough. She didn't look round.

Bram stood in the yard with the stink of it in his throat and looked at the dried black things on the rack and the grey gloves on her arms and the burns above them, and thought he'd seen that back before, on a girl at a bench in his own yard, a long time ago, who'd worked as if somebody might come and take it off her.

He didn't say so. It wasn't his to say, and she'd have spat at him.



Ferra came out after noon, because she'd been meaning to buy fish all week and because she had a bar to show the bench.

There was a trader on the quay by the pilots' hall with dried fish in bales and kelp in sheets and salt in skin bags, and Ferra stopped and asked the price of enough for the fleet for ten days, through Ilenne, and paid it in iron. Bram watched the trader's face while she counted the bars out onto his board. He'd seen the pilot's do it at the gate, Ilenne said, and it did the same thing now: a flicker of colour, fast, light and dark, and then held, and a look at the iron that was the look of a man doing a sum in his head and not liking where it came out.

The trader's boats would take it out to the hulls, under the watch's escort. That was the arrangement. Ferra had it written on a slip.

And then, before the first boat left, she had them put the load on the quay's scales and weighed it, bale by bale, with the trader standing there, and wrote each weight in her book.

The trader went slate at the throat. Bram saw it. It didn't need Ilenne to tell him: a man was being told to his face that he'd be watched for short weight.

Then Ferra said, through Ilenne, quite pleasantly, that she'd weigh the empty crates and bags too when the boat came back, and the trader's colour went somewhere else, uncertain, and Bram saw him work out that she didn't think he'd cheat her on the fish. She thought somebody might put something in the crates on the way back. Or take something out. And she wanted to know which, if it happened, to the ounce.

The trader looked at the iron on his board, and then at Ferra, and then at Ilenne, and said something that made Ilenne nod.

"He says weigh what you like," said Ilenne. "He says he'd weigh it too, with iron like that."

The bar came out of her coat when the fish was done.

It was one of the bars she'd paid the pilot in, or its twin: a foot long, two fingers thick, dark grey, with the marks of the roll on it. She carried it wrapped in a cloth under her arm. Bram had made the steel. Grayrock had rolled it. He knew it was good. He'd broken a dozen of its sisters on the testing frame at home and written down what each one took before it went.

The certifying bench was in a long hall on the island by the pilots' hall, and it was run, Ilenne said on the way, by the Rim's house. It was old. It was older than the house, she said. It was a joint thing, merfolk and smiths, from before the war or just after it; a smith and a merfolk sat at it together, always, and between them they tested whatever was going to be built with on the Rim, and marked it, and nothing went into a wall or a dome or a ship's frame on the Rim without the bench's mark on it.

So Ferra wanted the bench's mark on Kaldo's steel. If Kaldo's steel had the bench's mark, the Rim could build with it. That was the whole of it.

The hall was cool and dim and lined with shelves, and on the shelves, all down both walls, tally-stones: flat stones, cut with rows of marks, stacked on edge, hundreds and hundreds of them, going back. Ilenne said they were the bench's books. Everything built on the Rim, by year: what it was, who made it, what it was made of, who tested it, who signed it, when it was looked at again. Bram looked at the shelves going away into the dimness and thought that he'd have given a good deal for Kaldo's own works to be kept half as well.

At the far end there was a bench. A real one, stone, with a smith on one end of it and a merman on the other, and between them a frame that Bram looked at once and recognised in the same instant with great pleasure: a testing frame, a lever and a screw and a set of jaws, not so different from his own, older, beautifully made.

The merman was the clerk. He looked at the bar when Ferra unwrapped it, and turned it over with one finger, and looked for something on it, and didn't find it.

"He asks where the mark is," said Ilenne.

"What mark?"

"The hold's mark. Where it was made. The smiths' iron comes with the mark of the hold that made it. He tests a bar with a mark. He says without the mark..." She listened. "Without the mark, there's nothing to say it's what it is. He says he can't certify a bar without a mark. There's nothing to certify."

Ferra let the clerk wait.

"I'm not asking him to certify the mark," she said. "I'm asking him to test the bar."

Ilenne put it over.

The clerk opened his mouth. Then he shut it. He looked at the smith at the other end of the bench. The smith, who was the red-brown one from the day before, looked back at him, and said nothing, and Bram saw that the smith was enjoying himself.

"Put it in the jaws," said Ferra. "Turn the screw. Write down what it takes before it goes. That's a test. I don't need a mark for that. You don't either."

The clerk said something short.

"He says not today," said Ilenne. "He says he has to ask."

"Ask who?"

Ilenne didn't need to ask him that. She looked at the shelves. "The house," she said quietly.

Ferra wrapped the bar up again in its cloth. She did it slowly and carefully, the corners folded in, the way she did everything she meant to come back to.

"Tell him I'll come back tomorrow," she said. "And the day after. And every day until he's asked." She tucked the bar under her arm. "Tell him I'm very patient."

Ilenne told him. Bram looked at the testing frame one last time, at the beautiful old screw and the jaws that had held every bar the Rim had built with for longer than anybody could remember, and thought that he'd have liked very much to see what Kaldo's steel did in it.

On the way out, without meaning to, he read the end of the nearest shelf. He couldn't read the marks. But he could count, and he counted the stones for the last few years on that shelf, one for each thing built, and there were a lot of them, and every one of them had a mark at the bottom that was the same mark. Signed. Every year. Everything.
