Chapter 52 - The First Lathe

The foundry gave him his first honest stock in the last week of the month, bar and rod and plate that had cooled even and rung true when Bram struck it, and Emeric stood over the little pile of it the way a man stands over the first clean water from a well he has spent a year digging, which is to say with less joy than he expected and more arithmetic. The metal was the answer to a question, and the question was not the one most men would have asked of it. A smith looking at that stock saw swords and hinges and cookpots. Emeric saw the one thing that had to come before all of those and that none of them could be made well without, and he had told the crew what it was at Grayrock and told them again now so no one would mistake the slowness of the next month for the crew doing nothing. A lathe. A tool whose only product was other tools, the quiet keystone he had named back when the road was still just stakes in the ground, the thing that took the longest to build and the longest to learn and paid nothing back until long after a man had stopped believing it would.

There was a joke in it that stopped being funny the moment he had to live inside it. To make a good lathe you needed a lathe, because the parts of a lathe had to be turned true and only a lathe turned things true, and so the first one could not be born from a better one because there was no better one, there was nothing, there was a smith with a good eye and a straightedge struck off the standardized measures already in force across the barony and a great deal of patience for being wrong. The first lathe would be built by hand, crude and off-square and barely deserving the name, and it would have exactly one job in the whole of its short life, which was to make the second lathe truer than itself. That was the entire ambition. Not a machine that worked. A machine ugly enough and just true enough to give birth to a machine less ugly than it was, and that one to another, truth crawling up out of near-nothing one generation at a time, and he had to want that, had to hold it as the goal and not as a disappointment, because the moment he wanted the finished thing instead he would reach for the shortcut, and he had spent the morning refusing to look at the shortcut and had looked at it anyway.

The shortcut was in his hands, or nearly. He had been practicing, in the hours he stole and told no one were stolen, a thing he did not yet have a humble enough name for and so called magi-crafting to himself in the privacy where a man is allowed to be grand. Metal, held in the air on nothing, warmed and softened and coaxed to creep, a hair here and a hair there, into a shape it had not been poured into. Not made from nothing, never that, the mana did not invent the iron and did not change what the iron was down in its grain, it only moved heat and force onto it the way a hammer moved force, except that the hammer was everywhere at once and answered to the exact picture he held in his head. He could see the true cylinder. That was the whole of his advantage over every mage in this world, that he could hold the true form in his mind as a measured thing and not as a feeling, and now he had begun, barely, to be able to press that picture onto metal directly. He could make the part instead of building the machine that made the part. He could skip the ugly generations entirely and hand the crew a true spindle out of the air.

He could not, and the reasons stacked up faster than the wanting did. The smaller reason was that he was not strong enough, not close, and knew it in his own strained veins every time he tried a pass longer than a few breaths. His capacity was what it was, a cup, and this was a buckets work, and he did not even know whether the cup could be made larger or whether he had been born with the cup he would die with, which was its own quiet fear he set aside for a day with fewer other fears in it. He had two crutches for the smallness. Armand could stand as a buffer, the old tutor's throughput far past his own, taking the raw volume through his trained channel and letting Emeric supply only the shaping and the intent, the master carrying the weight so the apprentice could aim it, and that division was the only reason the thing was possible at all. And a flat stone, charged full, sat as a power bank at his elbow, pure mana in reserve to draw on when his own ran dry, substitute fuel for a tank too small, so that the limit on a day's work became the stone's charge and Armand's patience rather than the moment Emeric's own vessel emptied and his hands began to shake.

The larger reason was the one that mattered, and it was the standard, and the standard did not bend for how tired he was of building things the slow way. A lathe he pulled out of the air was a lathe no one else in the barony could make. Bram could not make it. The crew could not make it. If he made the machine with magic, then the machine's truth lived in him and nowhere else, and the day he was gone, or exiled again, or dead in some later chapter he could not yet see the shape of, the second lathe could not be born, because the thing it would have been born from had never really existed as a thing, only as a trick he did and could not leave behind. He had built a barony on the flat refusal to hang anything from a single thread, and here was the temptation to hang the whole industrial future from the single thread of himself, dressed up as cleverness. He said no to it out loud, alone, to hear how it sounded, and it sounded correct and felt like losing something.

So he found the seam between the yes and the no, and it took him most of a day of turning the problem over, and it lived exactly where the bootstrap was hardest. The hardest thing in the whole crawl up from nothing was not the hundredth true part. It was the first true reference, the very first flat plane and the very first true cylinder struck off in a world that held no flat plane and no true cylinder to check them against. Every measurement after that had something to be measured against. The first one had nothing, and men had spent centuries of real history sweating that first datum into being by hand through methods so slow they read like penance. That first seed of truth, and only that, he would allow the magic to make. Not the lathe. The datum the lathe would be judged against. And then, because a seed that could not reproduce itself was worthless, he would teach the crew the mundane road to the same truth, the hand methods that needed no mage and no stone and no him, so that the reference he made by trick could be remade by anyone with three plates and a scraper and the willingness to be wrong for a week. The magic would buy the seed. The method would make the seed grow without him. That was the only shape of it that did not break his own rule, and he took it.

The making of the seed was not the clean thing the word magic promises to men who have never done it. Armand set his broad hand on the feeder stone and opened the channel, and the mana came through him steady as a millrace, and Emeric took the fine end of that flow and bent his whole attention onto a short length of bar held a foot above the bench, and pressed the picture of a true cylinder onto it, and it fought him. Metal does not want to be true. It wants to be whatever cooling and casting made it, and moving it a hair meant holding a heat exact enough to soften without slumping and a force even enough not to favor one side, and doing both at once while his own small vessel ached and his stomach turned over. He could hold it for a few breaths and then had to let go, and the bar sagged and had to cool and be started again, and twice he pushed a hair too far and the excess vented off it as raw heat that scorched the bench and told him, in the only language overload speaks, that he had asked for more than the work could take. It was slow. It was ugly. He was sick by midafternoon and drenched through his shirt, and Armand watched him with the flat patience of a man who had taught this boy his letters and was not going to praise him for sweating. At the end of the long day he had one true flat the size of his palm and one true short cylinder, and they had cost him more than they had any right to, and he set them on the bench and looked at them and thought, without letting himself say it even inside his own head where grand things were allowed, that this was a seed of something much larger than a lathe, and then he put the thought away, because a man who god-moded the first crude pass would never do the boring thousand passes that turned it into a forge.

Then the real month began, and none of it was magic. He gave Bram and the two hands the palm-sized flat and the little cylinder and told them these were the only true things in the barony and everything else would be built to match them, and then he taught them the road that did not need him. He taught the three-plate method for making a flat truer than any single flat could be, three faces rubbed and scraped against each other in turn so that error had nowhere to hide and truth fell out of the geometry itself and not out of any master's hand. He taught turning a piece between two centers so that it trued itself against its own axis, the machine correcting the very error that made it. He taught them to check, and check, and distrust their own eyes, and write the numbers down. And out of all that hand-labor, off the standard measures and the seed he had made and the methods that needed no seed at all, the crew built the first lathe, and it was every bad thing he had promised it would be. It ran rough. It cut slow. It was off-true in ways they could measure now precisely because they finally had something true to measure against, and its whole worth was that it could, barely, straining, produce a second lathe measurably better than itself.

He made himself sit in that and not reach for the feeling he wanted. There was no triumph in it and he had known there would not be and had still hoped, and the hope was the thing he had to put down. A tool that makes tools is the multiplier under everything else, the number every other number in the plan gets quietly raised by, and a multiplier does not announce itself the day it is installed. It shows up later, in the rails that run truer than a hand could beat them and the axles that do not seize and the instruments that read the same answer twice, in a hundred places he would not be standing when they happened. He had performed exactly one small trick and had spent the rest of the month giving away the reason anyone would ever need him to perform it again, and that, he told himself, sitting with the ugly first machine still ticking and rough in front of him, was the shape of it done right. The win was deferred by design. He had built the thing that would still be building things after he was gone, and it had cost him a fever and a shortcut he was allowed to want and not allowed to take, and it was barely good enough, and barely good enough was exactly, precisely, the whole of what the first one was for.
