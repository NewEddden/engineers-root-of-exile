Book 2, Chapter 24: The Post

The smoke went up over the Rim at about half past six on the Friday evening, an hour before the sun was down, and Zale watched it go from his own hatch cover with a mug of tea in his good hand and thought nothing of it at all.

It came up from behind the big kiln yard by the geyser, on the dry ledge at the far end of the island: a thick grey column, going straight up in the still air and then leaning over gently to the east where the high wind took it. Kilns smoked. That was what kilns did. He'd watched that yard smoke every evening for three weeks from this deck and this one was only bigger.

Then the watch's horns went.

He'd heard them once before, the night the glass went on the seal: a long low sound from the watch house, two notes, the second lower, and then the same answered from the gate tower and from the next island and the next, going away round the ring. Fire, Ilenne had told him. Two notes was fire. Three was the sea.

"Flue," said Hesk, beside him, shading his eyes. "In the racks at the back, by the look. They stack the green pots on racks over the flues to dry them, and if a flue cracks the racks go up." He'd been talking to the kiln men. Hesk talked to everyone. "It's a bad one. It'll be out in an hour."

Along the Rim's street people were running toward it. Zale could see them from the deck, small and quick on their legs, coming out of doorways and going along the causeway toward the smoke with buckets.

Linnet's set crackled in the deckhouse. She put her head out.

"Ferra," she said. "From the bench's office. She's in the yard, she says it's the racks at the far end and nobody's hurt. And the queen's guards have come up the yard stair from Below to fight it. The captain and his twelve. She says they came up out of the stair at a run with their bidents and went straight to the racks." She looked at the smoke. "She says she's staying where she is. She's got the bench's clerks arguing about her steel."

Zale knew the captain. The quiet one, the one who'd come last down the stair behind Imani the first day and never made a noise on a step; whose men stood at the foot of the queen's house's bell stair at night.

"The twelve," said Mira, at his elbow. "Your door guards."

"My mother-in-law's."

"Why them?"

"Fire's the Rim's," said Zale. "And the yard's the queen's yard, on the queen's island, so the queen's guard goes. There's a stair from the yard straight down to the court's water. That's what they'll have come up." He drank some of his tea. "Ilenne says it's the fast way. For anybody who can swim a shaft."

Mira watched the guards go along the causeway until the smoke hid them.

"Who's at your bell, then," she said.

Zale thought about it.

"Thalune," he said. "She's always at the door. And her mother's house has its own." He put the mug down on the hatch. "It's a kiln fire, Mira. It's the Rim's own yard. You're not going to see a knife in a kiln fire."



Linnet read the post at a quarter to seven, on the after deck, with the set in her lap and the crew round her on the hatch covers and the deck, as she'd done every evening since the wall; and Zale sat on his hatch cover with his arm held in and listened, and looked at his crew.

They looked back at him. That was the point of it. They'd been alone at the gate all day with nobody on their own deck but Linnet and Hesk and the Crestless, and every evening he came up out of the sea and sat on this hatch and they looked at him and saw he hadn't drowned yet. He'd told Ward that on the set on Sunday and meant it. It didn't make the hour any less dull.

"From the Works," said Linnet. "Tess. She says rib nine of the twelve is done, and she doesn't like it, and she's doing it again." She looked up. "That's all she says."

"That's a lot, for Tess," said Zale.

"From home. The held ones, from the K Discovery's set." Linnet turned the slip. "Maren says the castle kitchen's had a letter of complaint from the Lady Rowena about the jam, and would the captain tell the king that she, Maren, stands by the jam."

The crew laughed. Zale laughed too, carefully, holding his side.

"Tell her the king's at the bottom of the sea," he said, "and I'll pass it on."

"From Lady Amara," said Linnet, "to the captain. She asks whether you're eating."

"Tell my mother I had an excellent breakfast."

"Did you?"

"No," said Zale. "Somebody tried to poison it. Don't tell her that."

The crew didn't laugh at that one. Linnet looked at him over the set, and wrote something on her slip, and didn't read the rest of it out; he'd learned that she didn't, when a thing on the slip was going to land wrong. She folded it and turned to the last.

"And one off our own deck," she said. "From the Crestless. Bren asks me to read it out." She held it a little away from her, as if it might go off. "The captain's bunks are a disgrace to the service. Mine is a foot shorter than I am. Mira's is a foot longer than she is, which she says is fine. Arthur hasn't said. Formal complaint. Bren."

Bren, at the rail, looked pleased.

"Noted," said Zale. "Tell him the last man who complained about my bunks was my brother, and he's a king now, and look what it's done for him." He got up off the hatch, slowly. "That's the post."

The sun was nearly on the western islands. The smoke over the kiln yard had thinned and gone browner. He couldn't see any flames.

Arthur and Mira and Bren fell in round him at the gangway, as they'd fallen in every dusk since Monday. Arthur at his right, a pace back. Mira at his left. Bren behind. Close. Close enough to put a hand on a man with a knife before the man had got the knife out. That was what Ward had sent them in for, and what everyone on the Rim had seen them do every evening for five days, and Zale didn't mind it. He'd minded on Monday. By Wednesday he'd decided it was nice to have people to walk with.



He walked back along the breakwater in the Mark II with his helm under his arm, as he'd walked it every dusk since he'd started going down.

The breakwater's crown was broad and flat and black, the stone still warm from the day under his boots, with the harbour on his left going to dark glass and the open sea on his right going to grey, and the watch-fires not lit yet. A quarter of a mile to the landing. Then the forty steps, and the water door, and the wet stair, and the pool, and the first bell. He knew every bit of it now. He could have done it in his sleep, and some nights very nearly had.

Ilenne was at the stage head.

He saw her from a hundred yards off. She was standing at the top of the forty steps on the landing where the breakwater met the Rim, in the last of the light, with her hands at her sides and the borrowed bident leaning on the wall beside her, waiting.

She'd never waited for him there. Not since the night of the beast. Every dusk since, she'd come up the stages with him in the afternoon, or not; and every dusk he'd come back from the post on his own along the breakwater and gone down the forty steps on his own and found her, or not, at the pool, or in the first bell, or at the bell at the bottom with her back to him and her face to the water door. She hadn't once stood at the top of the stair and watched him walk toward her.

She was doing it now.

He kept walking. He didn't speed up and he didn't slow down. Mira, at his left, had seen her; he felt Mira glance at him, and away. Ilenne's colour was something he couldn't see from here. He thought it was probably held. He thought it was probably held, carefully, and he didn't know what was under it, and he found that he wanted to, rather badly, more than he'd wanted anything in a week.

Fifty yards.

She didn't move. She was looking at him. Just at him; not at the knights, not at the smoke, not at the harbour. He couldn't remember when she'd last looked at him like that.

Twenty.

He opened his mouth to say something. He didn't know what. Something stupid, probably. Something about rope.

He heard the shot.



He knew the sound. That was the strange thing, afterwards. Nobody on the Rim had ever heard it, and he had: a flat enormous crack that hit the breakwater and the harbour and the black stone of the islands all at once and came back off them, and under it the deep heavy thump of the brake, the sound of the K Amara's dragon rifles on the range at home in the winter, the sound he'd stood twenty yards behind more times than he could count with his fingers in his ears. He knew it before he felt anything. He thought, quite clearly: that's ours.

The charm went off on his chest like a lamp.

He felt it. A burst of heat under his shirt where the shard hung on its cord, and the air in front of his left side going hard all at once, a whole slab of it, the way it had gone hard against the knife on the market stairs; and then a blow on that side, through the hard air, that was nothing like a knife. It was like being kicked by a horse that was standing on the moon.

The round came through the charm's hold. Not as a round. Something tumbling, slowed, the charm's whole catch spent on it in an instant and the thing still coming, end over end, and it went into him under the left arm below the strapped ribs and out at the back, and took him off his feet.

He was on the breakwater on his back. He didn't remember falling. The sky was red over him and getting darker, and there was a great deal of noise.

Mira's voice, close, sharp, flat: "Roof. Above the kiln yard. Flash. Four hundred." And somebody else, Arthur, saying something short. Bren shouting his name. Running feet.

The charm was cold on his chest. He put his good hand to it and it was cold, and dead, and light, and he understood that it had done what it was for, once, all at once, and there wasn't any more of it.

His side was wet. He knew it as you know a thing that's happening a long way off. Not yet. It would hurt soon. It was going to hurt very much.

Ilenne's face came over him against the red sky.

Her colour was gone. Not held; gone, the whole of her, white as the moon from the throat down, and her eyes very wide, and her hands were on him, one flat on the hole under his arm, hard, the other under his head.

"Down," she said.

He didn't understand her.

"Down," said Ilenne. "With me. Now." She looked up, once, past him, along the breakwater toward the Rim's lit street and the smoke, and back. "Not there. Down. My mother's bell."

Arthur was on one knee on his other side. Zale could see his face. Arthur was looking at the hole, and at Ilenne, and at the Rim; and Zale watched him decide.

"Go," said Arthur.



He didn't remember much of the stages.

He remembered the helm going on. Somebody did the clips, Mira or Ilenne. He remembered the forty steps, some of them, going down on his feet with Ilenne under one arm and Arthur under the other, and the water door, and the cold of it through the torn suit, and the wet stair. The pool. Ilenne's hand round his wrist, and then not his wrist, his whole arm, and the black water closing over the helm.

And then the pack drawing at his back, steadily, as it always did. He hadn't thought about the pack. It was breathing for him. Its gills along both edges of the grey shell on his spine took the sea in and gave it out and gave him what was in it, and the valve in the helm breathed with him, and he lay in the dark water on the end of Ilenne's hand and let it, because there wasn't anything else to do.

He braced. He did that without thinking too. The deep came down on him all over, as it always did, the big hand closing, and he met it from inside, and held; and held with a hole through his side, which was a different thing, and he felt the hole every time he did it, as if somebody had put a hot iron through him and was turning it.

The bells went past. He didn't count them.

Ilenne's hand was on the hole the whole way down. She'd got it under the suit somehow, inside the tear, flat on the wound at the front, and she held it there, hard, and swam with the other arm and her tail, and she was talking.

Not to him. He knew her voice in the water by now, how it came to him through the helm dull and close when she put her face to his glass. This wasn't that. She wasn't near the glass. She was close against his side with her mouth near the hole, and she was talking into the water there, low, on and on, a long unbroken sound with no words in it that he'd ever heard, rising and falling and clicking at the back like the animal man's voice at the salvage quay. Talking to his blood. He felt it. Under her hand his blood went quiet in a way it hadn't been, steadier, slower, the deep's weight in it easing off; as if she'd told something in it to lie down and it had.

He'd ask her about it later, he thought. If there was a later.

Somewhere about the fourth bell he stopped thinking anything.



The bell in her mother's house came up out of the dark round him all at once: air, and light, and the warmth of it on his face when she pulled the helm off.

He knew it. He'd slept in it for nearly a month. The small round room of thick green glass at the end of its anchor chain, with the chain going down through the floor to the ring in the rock, and the water door in its side, and the lamp stones in the glass, and the two pallets of woven weed on the curved floor, his and hers, a hand's width of glass between them.

She got him onto his pallet. He didn't help. He heard himself make a noise when his side went down on the weed and decided not to do it again.

Thalune was at the water door.

She was where she always was, in the sea on the far side of the sheet of water in the door, keeping her place with her tail, with her hands folded and her face to the glass. Only her colour had changed. Zale had never seen it change before, not once in nearly a month of her watching them sleep. It had gone a dark, rough, bruised colour from the throat down, all of her, and it was moving.

Ilenne went to the door on her knees and said something through the sheet of water to her, fast and hard, in her own tongue. Thalune heard nothing; words didn't come through water from air. But she saw Ilenne's colour, and Ilenne's hands, and what was on the pallet behind her. She turned in the water and was gone, up, fast.

"My mother's healers," said Ilenne, coming back. "She goes for them. They come."

"Where are your mother's guards?"

Ilenne looked at him.

"At the fire," she said.

She had a knife. She'd had it the whole way down, on a cord at her hip; he hadn't known. She knelt over him on the pallet and cut the Mark II off him, from the neck down the front to the waist, quick and careful, the way you'd skin a fish you meant to eat, and peeled the black rubber back off his chest and his arms and his side, and the wool under it, and threw it all onto her own pallet behind her: the suit, its torn side, its belt, its weights, and the gill pack still fixed in its harness on the back of it, its grey shell dull and wet.

Then she looked at the hole.

He watched her look at it. He couldn't see it himself without lifting his head, and when he tried to lift his head she put her hand flat on his forehead and pushed it back down without looking at him.

It was under his left arm, below where Imani had strapped him, through the side of his chest wall from front to back; he could feel the back of it against the weed. Her hand on the front of it was red to the wrist. Her colour, over it, was moving so fast he couldn't follow it, white and dark and white.

"It's not in the chest," she said. "It went round. Not in." She was talking to herself. "The charm turned it. It went round."

"Good charm," said Zale.

She didn't answer. She took a fold of something out of a niche in the glass, cloth or weed, and pressed it on the front of the wound, and put his own good hand on it, and pressed his hand down hard with hers.

"Hold," she said. "Hard. Like that."

"I'm holding."

He was. His hand on the cloth on his side, and her hand on his hand. He looked at the cord on his chest where the charm hung, cold and dead and light as a leaf.

"You know," he said, "that's the best bit of jewellery I've ever owned."

She didn't laugh.

She knelt there over him with her hand on his and looked at the charm on its cord, and then at his face, and her colour went still. Not held. Still, the way water goes still. And she didn't speak, and he thought, lying on his back in her mother's bell with a hole through him and the sea all round, that he'd never seen her look at anything the way she was looking at him, and that he'd have liked a better reason for it.

The bell rang.

That was what it sounded like. A huge flat clang, through the glass, through the chain, through the floor under him, through him; the whole bell jumped on its chain and settled; and a bloom of white came up in the water outside the glass on the far side, by the anchor chain, and went into a cloud, and drifted.

Ilenne's head came up.

Outside, in the dark beyond the white, there were shapes in the water. Several. Moving. He couldn't see them properly, only the movement, dark on dark, at the edge of the lamplight through the glass; and then the glass itself on that side, over the anchor chain, gave a small sharp sound, a tick, like ice on a pond in March.

A crack came in it. A fine white line, the length of his hand.

Ilenne stood up.
