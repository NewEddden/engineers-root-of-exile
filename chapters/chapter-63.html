Chapter 63 - The Crown's Share

He wrote the letter to the crown himself, in his own hand, which Ferra advised against and Keller advised against and he did anyway, because the two of them were right about everything except the one thing that mattered, and the one thing that mattered was that a letter in a clerk's hand said a man had delegated the telling and a letter in the prince's own said he had chosen to tell. The choosing was the whole of it. A strategic material discovered on royal-granted land and not reported was, by any reading of the charters Keller could lay in front of him, the crown's to seize and the holder's to answer for, and a man answering for it after the fact answered as a thief caught, no matter how clean his hands. A man reporting it before the fact reported as a subject doing his plain duty, and the difference between those two men was not the crystal, which was the same crystal in both hands. The difference was who spoke first, and about that he had learned, at real cost, that speaking first is worth more than being right, because the first speaker sets the shape of the room and everyone after him argues inside a shape he built.

So he built it. He did not hide the vein, which would have been a lie and would have died the first day a price sheet twitched. He did not oversell it either, did not write the crown a letter breathless with tonnage that would read, to a suspicious court, as a boast dressed as a report, a man so pleased with his find that he could not help waving it. He wrote it flat. Here is a working. Here is what came out of it on the first honest tally, the real number, the one he had ordered tarped on the open slate and now handed to the crown unhedged, because a number you volunteer before anyone demands it is a number no one can later say you concealed. Here is what I judge it will yield across the year, ranged and not fixed, because I do not yet know and will not pretend to. Here is the crown's share by the terms of my grant, calculated to the ounce and offered, not withheld to be negotiated. And here, at the end, quiet, the sentence that was the letter's true cargo: I report this now, while it is small, so that no man may report it for me later, when it is large, and put his own coloring on it.

That last was aimed, though it named no one, and Keller caught the aim and did not like it. "You are telling the crown to expect someone to carry them a worse version of this," Keller said. "You are, in effect, pre-answering a charge no one has brought."

"I am pre-answering a charge someone will bring," Emeric said, "and the value of answering it before it is brought is that I answer it in a room I furnished, and he answers it, when he comes, in the same room, and finds I have already sat in the good chair. A man who accuses me of hiding the vein now has to explain why the crown heard of it from me, in full, months before he troubled himself to speak. He does not get to be the one who uncovered it. I uncovered it. He gets to be the one who came late with the same news and a nastier face on it, and late nasty news is a thing courts have always known how to discount."

It was the pattern from the trial season turned inside out, and he knew it as he did it, the same instrument played the other direction. Then he had built an honest foundation of true, verifiable, freely offered fact precisely so that a single deception could ride on it unquestioned, the truth doing the work of making the lie load-bearing. Here there was no lie to carry. There was only the foundation, offered for its own sake, honesty deployed not as the floor under a deception but as the deception's absence made into a weapon, because a man with nothing to hide who volunteers everything leaves an accuser nothing to uncover, and an accuser with nothing to uncover has to invent, and invention in a court that has already heard the honest version reads as exactly what it is. It was cleaner than the trial had been. It cost him nothing but the crown's fair share, which he owed anyway, and it left him standing in the one position his enemy could not dislodge him from, which was the position of the man who had told the truth first and all of it.

He sent the letter down the stipend channel, the same dull legal artery that carried his exile's allowance and his tallies and his forfeit-barony accounts, because a thing sent by the ordinary channel was a thing filed as ordinary, and he wanted the crown to receive the largest fact in the kingdom as one more line of routine administration from a well-behaved exile, not as a courier's sealed alarm. The framing was the message. A man who sends world-shaking news by the dull post is a man telling the reader, without a word, that he does not consider it a threat, and a reader handed that framing tends, at least at first, to accept it.

While the letter rode north he turned back to the working, because the letter defended the vein and the vein still had to be made to yield, and the yielding had a problem that no amount of good legal posture solved, which was water. The deep vein was deep, deeper than any working in the district, and deep meant wet, and wet meant a shaft that filled faster than men with buckets could empty it, and a flooded shaft was a rich shaft that gave nothing, riches sealed under standing water like a purse dropped in a well. The old answer was more men and more buckets, which did not scale, a chain of arms that lengthened until the men at the top were hauling water the men at the bottom had hauled the season before, the whole line running to stand still. He did not have the men. He had never had the men. Every problem in Kaldo since the plague had been, underneath, the same problem, too few hands, and the answer had always been the same answer, put a fire where a hundred hands would have gone.

So they began the dewatering rig, a steam engine set at the shaft head with its beam rocking a pump-rod down the wet dark, the same engine in principle that ran the mill and the foundry blast, turned to the plainest labor there was, lifting water out of a hole. There was no mana in it. That was the point of it, as it had been the point of the flue that cleared the dead air, that the thing keeping the richest working in the district alive was a fire, a boiler, a rod, and a pump, all of it teachable, all of it replicable, all of it running whether or not the prince was standing at the shaft head or was dead in the ground, because a barony that needed him to stay alive to keep pumping its water was a barony he had failed to build. Toller's men set the engine, and argued with it, and moved the boiler nearer than the drawing said and were right to, and inside a fortnight the shaft was drawing down instead of filling, the water going up the pipe steady as a heartbeat, and the wet dark giving back the stone the water had been guarding.

The crown's answer came before the shaft was dry. It was brief and correct and gave nothing away, an acknowledgment of the report, a note that the offered share was accepted at the stated calculation, a line thanking His Highness for his diligence in a matter of realm interest. Nothing in it warm and nothing in it cold, a door neither opened nor shut, which was, Keller said, the best a man could hope for from that quarter, and Emeric agreed, because the value of the letter had never been in what the crown said back. The value was that the crown now knew, in full, from him, and that the day Aldane, or whoever Aldane fronted for, carried his own version north with its own coloring, he would carry it to a court that had already read the honest one and filed it under diligence. The lawful road against Emeric had just gotten longer and colder, walled at exactly the place Emeric had walled it, and a man who finds his lawful road walled does not stop wanting the thing at the end of it. He looks for the unlawful one.

Emeric knew that. He had built the wall knowing it, the way a man dams a stream knowing the water does not vanish but only rises and goes looking, and finds, in time, the low place he did not think to raise. He had defended himself in the one court that could take everything from him at a stroke, and in doing so had shoved his enemy off the books and into the dark, where there were no charters and no shares and no dull channels, only a bought contractor and a quiet accident and a man who would swear he knew nothing. He had made himself safe from the law and, in the same stroke, made himself the target of everything the law could not reach. He did not yet feel the trade. He would.
