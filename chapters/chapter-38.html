Chapter 38 - The Long Account

Rylan came to be hanged, which was the only thing he could imagine he had come for, and stood in the hall the next morning in front of the man he had sold with his back straight and his eyes forward and his boy asleep at last under his own roof, and waited to be told the price.

Emeric knew the look. He had all night to learn it, on the boat and after, the look of a man who has done the worst thing he will ever do and has decided the only clean thing left to him is to stand still and let the consequence land where it belongs. Rylan had built the whole of the last week to walk himself to this room. He had lured his lord onto an empty wall, and carried him down to the water, and he had done it for a reason no court in the kingdom would credit and every father in it would understand, and now he expected the rope, and part of him, Emeric could see, wanted it, because the rope was simpler than the thing he was going to have to do instead, which was live.

Ward stood at the side of the hall with two of the clean guards, and they were waiting for it too, the arithmetic they all knew, the sum Ward had laid out to him in the grey light on no sleep, flat and correct and merciless. He opened your gate. The men know he opened your gate. You put him on a rope and every guard in Kaldo understands the cost of it before noon and no one ever again asks whether a lord will look the other way for a man he likes. It was clean. It was true. It cost nothing Emeric would miss and everything he could not name, and it was the answer a careful man would give, and Emeric had spent all night deciding he was not going to be a careful man about this if it killed him, because he had watched a careful man work last night, on a river, with a fire, and he knew now exactly what the far end of careful looked like.

"You think you're here to be sentenced," Emeric said. "You're not. Sit down before you fall down. You haven't slept."

Rylan did not sit. "My lord, I opened the wall. I put you in the water. Whatever you've decided, say it. Don't be kind about it. I told you on the boat I can't bear it kind."

"I'm not being kind," Emeric said, and found that it was true, that this was not kindness, that kindness was a soft thing and this was hard and had edges. "I'm being accurate. You want me to sentence the man who opened my gate. Fine. Let's find him. A man put a knife to your son and gave you one door and stood over you while you walked through it, and last night that same man set fire to a boat with your boy in it to tidy up. Point me at the man who opened my gate, Rylan, because the one standing in front of me carried my dead weight down a bank to save a child and then broke a guard's neck in the dark to save me, and I've got no charge that fits him."

Rylan's jaw worked and nothing came out of it.

"Here's what's true," Emeric went on, because someone had to say the shape of it plainly and it was not going to be the man still braced for a rope. "They found the softest thing you own and they put their hand on it, and they could do that because I built a barony on the idea that a guard whose family I feed and house and keep safe will hold the line, and I was right, and being right drew a map to Finn. That's mine. The single hand on the single wall, the one gate for you to open, the one man who answers a cry on the empty side, all of that is mine, I built every plank of it and told myself for weeks it was a problem for a quieter season. You didn't break my system. You were the place my system broke. There's a difference, and I'm not going to hang a man for standing where I left the crack."

He let that sit, and then he said the part that was harder to say than any sentence, because it gave the man nothing to brace against.

"Take the week," he said. "Go home. Sleep. Sit with Nessa. Be there when Finn wakes up and keeps waking up for a while thinking he's still in the corner of that hold, because he will, and he'll need his father's face when he does. It comes out of no one's coin but mine and it's written down as owed to you, not taken from you, and I don't want to see you at a post or a board or a wall for seven days. That's not me putting you down easy. That's me telling you your family had something done to it that was partly done because of me, and you're going to go tend it, and then you're going to come back."

"Come back," Rylan repeated, as if the words did not fit together.

"The week after. At your post. And I don't want the man who left it," Emeric said. "I've had that man. He was careful and he was quiet and he did his sums and he was very good and I don't need him anymore. I want the one who came out of that boat. Come back with that one."

He watched it land, watched the thing the man had built to hold the rope have nothing to push against and start, slowly, terribly, to give, and he did not make him carry the giving-way in front of the hall. "Ward will see you out," Emeric said, quieter. "Go home."

When he had gone the hall held the particular silence of men who have watched their lord do something they do not have a category for. Emeric felt it the way he had learned, badly and late, to feel a room, and for once he read it right, because it was not a subtle thing. A lord had not hanged a man who opened his gate. The guards did not like it. He could see them not-liking it, the careful blankness of soldiers keeping a thought off their faces, running the same sum Ward had run, the sum that says if the gate can be bought once at the price of a man's child then it can be bought again, and mercy is just the lord telling you the door is still for sale. They were not wrong to think it. He would have to answer it, and not with words, with a wall that could not be opened by putting a knife to anyone's son, and that was a thing to build and not a thing to say. So he let them keep the thought off their faces, and they let him keep his own counsel, and no one said any of it aloud, which was its own kind of trust, the narrow professional kind that would have to do until he had earned the other.

Ward came back from seeing Rylan out and stood where he had stood, and the conversation that had been waiting behind his silence on the water was still waiting, and Emeric did not make him open it.

"You were right," Emeric said. "In the yard. You told me he was off and I ran it through the only machine I've got and got back a reasonable man with a child, and I was so pleased with how reasonable it came out that I never felt the place it was wrong. I read him the only way I can read anyone and it's the wrong way and it has always been the wrong way and last night it cost what it was always going to cost. You didn't reason it. You felt it, and you were moving before you had a reason you could have said out loud, and that's the whole difference between us, and it's the reason I'm standing here." He met the older man's eyes. "Write it down somewhere I can't file it under something else later. The machine I built to watch that wall woke no one, all night, exactly as I designed it. The man I talked you out of trusting your own eyes about woke everyone. I want to remember which of those two saved me."

Ward, who reported outcomes and not workings, and who did not deal in absolution any more than he dealt in blame, took that the way he took everything, and set it down, and gave back the only thing he had that was worth more than forgiveness, which was the truth. "He'll be a better guard for it," Ward said. "The ones who've been broke and kept usually are. If you meant a word of the week, he'll come back owing you a thing he can't pay, and men like that are the ones you can't buy off you." A pause. "You still built a wall with one hand. That part he's right to make you fix, whatever you do about the man."

"I know," Emeric said. "I'm starting on it today."

And there, standing in the emptying hall with the smell of the river still in his hair and his wrists raw under the wrappings, he did the last accounting of the whole affair, and this one he did not do for comfort, because there was no comfort in it, only work. He had learned, at a boy's expense and a father's and very nearly his own, the one thing his clever second life had never once made him buy, which was that a system with a single point of failure is not a system. It is a man, alone in the dark, waiting to be reached for. He had been that man on the wall and he had been that man in the boat and he was not going to be that man again, and fixing it was not one job but the whole shape of everything that came next.

He would need a second hand at the network, and a third, taught real delving the slow expensive way, bonded to no one's fear for their children, a door into his own systems that took two people to open and burned anyone who forced it, so that a knife to a loved one could never again be a key. He would need to be more than one delver's worth himself, deeper into the craft than he had let the suppressed boy in him go, and he would need the thing he had reached for on the wall and not had, the casting that lives in a trained body and answers an empty hand, ground in over years he should have started years ago. And he would need a body worth training it into. The four elites were some of the best this kingdom had made and this world was ending, quietly, under his own hands, and the men who held Kaldo could no longer be men who happened to be strong. They would have to be made, the way he made everything, on purpose and to a standard and teachable to the next ones, this world's knight training and the other world's soldiering both, real strength and real tactics and the sense of your own body in space that keeps a bound man alive in black water, coordination and survival and the water itself, the whole of it, built now, in the quiet, before the next boat came, because there was always a next boat.

And under all of it, cold and steady and not going anywhere, the last entry, the one he did not have to write down because he was never going to forget it and neither, he knew, was the man walking home to his son. There was a man on the water somewhere this morning who did not shout, getting smaller, unhurried, giving nothing away, carrying with him a name Emeric could not yet prove and a frequency he could not yet present and a certainty that would have to become the slow patient thing certainties become when they cannot be spent. Marrow had cut his losses and burned his own men and stepped off a stair into black water as calm as anything, and he had walked away thinking the night a wash, a job lost, a bill unpaid, nothing owed in either direction.

He was wrong about that, the way he had been wrong about the man he took for a spent and compliant father, and about the prince he took for cargo. The account was open. It was going to stay open, and it was going to be added to, patiently, in a hand Marrow had taught two people in Kaldo to keep, and one day, not soon, not carelessly, not in fury, it was going to be closed. Emeric did not say any of that in the hall. He had learned, finally, to stop saying the things that were better done. He only stood a moment longer in the quiet, and let the entry settle where it would sit for as long as it took, aand then he went to find Bram, because he had a great deal to build.
