Chapter 41 - The Signature

The last stretch of days had been eventful in the way a bridge is eventful right before it comes down. One plot tangled into the next, one runaway thread tugged another loose, and Emeric had given up on holding the whole shape of it in his head at once. The board was too crowded now. All he could do was work the piece in front of him and trust the rest to stay where he had left it, which, lately, nothing had.

So he went looking for the one thing from that night worth keeping.

He tried to find the sensation again, the one he had stumbled into on the boat with his wrists bound and his sight taken from him. It had come to him then out of pure need, the way a man who has never swum will still kick when the water closes over his head. He had pushed his own mana out into the dark of the hold and felt it come back to him changed, carrying the shape of things it had touched, the curve of a barrel, the seam of a plank, the breathing weight of a man in the corner who thought he could not be seen. Definite and blurry all at once. A room read without eyes.

Delving had more to it than anyone here seemed to know. That much was clear. Stress had cracked something open in him that comfort never would have, an upgrade paid for in fear, and now that the fear was gone he could not quite make the door open again on command. It sat just out of reach, like a word on the tongue. He was an engineer before he was anything else, in this life or the last, and an engineer does not leave a working thing to luck. If it happened once it could be made to happen on purpose. And if it could be made to happen on purpose, it could be made to happen without him.

It was only then that he remembered he was not working from nothing. He had his teacher's notes. Better than the notes, he had his teacher.

He nearly told Ward to fetch Armand. He caught himself. Since the river, Ward had been stuck to him like mould on stale bread, and asking the man to walk twenty paces away from his charge would earn him little more than a look and a very slow refusal. Easier to go himself. He could kill two birds with one stone. He would keep Ward's mind at ease and give an old teacher the small gift of a student who still needed him. Emeric had learned, late and at cost, that people gave more when they felt necessary than when they felt commanded.

"Hello, Professor," he said, and felt his own face pull at the word. It was not one he had used on anyone in years, in either of the lives he carried.

"Hello, Emeric, my genius student." Armand's whole bearing lifted. He had his favourite back, and he was not the kind of man to hide that he was glad of it. "How may I help you?"

"After the plot," Emeric started, "I've been thinking-"

"Oh. I almost forgot to ask how you are." The old man's voice dropped a register. "It must have been a hard thing. Bound, hooded, no way to cast. A mage learns to lean on the one thing that never leaves him, and then to have it held just out of reach, that would frighten anyone."

"That's one of the things I came to talk about, in a way." Emeric turned it over before he answered honestly. "But I don't have much feeling left for these plots anymore. Anger, mostly. What sticks with me is that I never saw it coming, and I should have. I can tell you exactly where a thing with structure will fail, magic or masonry, it doesn't matter. Show me a load path and I'll show you the crack before it's a crack. People, though. People, I read like a foreign language I only know the swear words in. Trust too easily and you go blind. Trust no one and you never let a single soul in to help you carry the weight. And what I'm building here needs more hands than I have, and it needs them soon. All I can do is build for today so there's a tomorrow to have hands in."

"It seems my disciple grew wise in the years I couldn't watch him." Armand gave a slow nod, the kind he had once reserved for a rune drawn correctly on the first attempt.

"There's a lot to talk about. But first, I want to tell you about something I found. And how I found it."

He told it plainly, the way he told everything, start to finish and no ornament. The ambush on the outer road near Kaldo. The hood and the boat. And the stone. The one his teacher's whole profession had labelled useless and discarded, the flat rune that gave no reading on any test they had, the thing Emeric had taken to calling the control stone for lack of a better word. He told Armand what it did, or rather what it let a delver reach, the layer under the layer, the base function of the mana stones and, past that, the base grain of the world itself.

Armand's face did something complicated. Emeric watched him try to hold two opposite things at once and mostly fail.

Here was a thing his colleagues had ground themselves against, declared dead, and tossed onto the pile, only for it to turn out to be a key to a door none of them had even known existed. Part of him burned with something almost boyish. A path this unexplored meant that every step forward could be a marvel, a genuine first. The other part of him had gone cold. New knowledge always arrived hand in hand with new danger, and a small, careful voice at the back of his skull refused to believe that, in all these years and across the whole empire, not a single soul had ever discovered this.

He could not prove that voice right.

He was simply no stranger to the smell of buried things.

"So you're telling me," Armand said slowly, "that this so-called control stone lets you delve past the surface of a mana stone, past its function, into the base workings of, of everything." He was leaning forward now. "You're telling me that hooded and bound, you pushed your mana into a black room and read the echoes coming back, and from that you knew what was around you. Do you understand what that is? Push that far enough and you'd have the plan of a whole building without setting foot inside it. Read a man's next spell from the way the air moves before he casts. Read his next step from the shift in the mana off his own body, if you trained your own body hard enough to feel it that fine. Emeric." He sat back. "This is the sort of thing men would kill to learn, and then kill again to keep anyone else from learning."

"That's the problem, exactly," Emeric said. "I've known about this stone since around the time the late baron was still walking and breathing. Only a handful of people know what it really is. And now you're one of them."

"If I weren't so many years your senior, I'd be sore I didn't get the invitation first." A dry smile. "But I suppose I've forfeited the right to complain about missing things."

"You weren't here. You were off babysitting ten grown men holding a tower up with their bare will, some eccentric rite you all decided counted as tradition." Emeric let the corner of his mouth go up so the old man would know it for the joke it half was. "I'm fairly sure I could automate that too, given a season. Imagine it. Take those ten men's labour and their years and spend them on something that isn't propping up an old slab of stone so a handful of greybeards can feel holy about it."

He let the disdain sit there a beat too long, felt Armand notice, and reeled it back in. That was old anger and it had nothing to do with tonight. He got back on the road he had come here to walk.

"Anyway. Here's the thought. The same way I resonated with the mana in that hold and got a blurred plan of the space around me, what if we built a device that could hold a single man's resonance, his own particular grain, the way no two men's are quite alike, and use it to find him. Distance is the wall I keep running into. But I have a strong suspicion that distance and a signal carried without a wire behave the same everywhere, they thin as they travel, they don't just stop. So, past a certain range, nothing. But inside that range, if the man is there to be found, we should be able to say where he stands."

Armand was quiet for a moment, and when he spoke there was weight under it.

"I've seen the shape of something like this, up in the tower. I can help you build toward it. But I'd be a poor teacher if I didn't say the rest of it out loud first." He folded his hands. "A thing like this, built and known to be built, buys you a great many powerful enemies at once. Those old men you're so quick to mock don't take kindly to their proudest arts being turned into a child's plaything. A device like this is a pipe put into a waterboy's hands. It makes it easy for one man to do what used to be another man's whole reason to exist, and that, always, breeds friction." He sighed, and the boy came back into his eyes. "Still. I'm a hopeless sucker for a new thing. I'll help you."

They started that night, at the workbench under the one good lamp, with the notes spread between them and the control stone sitting dark and unremarkable on a square of cloth. And within the hour Emeric walked straight into the wall he should have seen coming, the one that had nothing to do with mana and everything to do with the fact that he had built the frame before he had the picture to hang in it.

The device could hold a signature. That was the whole idea. But a signature was not a thing you could invent out of clean air. It was not a number he could set on a dial. To hunt a specific man, the device had to be tuned to that specific man's grain, and to tune it he needed a true sample of that grain to tune against. He had built, in his head, a lock with no key cut for it. He sat back and rubbed the heel of his hand into his eye and felt the old familiar stupidity of a man who plans the machine before he checks whether the world will feed it.

And then it came to him whole, the way his best understandings always came to him, clean and complete and one moment too late to have spared him anything.

He had a sample. He had three.

He had felt this grain before he had ever known to file it. The corridor at the banquet, back in the capital, the coming-of-age visit, a man passing behind him in a hall full of men, a wrongness at the edge of his sense he had swept aside as nerves and wine and the general oppression of that whole night. He had put it under nothing. Then the road near Kaldo, the reports of what he had told himself was fox-traffic worrying the wall on stretch six, too regular for animals, too patient, a pattern he had squinted at and dismissed because animals were the boring answer and the boring answer is usually right, only this time it had not been. And then the boat. The breathing weight in the corner of the hold that he had read without eyes and had assumed was one more of the men who carried him.

The same grain. Three times. Once before they took him, once while they watched him, once while they had him. He turned the three over against each other the way he turned any two truths that no one else had reason to lay side by side, and where they overlapped, they gave him a shape. A man's shape. He did not have Marrow's face and he did not have his name from Marrow's own mouth, but he had the one thing the man could not take off or change or leave at home. He had his signature.

"I can key it," Emeric said quietly. "I don't need a fresh sample. I already carry his."

Armand looked at him a long moment, and understood, and did not look entirely happy about it, which was to the old man's credit.

Emeric was already seeing the honest limits of the thing, because seeing the limits was the only way he knew to love a machine. This would not be a fix. Whatever they built here would not put a hand on Marrow's collar tonight, or lift a curtain and show the man standing frozen in a doorway. Resonance thinned with distance and went vague at range, and a signal that arrived vague could tell you a direction and a nearness and little more. Worse, the quarry was not a wall or a barrel. Marrow could go still. Marrow could go dark, could keep to dead ground and thin his own presence the way a good hunter keeps downwind. The device would give false grain and empty rooms as often as it gave a true reading, and every false reading would cost patience Emeric was not sure he had.

What it would give, on a good night, at the right distance, was a cold and workable lead. A bearing. A here rather than a there. Not the man, but the ground the man had lately stood on. And that, Emeric decided, turning the dark little stone over between two fingers, was enough to change the whole nature of the thing.

He had been carrying it as a chase, and a chase was a thing you lost by exhausting yourself against a man who ran faster than you could. This turned it into something else. An instrument. A slow one. You did not sprint after a bearing. You read it, and you waited, and you read it again, and you let the man's own movement draw the line for you a fraction at a time until the line had nowhere left to point but at him.

"We build it quiet," Emeric said. "No one outside this room. Not because I'm ashamed of it. Because you're right, and I'd rather have the thing than the enemies it buys."

"Now that," said Armand, reaching for the notes, "is the wisest thing you've said all night."
