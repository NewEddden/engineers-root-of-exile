Book 2, Chapter 2: Two Places at Once

The hull came in over the shoal at eight minutes to two on the Wednesday afternoon, white, low, and slow, and Amara had it in the glass for a full minute before she let herself believe it was going to keep coming.

She'd been in the bay drum since one. That was the arrangement for the daylight hours, which were the only hours the eye was any use: her made body stood on the old Motion Shop stand in the Well's lab with its hands at its sides and the set humming in its back, and the rest of her went down the chain to whichever drum Ambrose's list said was due a look, and looked. Today it was BR-01, over the bay. A drum had no eyes of its own. It had a mirror, silvered and curved, in a pod on a yoke, and a second mirror, and a little box behind them that turned what the mirrors gathered into something she could hold, and she held it, an hour at a time, because Imani had told her an hour was what she was allowed and Imani had said it in the voice that wasn't arguing.

The trouble with the eye was that it saw everything at the same weight. The tide on the shoal, a coal barge at the river mouth, a boy on the sea wall with a kite, the gulls. It took a while to learn not to look at all of it. She'd been looking at the kite when the hull came round the end of the South Sentinel and stood in toward the coast.

White. Long. The Church's lines, the two keels and the high stern, and the Eye painted in gold on the bow, which the eye made a fuss of, because gold caught the light. Its necks were running at a crawl. It had a lamp going at the masthead, the slow white flash that meant a hull on its lawful business and asking nobody's leave. It wasn't asking for a mooring either. It came up over the shoal at perhaps three thousand feet and turned along the coast, toward the town.

She put the mirror on its stern and kept it there until she was sure. Then she let go of the drum.

Coming back into the made body was like coming in out of the wind to a room with the fire out. The hands were where she'd left them. She lifted one, which was a habit, not a need, and pressed the set's key at her collarbone, and the line room answered on the first beat.

"Ambrose."

"Majesty."

"A Church hull over the shoal, coming along the coast at about three thousand. One. White, gold Eye, routine lamp. I want Harrold. And I want Tess to know, because her towers will already have it and she'll want to know I know."

"The towers have it," said Ambrose. "The point's been ringing for a minute." A pause, and something said away from the handset. "The regent's in the Board room. He's coming to the set."

She waited. Through the lab's long window the sky was a hard spring blue all the way down to the haze, and somewhere in the haze, five miles below the Well's floor, a white hull was turning along her son's coast with a lamp on its mast that said it had every right to.

"Amara," said Harrold.

"It's slow," she said. "It doesn't want anything here. It wants to be seen not wanting anything."

"Then it's looking."

"Yes."

"For him."

"For him," she said. "Shall I call the fleet, or will you?"

A little silence on the line, of the kind she'd been married to for most of a life.

"You," said Harrold. "You'll get him faster."



The call came to the K Discovery's long set at four minutes past two, and Sorrel took it, and held out the handset to Emeric without a word, and he knew from that before he heard anything.

He listened to his mother for half a minute with the sea going by under the bow. The escort's slick lay a mile ahead, long and smooth, as it had lain since six that morning. Ilenne had gone back to Zale's ship at first light in Zale's boat with her hood up and her hair wet, and Zale had reported by set that she'd been quiet about the tank, and Fletcher very loud.

"Put Harrold on," he said.

The line room's hiss, and then his father, small and flat in the handset at a thousand miles and more.

"I've the knights at the field," said Harrold. "Twenty. Osric's sending Declan up with them. The towers have it in their sights from the point and the Grayrock road. If you want my plan, it's to put the knights round it and walk it out to sea."

"Hold them."

"Say why."

Emeric had his reasons in a line already and he gave them in the order he had them. "Twenty knights round a Church hull over the lower ward is a fight, or the start of one. Somebody's stone flares, somebody on that deck thinks it's a spell, and you're picking glass out of the market for a week. They came to see if I'm there. If I'm there, they've nothing to stay for." He looked at the slick ahead. "I can be there in an hour. I'd sooner they saw my face than your knights. My face is only a secret."

"Only."

"Let them have it. It's cheaper than glass."

There was a pause on the line, longer than the distance accounted for.

"You're asking me," said Harrold.

"I'm asking you."

"Then hear what I think of the asking, before I answer the rest. You sealed it. You wrote it down as law, so that what I sign binds and what I order is ordered, while you're gone. And now you can come home in an hour from the far side of the world, and put your face over the town, and ask me nicely if I'd mind." His voice didn't rise. It never did. "If you can do that whenever there's a white hull in the sky, the seal's a courtesy. And the next man who comes to my table with a hard decision will wait a week to see if you'll come home and make it."

Emeric had nothing to say to that, because it was true.

"So it's my decision," said Harrold. "Under my seal. Here it is. The towers keep it. If it goes below two thousand feet over the town, or if it's not gone in an hour from now, the knights go up and walk it out, and I'll not ask the king first. If you're there before that, you're there." A breath. "Amara's here. She's heard it."

"I've heard it," said his mother's voice, from a little further off, giving nothing either way.

"An hour from now," said Emeric. "Ten past two."

"Ten past three," said Harrold. "By the board clock. Not yours."



Ward was on the set before he'd hung the handset up. He'd heard the whole of it; the K Amara's bridge listened to the K Discovery's long set as a matter of course, which had been Ward's rule since the night of the second relay.

"You'll want the convoy in the Band," said Ward.

"I'll want the convoy in the Band."

"All eight. Past the wall. Racks open. Parked, for as long as it takes, with you lying on a deck with nobody in you." Ward didn't make it a question. He was counting, and he counted out loud so the bridge could hear. "We can't run. We can't drop to the sea fast with the necks open, not without tearing a rack. Imani's unchanged go five miles up a day early, after two days of this air, and stay there as long as you're gone. And if the chain breaks while you're in it, you've told me yourself you don't know where you come back to. If you come back." A pause. "That's the cost. I'll say it once, so it's said."

"You're right," said Emeric.

"I know."

"The racks are due tonight anyway. We'd have gone up at dusk."

"At dusk, with the sea under us and the fish gone to bed. Not at two in the afternoon on a Wednesday because a Church captain wanted to look at a town." Ward let a breath go. "Ten minutes to close up. I want every hull at the mark before the necks open, and I want the escorts' guns manned the whole time we're up. Tell the fish."

Ilenne told the fish. She went to Zale's rail and leaned out and put her face in the sea and said something into it, and came up and said to Zale, who said to the set, that the old one didn't like it and would wait where it was. The slick stopped. It lay on the water a mile ahead and went no further.

They went up at twenty-two minutes past two, all eight, in the order on Ferra's sheet.

It took them half an hour to reach the Band, because the unchanged had to be brought up slowly, and the K Endeavor's hospital deck had people lying flat at the mark with their mouths open to work their ears. He felt the thick air thin under them by stages, until it was the ordinary air of home, and then thinner, and colder, and then the Band came round the K Discovery like a held breath let go, and Sorrel brought her to a stand on quarter veins with the cloud a mile under the keel and the sun very bright on the brass. All along the line, the necks opened. He heard the racks begin to pull.

Somewhere behind him, faintly, from the K Amara, he felt Ferra not look up from her sheet.

"I'll lay the drum while you're out," said Sorrel. "It's due."

"Lay it." He was already lying down. They'd put a mattress for him on the forward deck in the lee of the deckhouse, with a blanket over it and a second one folded at the foot, and two of Sorrel's hands to sit by it, and a bucket, because Imani had asked for the bucket. He lay on his back with his hands on his chest. "If I'm not back by four..."

"Then I'll still be here at five," said Sorrel. "Go on."



The chain went out from under him into the east.

He'd done it before, since January, and it hadn't got less strange. You didn't go anywhere. You went into the nearest drum's decider as you'd lean on a door, and the door was open to you because it knew your note and nobody else's, and on the other side of it was the next one, three hundred miles off, and the one after that, and you went through them as fast as you could think of them, which was very fast. The Band carried you. That was all he'd ever been able to say about it. It was the same stuff, a mile thick and a thousand long, and a mind laid along it went down it the way light went down Tess's glass rod in the line room, all the way and all at once.

Back down the chain. The wall water's. The second, halfway. The first, four hundred miles out, the one his mother had called good. BR-01 over the bay, where she'd been sitting with the eye. And the Well.

Sentry five opened its eyes on the hangar floor.

It was colder than his own body and it weighed what it weighed, which was more, and it didn't breathe unless he told it to. He told it to, out of habit, and the chest went up and down. The hands closed when he wanted them closed. His mother had said, the first time she saw him ride it, that he moved it better than she'd moved hers in her first year, and then that she didn't like watching him do it, and both had been true.

She was standing at the lab door.

He hadn't expected her there. He should have. She'd been in the bay drum, and she hopped from the Well, never the ground, and she'd come back into her made body to make the call, and the made body was standing on the Motion Shop's old stand in the Well's lab, twenty yards from the hangar floor.

"Two fifty-one," she said.

He looked at the clock over the hangar door. Two fifty-one. The climb had eaten the half hour he'd thought it would and a good piece of another.

"Where is it?"

"Over the market. It went down to eighteen hundred at two forty-four and stayed there. Your father sent the knights up at two forty-five." She said it level, as a thing to be known. "They're on the line now. Round it. Declan has them."

"On his order."

"On his order."

He went past her to the hangar's outer doors, and she put her plated hand out flat as he passed, against his chest, not to stop him. She took it away.

"Your hands are cold," she said.

"They're always cold in this."

"I know. I meant it as a thing a mother says."

The doors were open on the sky. He went off the lip of the hangar floor and fell.



The sentry's stones were not his stones. They did what he told them and nothing extra, and they wanted telling exactly, so that coming down five miles in it was less like flying than like being lowered on a rope he had to keep paying out himself. He paid it out fast. The town came up under him like a map you were leaning over: the dome, the walls, the river, the lower ward, the market roofs, and a white hull sitting over them with twenty small dark shapes standing round it in the air in a ring, perhaps two hundred yards off it, not moving.

He could feel the knights' stones from a mile up. Lit, every one, held. He could feel the hull's lift stones too, a long even note, and under that, on the deck, a crowd of people trying hard to look as if they were standing about.

He went straight down through the ring, between two of the knights. One of them was Declan.

He knew him by his stillness first. Declan held a line in the air as if somebody had nailed him to it, shoulders square to the thing he was watching and his weight forward on nothing, and his face, as Emeric dropped past it, was calm and completely unsurprised. He moved his eyes, and nothing else, to follow the king down.

"Majesty," said Declan, at a conversational pitch that carried perfectly across thirty yards of air.

"Declan. Hold where you are."

"Holding."

He came down on the hull's after deck, beside the wheel, on both feet, heavily, because the sentry weighed more than he did and he'd never yet landed it lightly. The planking took it with a sound. Nine or ten people on the deck turned round. A boy with a signal flag dropped it. A tall man in a grey coat with the gold Eye on a chain on his chest, who had been standing at the rail with a glass on the dome, put the glass down carefully on the binnacle cover, as though he didn't trust his hands with it.

"Who has this deck?" said Emeric.

The captain had it, and said so. He was a square man with a sailor's face. He also said, before anyone could stop him, that they were on the Church's lawful business, flying the lawful lamp, and had been passing.

"Passing to where?"

The captain looked at the man in the grey coat, which was an answer of a kind.

"We're bound for Sallow," said the man in the grey coat. He had a pleasant voice and a pale, clever face, and he'd got his hands behind his back. "Along the coast. We came in to look at your towers, Majesty, I'll own that. Everybody wants to look at your towers. We meant no offence by it."

"You came down to eighteen hundred feet over my market."

"To see them better."

"There are twenty knights round your hull."

"We noticed." The man's eyes went to the ring, and came back, and did it without any hurry. "We'd been told you'd gone to sea, Majesty. Off the edge of the map, the pulpits say. With eight ships and a mermaid."

"I did."

The man waited for the rest of it. Emeric didn't give it to him.

He could feel the deck through the sentry's feet, and the wind on the sentry's face, faintly, as a thing reported rather than felt. There were two mages aboard, a small one forward and a better one in the grey coat, and the grey coat's was held very still. He kept his own weight where it was and his hands at his sides, because a silicone hand on a man's arm would have told the man more than anything Emeric meant to say.

"You've seen the towers," he said. "You've seen me. You'll be wanting to get on to Sallow."

The man in the grey coat looked at him for the length of a breath, openly, as a buyer looks at a horse he's been told is lame. At his face. At his coat, which was the blue working coat from the Well's locker, and dry. At his boots. At the sky behind him, above the knights.

Then he looked up. Not at the knights. Higher. He put his head back and looked straight up into the blue over the hull, where there was nothing at all to see but a pale fleck at the very top of the sky that might have been a cloud's edge, and stayed looking at it a beat longer than a man looks at nothing.

"Of course," he said. "Sallow. Captain."

"There's one more thing," said Emeric.

The man waited.

"Your master had a letter from me in February. Tell him I read it again this morning, and it still says what it said."

The man in the grey coat inclined his head, which was all he'd ever have done in that coat in front of his own crew, and his face didn't change at all. But Emeric saw his hands, behind him, take hold of each other.

The captain gave the orders. The hull's necks came up from a crawl to a walk and the deck tilted under them as the stern lifted, and Emeric stepped off the rail into the air before it could carry him anywhere, and dropped a hundred feet, and hung.

The hull went out over the sea wall at three minutes past three, climbing, heading north-east along the coast for Sallow with its lamp still going. Declan's ring opened in front of it and closed behind it and went with it, two hundred yards off, at its pace, as far as the shoal.

From the Well's set in the sentry's back, small and flat, he heard the board clock's minutes counted down in the line room by a switchboard girl who'd been told to count them aloud, and at ten past, Harrold.

"It's gone. Declan, come home."

"Coming home," said Declan's voice.

The ring broke up over the shoal and came back in toward the field in a long loose line, unhurried. Not one of them looked up at the king hanging in the air over the market. He'd been in the line, and they'd held it, and they'd stood down on the regent's word, which was the word they'd gone up on.

He hung there a while longer, over the roofs.

The market was full. Of course it was full; it was a Wednesday. People had come out of the covered rows to look at a Church hull with twenty knights round it, and now they were looking at him instead, a man in a blue coat in the sky over their heads, and somebody waved, and then a good many people waved, and a boy on the sea wall with a kite let go of the string to do it. He watched the kite go out over the water. He put a hand up, because he'd have looked a fool not to, and then he went up into the blue, fast, the way he'd come, and didn't look down.



His mother was still at the lab door.

"He looked up," she said, before he'd reached her. She'd had the bay drum's eye on the hull's deck the whole time. Of course she had.

"I saw."

"Why would he look up?"

"Because I came down." He stood the sentry at its mark on the hangar floor, where it stood when nobody was in it, and squared its feet. "He's a careful man. A man drops out of the sky on your deck, you look where he dropped from. He'll think I came home on a fast hull and it's sitting up there somewhere. Or that I never went at all, and the fleet's gone without me, and the pulpits are wrong."

"Will he."

"It's what I'd think."

She looked at him with his own face on, and he couldn't read her. Her made face had never been easy to read, and she wasn't trying to help.

"Your father's on the set," she said. "He wants to say a thing to you, and he wants to say it before you go."

Harrold said it in two sentences. "The knights went up on my order. They came down on mine."

"Yes," said Emeric.

"That's how it'll go. All year."

"That's how it'll go."

"Then go back to your ship."

He went back to his ship.



He came back into himself cold to the bone, with the blanket off and the second blanket over him instead, and Sorrel's two hands sitting on the hatch cover beside the mattress eating something out of a tin.

The cold was the Band's. His body had lain in it for an hour and fifty minutes with nobody in it to keep the heat in, and it had done what a body does on a mountain: drawn everything inward and let the hands and feet go. He couldn't feel his fingers. He lay and told his blood where to go, which was easier than it ought to have been, because the Band was all round him and wanted to help, and after a while he could.

"He's back," said one of the hands, round a mouthful.

"He's back," said Sorrel, from the wheel, not turning. "Eleven."

He sat up. The sun had gone round. "You laid it."

"Ten to three. It talked at three. Ferra has it." She did turn then, and looked at him, a quick sailor's look, top to toe, as she'd check a sheet. "You're blue."

"I'm thawing."

"Imani says four on her deck with their noses, all at the mark, all stopped. The K Amara's fine. Ward says, and I'm to say it as he said it, the racks are full and he'd like to go down now if the king's finished."

"The king's finished."

They went down slowly, because the unchanged had to be brought down slowly, and the thick air came back up round the hulls by stages, and the cold went out of him. By half past five they were on the swell again a mile behind the slick, which hadn't moved, and by six the old one had gone under for the night as it had the night before, and the sea anchors were out, and the lamps were coming on behind the cabin ports all down the line.

He called the K Amara at seven, on the set, with Sorrel's hands gone below and the forward deck to himself. Ferra came on at once. Imani came on a minute after from the K Endeavor, and he heard Bettany in the background telling somebody to lie down.

He told them all of it. The ring, Declan, the man in the grey coat, the look upward, the letter. His mother's hand on his chest. Harrold's eleven words. He told them the way he told things, in the order they'd happened, and when he'd finished there was a short quiet on the line with the swell in it.

"You said he'll think a fast hull," said Ferra.

"Or that I never went."

"You said that's what you'd think."

"Yes."

"I'm only saying," said Imani, "that you're not him."

He looked out at the dark, where the slick had been. He'd been making a list in his head since the hangar, and it hadn't got shorter, and he gave it to them now because a list said aloud was a list somebody else could hold.

"Why send a ship at all," he said. "They could have sent a man on a horse to stand in the market and count my towers. They sent a hull. Why that day: we're eight days gone and they knew it the day we went. Why wait? And why so close to the Well? He came in under it. The towers are on the point and the Grayrock road; the market's nowhere near either. He came in under the Well and went down to eighteen hundred, and stood there, and waited to see what came down."

Nobody answered him, because none of the three of them had an answer.

"And then he looked up," said Ferra, after a while. Her voice had a different sound in it, the sound of a figure she didn't like being said aloud. "Before he looked anywhere else."

"Yes."

"Then put that on the list too," she said. "First."
