Book 2, Chapter 21: One Body Heavy

The call came at twenty past seven on the Sunday morning, and Mira was standing four feet from the board when Ward took it, because she'd brought him his tea.

She'd brought it because it was her turn on the bridge deck's morning watch and because nobody else would; Ward drank his tea black, stewed, and lukewarm, and said thank you for it as if you'd done something brave. He took the cup in one hand and the set in the other, and Ferra's voice came out of it from the Rim, thirty miles off, as clear as if she'd been in the next cabin.

"Crate fourteen," she said.

"Go on."

"Yesterday's second load. I weighed it on the quay before it went out at four. Your quartermaster weighed it at your rail at half past six and read me the figure at seven, the way we agreed." There was the sound of a page. "It went out of the Rim heavier than it came onto your deck. About seventy kilos heavier."

Ward put his tea down.

"Seventy," he said.

"Sixty-eight on my scale. Your quartermaster's scale reads a little light; I've allowed for it. Call it seventy." Ferra paused. "Fish don't get out and walk."

Mira looked at the board, because it was something to look at. On it, in grease pencil, under the date, was the line the quartermaster wrote every evening: the loads in, the empties out, each with its weight. Crate fourteen was there with the rest, in the same hand, looking like nothing.

"One body's worth," said Ward.

"One body heavy," said Ferra. "Somewhere between my quay and your rail it stopped being in the crate. I don't know where it is now. You do. It's on your ship." She waited. "I'll be on this set all morning."

The set went quiet. Ward stood there with it in his hand and looked at nothing for about as long as it took Mira to count four. Then he put it back on its hook.

"Get me the officer of the watch," he said. "And Arthur. And go round the knights' deck and tell everybody who's asleep to stay asleep and everybody who's awake to stay where they are. Quietly."

"Sir."

"Mira."

She stopped.

"He'll have heard the board," said Ward. "If he's anywhere near the bridge, he's heard me say seventy." He picked up his tea again and drank some of it. "So he knows."



The K Amara came up off her anchor at the Grey Mark at a quarter to eight and went to sea.

Mira felt her go from the knights' deck, under her feet: the slow heavy beat of the diesel coming up through the plates, and the anchor cable rumbling in, and then the long lean as the ship came round off the reef with the grey pillar of the mark sliding past to starboard. Escort three came round after her a cable off, as she always did, as if she were tied on.

Twenty knots. Ward had said twenty and the engine room gave him twenty, and the K Amara went out over the flat grey sea away from the mark with her black side to the morning sun and her wake opening out white behind her, and at twenty knots no boat on that water was coming alongside her to take anybody off.

He'd told the watch's station at the mark as she moved. Mira had heard him do it, by loud-hailer across a cable of water to the station officer standing on the pillar's ledge in his dark coat: that the K Amara was getting under way for a search of her own hull, outside the markers, in company, and would come back to her anchor when it was done. The station officer had cupped his hands and called back, in his careful Kaldo, that this was the K Amara's business. Ward had thanked him.

"It tells him he's found," said Arthur.

They were in the knights' mess, the four of them, with the door shut: Ward and Arthur at the table, Mira against the door, Bren in the corner with his arms folded because he'd been told to stand in the corner and not talk and was doing it as hard as he could.

"It does," said Ward. "He knew already. Now he knows I know. That's the price." He had the ship's plan unrolled on the table, held down at the corners with four mugs. "And now he's on a ship that's going twenty knots through water he'd last about a day in, as far as I know, and there's nothing out there for him to swim to but the Grey Mark, and that's behind us." He looked up. "So he stays aboard. And he hides. And we find him."

"Where'd he come aboard?" said Bren, from the corner.

Everybody looked at him.

"I'm asking," said Bren. "I'm in the corner. I'm asking from the corner."

"The crates come up the side in a net," said Ward. "The net lands on the starboard apron. The quartermaster weighs them on the apron and the hands carry them below to the cold store. Somewhere between the net and the cold store, one of them got lighter." He put his finger on the plan. "That's forty yards of deck and a ladder and a passage. Any of it. He's been aboard since half past six yesterday evening. He's had a night."

"He'll have moved," said Arthur.

"He'll have moved."

"Then we start at the apron and go aft."

"You start where you like," said Ward. "Take a dozen. I want him alive. I want whatever he's got on him more than I want him, and I want him more than I want anyone hurt." He rolled the plan up. "If he goes over the side, you don't shoot him in the water. The deck order stands. You fire at what lands on my ship."



The trouble was the boots.

Mira knew it by the third compartment. They went through the K Amara a section at a time, the way they'd drilled it at home: two knights on each ladder, a pair to each passage, doors opened from the side, stores lit, every hatch and locker and space behind a pipe looked into. And everywhere they went, they made a noise like a squad of men walking on a tin roof, because they were a squad of men walking on a tin roof. Steel deck plates, steel ladders, knights' boots with the nailed soles. You could hear them coming two compartments off. She could hear them coming, and she was one of them.

Twice, in the first hour, they'd come into a space and found it warmer than the next one. Once, a door in the after stores that had been bolted when they last went past it was unbolted. Once, in the passage outside the cold store, Arthur stopped dead and lifted his hand and they all stood and listened, and somewhere aft and below a hatch went down, gently, the way you'd close a door on a sleeping child.

He was moving ahead of them. Every time.

At ten Mira sat down on a coil of line at the foot of the after ladder and started undoing her boots.

Arthur watched her.

"He hears us," she said. "We sound like a forge falling down a stair." She pulled the first one off and set it on the deck. "He doesn't hear socks."

Arthur considered this for about two seconds. Then he sat down on the bottom rung of the ladder and started on his own.

"Bren," he said.

"I'm coming."

"You're staying. You're taking the rest of them up the forward end, loud, nailed boots and all, every hatch, all the way. You make as much noise as you can make."

Bren looked at Arthur, and at Mira, and at their boots on the deck, and at his own feet.

"That's not fair," he said.

"It's the job," said Arthur.

"It's a job anybody could do."

"Nobody does it like you," said Mira.

Bren opened his mouth, and shut it, and went red, and went up the ladder to the forward end with the rest of the search party behind him, and Mira heard him begin, a deck up, in a voice that carried right through the ship: "Right! Every hatch! Every locker! Hands on the rail where I can see them!"

"He's going to love that," said Arthur.

"He is," said Mira. "Come on."



The knights' apron stores ran along the inside of the hull under the starboard apron, a long low compartment with racks down both sides: the spare plate in its frames, the rifle cases, the line and the netting, the boxes of the knights' rounds sealed in their tins, and at the after end, behind the last rack, the canvas: the folded storm covers for the aprons themselves, a great slab of it stacked to the deckhead, smelling of tar and salt.

They came into it in their socks with the lamps hooded. The deck plates were cold through the wool. Bren was four compartments forward and a deck up, shouting about lockers. Nobody had been through the apron stores since eight.

Mira went down the left side and Arthur down the right, and they didn't talk. She went slowly. Ward's knights' stores were the most orderly place on the ship, and she knew them; she'd stowed half of it. She knew what the racks looked like full. She went along the plate frames looking at the gaps.

The storm covers were wrong.

Not by much. The stack went up to the deckhead and it had always gone up to the deckhead, and now there was a hand's width of dark between the top cover and the deckhead where there hadn't been. She stopped. She lifted her hooded lamp and let a little light out of it onto the canvas, and there, low down at the back corner where the stack met the hull, was a fold that went in instead of out.

Arthur had seen her stop. He came across the aisle without a sound.

She showed him with two fingers. He nodded, and took hold of the top cover, and she took hold of the next, and they lifted them back together in one long careful heave and laid them over.

There was a hole in the middle of the stack, built out of canvas, a boy's den in a hayrick: a hollow just long enough for a man to lie in, lined with a fold of cover, with a hand's width of air at the top. It was warm. She put her palm flat on the canvas at the bottom of it and it was warm, a bed a minute after you've got out of it.

There was a little heap of fish bones in one corner. A water skin, half empty. And a slate.

It was a merfolk slate, the kind the watch carried on cords at their belts, a hand long, dark and smooth, cut on both faces in their marks. Mira picked it up by the edges.

"He left it," said Arthur, very low.

"He left it when he heard Bren," said Mira. "He didn't hear us." She turned the slate over. "He'll want it back."

Something moved forward along the stores, behind the rifle racks, on bare feet.

She saw him. Only for an instant, between two frames of plate at the far end, where the compartment's forward door was: a man, tall and narrow and grey-skinned, on his legs, in a dark wrap, bent low, going for the door.

Arthur was already moving. Mira went the other way, up the aisle on the left, faster, in her socks on the cold steel, because she knew where that door went. It went to the foot of the ladder up to the starboard apron. The apron where the net landed. Where the rail was.



She came up the ladder onto the apron into the full glare of the morning with her rifle off her back, and he was ten yards ahead of her, running.

He ran well. He ran the way the watch ran on the Rim, long-legged and low, barefoot on the deck, and he'd come up the forward ladder from the stores a breath before her and was across the apron already, past the net davit, making for the rail at the after end where it was lowest. The knights on the apron hadn't seen him yet. They were looking forward, at the noise of Bren.

She'd been carrying the tag magazine since the morning. She'd put it in when Ward said alive. She felt it now without looking, under her left hand, on the rifle, the one with the green tape on its base plate, and she knew what was in it.

He reached the rail. He put one hand on it and went up and over in one movement, clean, a long grey body clearing the rail into the air above the sea.

Mira had him.

She had him at twelve yards in the open against the sky, and she could have put a ball round through the middle of his back before he'd cleared the rail, and he'd have gone into the sea dead and she'd have been within Ward's deck order to the letter. She knew it. She saw it, very clearly, in the second she had: the round, the back, the sea. He wasn't attacking anyone. He was a man running away.

She fired the tag.

It took him high in the right shoulder as he cleared the rail. She saw it burst. A puff of dark blue, a spray of it over his shoulder blade and down his back, and he jerked in the air, and went down the K Amara's side out of sight, and she heard him hit the water.

The smell came up a moment after. Fennel. Sharp and green and absolutely wrong on a warship's deck in the middle of the sea, and under the fennel, faintly, the dry flat smell of the stone flour that was in it, the one that Kaldo's sense could follow for a day if anybody's sense could get near enough to try.

The knights along the apron had turned round at the shot. Three of them had their rifles up.

"Hold," said Mira. "He's in the sea. Hold."

They held. Arthur came up the ladder behind her, breathing hard, in his socks.

She went to the rail and looked down. The K Amara's wake was going past, white and loud, twenty knots of it. There was nothing in it but foam.

"Ball would've done it," said one of the knights, a corporal off the port watch, at her elbow.

"It would," said Mira.

"So why the..."

"Because he was going over the rail," said Mira, "not coming at us. Because the captain wants to know who sent him, and a dead man doesn't go home." She turned round. "And because he's blue now. All the way down his back. And he smells like a salad."

The corporal didn't seem to know what to do with that. Arthur did. He put his hand on her shoulder for exactly as long as it took to say it, and took it away.



Ward stopped the ship.

He brought her round in a long slow turn, and slowed her to steerage way, and put every knight who had eyes on the rails, and they went back down their own wake for two miles looking for a grey head in the water. Escort three came round with them. Nobody saw anything. A man who could stay under for as long as he liked and swim faster than the K Amara could turn didn't need to show a head to anybody.

"He'll make for the Grey Mark," said Ward. "Or the nearest Rim boat."

"We're nine miles off the mark," said Arthur.

"He's a fish," said Ward. "Nine miles is a morning."

He took her back to the mark. He'd said he would, to the station officer, and he hailed the pillar again as they came in. Mira was at the starboard rail with her rifle, where Ward had put her, watching the water off the reef for a blue back.

There was a boat at the mark.

It was one of the Rim's traders, a broad black boat with a brown sail furled and two men at the oars, come out for yesterday's empties as it came every day at noon; it lay off the K Amara's bow as she came up to her anchor, rocking in her wash, waiting to be called alongside. Mira had seen it a dozen times from the apron. She looked at it now through her sight, because she was looking at everything through her sight.

A figure stood up in it.

It came up in the bows all at once, fast, out of the bottom of the boat where it had been sitting, and it had something long in its hands, something it was lifting, and it was turning toward the K Amara's side. Thirty yards. Less. Mira's rifle was on it before she'd thought. She had the shot. She had it clean, the chest, the long thing coming up, and her finger was on the trigger, and the ball magazine was on the rifle now because the tag was spent.

"Hold," said Ward.

He was at the bridge deck's rail above her. He hadn't raised his voice.

She held.

It was a boy. Twelve, thirteen, thin and brown in a fisherman's shirt, standing up in the bows of his father's boat with a boathook in both hands, lifting it to catch the K Amara's line when it came down, the way he'd have done it every day of his life. He'd stood up because the big ship was coming in and that was his job. He saw the rifle. He saw half a dozen rifles. He stood very still with the boathook in the air and his mouth open.

Mira took her finger off the trigger and lowered the rifle, slowly, so that he'd see her do it.

"Sorry," she said, to nobody, under her breath.

And behind her, at the far end of the apron, a knight said, "There," quite loud; and she turned, and everyone turned, and on the far side of the trader's boat, under its keel and out the other side, she saw it for an instant in the clear green water: a long grey shape going down and away, fast, toward the reef and the open sea beyond it and the Rim thirty miles off, with a smear of dark blue down its back.

It was gone before anybody could have done anything about it, even if there'd been anything to do. While every gun on the apron was on a boy with a boathook, he'd gone under the boat.

The boy's father had stood up too, at the oars, with his hands spread. Ward called down to him in Kaldo, slowly, with a hand turned to beckon him in, that he was welcome and to come alongside for his empties, and the boy put the boathook down very carefully in the bottom of the boat and sat on it.

At half past eleven the K Amara was back on her anchor off the grey pillar on the reef, as the queen's order had her lie, and nobody aboard her said anything about the morning to anyone outside her.

Ward put the slate on the bridge deck's chart table and looked at it.

"Armand's below," he said. "At the floor. With Emeric."

"Ferra's got Ilenne's word book," said Mira.

"Ferra can't see the slate."

"No, sir." Mira picked up the slate by the edges. "But I can."



It took them two hours.

Mira sat at the chart table with the slate under a lamp and the set at her collar, and read out the marks; and Ferra, on the Rim, thirty miles off, in the guest rooms under the sea with Ilenne's word book open on the table in front of her and Imani beside her, found them.

"Next one," said Ferra.

"A long cut," said Mira. "Down, slanting left. Two short ticks off it on the right side, halfway. And a dot under."

A page. Another.

"Is the dot round or long?"

"Round."

"Then that's a number. That's the mark they put with numbers." A pause. "Read me the row. All of it. Slowly."

Mira read her the row. It was mostly dots and short cuts, and she read them off one by one, beads off a string, and Ferra was silent at the other end for a long time.

"Again," said Ferra.

Mira read it again.

"That's your watches," said Ferra. Her voice had gone very flat. "The K Amara's. The deck watches, by the bell, with the change times. Four hours and the dog watches, and the knights' bleed-off on the aprons by watch, and how many on deck at each. It's exact." A page. "There's a mark beside the two in the morning that Ilenne's book says is the one for few. Thin. Not many."

Ward was standing at Mira's shoulder. He said nothing.

"Next row," said Ferra.

The next row was the delivery boats: the times they came out from the Rim to the mark and the times they went back, by the sun, for six days, each with a small cut beside it that Ferra decided meant the escort boat. Then a row Ferra took a long time over. Then a row of three numbers, close together, with a mark Ferra found on the third page of the book and read out to Imani and then to Mira:

"Ilenne's word for it is from the place to the place. A going. A distance." A pause. "Mira. What's the third number."

Mira read it.

The set was quiet.

"It's a time," said Ferra. "It's a quarter of an hour. Near enough. From the mark to the ring." She said the rest in the same flat voice. "That's you. That's the K Amara at cruise, lifting from her anchor, from here to the ring. Somebody timed it off a drill."

Ward's hand came down on the edge of the chart table, beside Mira's elbow, flat, without any noise at all.

"Last Thursday," he said. "The lift drill. Up off the anchor, out to the ring at cruise and back. We did it once to see if the stores shifted." He looked at the slate. "There was a Rim boat out at the mark selling fish."

"There's a last row," said Mira.

She read it. It was short. A mark she'd seen before, at the head of the slate, that Ferra had said was the one for land man; and a sign beside it she hadn't seen anywhere else, three short strokes, a long one, three short; and then the mark for the sun going down; and then a mark for up, and a mark for down.

Ferra didn't need the book for that one.

"It's Zale," she said. "The three and the one and the three, that's what they cut for his ship at the pilots' hall. The pilots mark every hull with a sign." She stopped. "Up at dusk. Down after. Every day."

Nobody on the bridge deck said anything.

"Somebody's timing my warship," said Ward at last, quite quietly, "and watching his dusk." He straightened up. "And reading my routine off the outside of my own hull like a notice on a board."

He changed the watch rotation that night. Mira watched him do it at the board with a grease pencil and a cloth, every watch and every change time, and the knights' bleed-off on the aprons, and the hour of the morning drill, and the weights at the rail taken twice instead of once, by two men, and each crate opened on the apron and looked into before it went below.



Zale came on the set at the evening post, from his own deck inside the gate, and Ward told him.

He told him all of it, plainly: the crate, the hide, the slate, the rows. The watches. The quarter of an hour. And the last row.

"Up at dusk," said Ward. "Down after. Every day. It's on a slate somebody brought aboard my ship in a fish crate. It's what they wanted to know."

The set hissed.

"So stop," said Ward. "Stop coming up at dusk. Or come up at a different time every day. Or don't come up. Have Linnet read you the post down the stage head on a bit of paper."

"No," said Zale.

Mira was at the board with the grease pencil, doing the new rotation out fair for the knights' deck. She didn't look round. She didn't need to.

"They're my crew," said Zale. His voice came through quite level, salt-dry, the way it went when he'd decided a thing a while before anybody asked him. "They're alone at the gate. They've no captain on their own deck from morning to night but Linnet with a set in her lap, and that's every day I'm down there in that bell. I come up at dusk because they need to see me come up. I sit on my hatch and Linnet reads the day and they look at me and see I've not drowned yet. That's what I'm for."

"It's what somebody's waiting for."

"Then he can wait," said Zale. "I'm not being frightened into hiding by a man who couldn't say it in the water." A pause. "You can tell Emeric I said so. When he's up."

Ward looked at the set for a while.

"All right," he said.

That was all. Mira had known him a long time. She'd seen him argue a thing three times, and four, in the yard at home, when he thought he was right and it mattered. He didn't argue this one twice. He hung the set on its hook, and turned round to the board, and looked at her rotation over her shoulder.

"Arthur," he said.

Arthur had been sitting in the corner of the bridge deck on a locker for an hour without saying a word, which was what Arthur did. He stood up.

"You, Mira and Bren," said Ward. "In on the delivery boat tomorrow. I'll have the watch's leave tonight." He picked up a grease pencil off the ledge and turned it over in his fingers. "You'll berth on Zale's ship at the gate. Rifles on the body, logged at the water door, the way the arms term has it. You'll cover his habit. Up the stages and down. Every dusk. If he won't stop doing it, then somebody stands next to him while he does it."

"Sir," said Arthur.

"It's my order," said Ward. "Not Emeric's. He's at the floor. I'm deciding it here." He looked at Mira. "You've not been on the Rim."

"No, sir."

"You'll hate it," said Ward. "It's all stairs."



The delivery boat came out to the Grey Mark at two on the Monday afternoon, with the watch's escort boat beside it and the day's fish in its crates, and Mira watched it come from the starboard apron with her kit bag at her feet and her rifle slung and her boots back on.

The crates came up the side in the net. Two men weighed every one of them on the apron, and read each figure aloud, and wrote it on the board, and said it into the set for Ferra on the Rim, and opened it, and looked in. Then the empties went down. Then the three of them went down after the empties, down the rope ladder into the boat, Arthur first and then Mira and Bren last with his bag bumping on the ladder all the way down.

The boatmen were Rim men on their legs, three of them, broad and brown, and they looked at the land knights coming down into their boat with rifles on their backs the way you'd look at a cow coming down a ladder: interested, and not very keen. Then they got on with the oars.

Bren talked most of the way in. Mira let him. He talked about the morning, which he'd been told about at length by now and which he told back to them anyway, and about the hide, and about the slate, and about how he'd had the worst job and done it best, which was true. He talked about the Rim, which he'd seen from the K Amara's rail and from the air and never once from the water, and what he thought it would be like to walk on.

"Stairs," said Arthur.

"Ward said stairs."

"It's stairs."

The ring came up out of the sea ahead of them in the afternoon light, the long black curve of the breakwater with the islands strung along it and the city on the islands, terraces and towers and the smoke going up off the furnaces on the dry ledges by the geysers, and the gate, the great arch in the breakwater, with Zale's ship lying just inside it with her crew at her rail.

Mira watched the quay come up.

It was full of people: the Rim's porters with their barrows, a watch officer with a slate, two of Voll's knights at the foot of the water stair in their blues, Linnet Shaw at the top of Zale's gangway waving a piece of paper at nobody. And at the end of the quay, by the stack of empties from the morning, on an upturned crate with her chin on her fists and her kit bag nowhere in sight, Maeve.

The delivery boats did the hulls at the ring in the morning before they came out to the mark. Mira, sitting in the stern with her rifle across her knees, understood without being told that Maeve had found a reason to be on this one's morning run, Sorrel's empties or a message or nothing at all, and had got off it at the quay when it put in to load for the mark, and had stayed there since, so that she'd be on the quay first.

The boat came alongside. Arthur went up the steps. Bren went up after him, talking.

Mira picked up her bag and went up the water stair onto the quay, the first time in her life she'd stood on the Rim, and the stone was warm under her boots from the sun and smelled of fish and smoke and salt, and the sound of the city came to her all at once: hammers, gulls, a voice shouting a price, water slapping under the piles.

Maeve didn't get up off the crate.

"You're blue," she said.

Mira looked down. There was a speck of it on her cuff, dark blue, dry, the size of a thumbnail, from the burst at the rail. She hadn't seen it.

"It's off a man," she said.

"I know," said Maeve. "Sorrel heard it on the set. Everybody heard it on the set." She looked up at Mira from the crate with her chin still on her fists. "You smell of fennel."

"Still?"

"A bit."

Mira stood on the warm stone of the quay with her bag on her shoulder and her rifle on her back and the whole Rim going on round her, loud, and looked at Maeve on her crate; and Maeve looked back at her and didn't ask anything at all.

"I've to berth on Zale's ship," said Mira.

"I know," said Maeve. "I'll walk you."
