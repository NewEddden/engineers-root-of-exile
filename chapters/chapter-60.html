Chapter 60 - Down From the Tower

They came down from the tower in ones and twos across the back half of the season, and no order had sent them, because no order could. Armand held rank at the tower the way a river holds its old bed after it has moved, in name and memory and nothing that ran anymore, and he could requisition a man about as easily as a retired general can call up a company he no longer commands. What he had was not the tower's authority. It was the older, smaller, more dangerous thing that survives authority, which is that a certain kind of student had followed the man and not the post, had sat under him because of what he was and not what he could hand out, and when the man left, the reason those particular students had stayed left with him. They did not come for Emeric. Most of them had never heard of him except as a joke. They came because Armand was where the crystals were now, and the funding, and the problems, real problems a man could put his hands on and be wrong about and try again, instead of the tower's rationed theory and its long patient starvation of anyone who wanted to actually build the thing the theory described. They came, in short, for the thing the tower had stopped giving them, and Armand was merely where it had gone.

They arrived resenting him, and he had expected that and found it worse in the room than he had expected on the page. The resentment had a shape he could have drawn from memory, because it was the shape the court had always used on him and he knew every line of it. A prince. The master's own pet, hauled up the tower as a boy and shown the council gallery while other students climbed. A drunkard who had wasted a decade proving he was nothing and now, they had heard, was to be swallowed whole as a genius on the strength of the same old man's say-so, the nepotism of it stacked two deep, favorite of the tutor and favored by blood both at once. They had been told by tower gossip, which is the most confident and least accountable of all the world's authorities, that he was a dabbler who had bought a reputation, and they came prepared to find that, and a man who arrives prepared to find a thing generally does. The loudest of them was a lens-grinder named Callen, and Callen was loud because Callen was good, and knew it, and had earned the knowing.

Emeric watched Callen the first week the way he watched any well-made tool, with attention and no warmth, and saw the quality plainly. The man had the finest hand for focusing glass Emeric had met in this world, the patient devil's work of grinding a crystal lens true enough to throw a rune's fine strokes up large and sharp so a carver could set them without a hair's error, and Callen had given years to it and could do it half-asleep and correct the grind of a flawed blank by feel. So when Callen looked at the prince putting down the high art of the tower to stand ankle-deep in a millrace arguing with a foreman about the seating of an iron screw, he read exactly what his training told him to read, which was a man who could not do the real work and had dressed his incapacity up as a choice. It was, almost word for word, the thing Armand himself had said to Emeric on the airship, the master of the sword insisting on the stick, except that where Armand had said it in bafflement and let it go, Callen said it in contempt and meant to keep saying it, and the difference between the two men was thirty years and a willingness to be surprised.

So Emeric did not argue with him, because an argument was the one arena where a man like Callen never lost, being quicker in it than the truth. He took him to the water instead. There was a demonstration he had been holding in reserve for years, since a plague village where it would have been useless, held for exactly this and no other purpose, for the day an educated skeptic with a good enough eye stood in front of him needing not to be told a thing but to see it. He did not build the instrument for Callen. He made Callen build it. He set the man to grind two small lenses, which Callen could do in his sleep and did with an open sneer at the crudity of the blanks, and had him stack them in a tube at the spacing Emeric named and no other, and gave him a drop of water off the bad reach below the old latrines and a drop off the clean spring above the town, and told him to look, and said nothing else, because the whole art of it was to say nothing else.

What Callen saw took the color out of him the way it takes the color out of every man who sees it the first time. The clean drop was still. The bad drop was alive. It seethed. It was a city of small furious things going about their business with a purpose that owed nothing to him and had been there the whole time under every sad list of the dead anyone had ever kept, and Callen, who had spent his life sharpening glass to see small things clearly, had never once in that life turned the glass on water, because water was not a rune and there had never been a reason. That was the whole of the trick and Emeric named it aloud for him plainly, because a trick a man could not see the shape of was a trick he learned nothing from. Callen had held the craft of the lens in one hand for years. In the other hand, in Imani's ledgers, in the physician's patient columns that clustered the dead by which water they had drunk, the world had held the fact that death ran with the water and not with the wind or the stars or the sin. The two truths had sat a hand's breadth apart in one small town and no one had ever set them together, because the man who owned the lens had no reason to look at water and the woman who owned the ledger had no lens, and Emeric had done nothing but carry the one across the small gap to the other, the same ninety-degree turn he did to everything, taking a thing they already had and pointing it at a thing they had never thought to point it at. Callen did not convert because he lost an argument. He converted because he had ground the glass himself and looked through it himself and could not un-see what his own hands had shown him, and there is no conversion in the world more total than that one.

He asked the right next question within the hour, which was the measure of the mind under the contempt, and the question was the one Emeric had been waiting for and dreading a little, because the answer to it was a door he did not entirely want to open in front of new people. Could they not simply delve it, Callen wanted to know, feel the sickness in the water directly, sense the thing the glass had shown. And Armand, who had come to stand at the edge of it, answered before Emeric could, in the flat voice of a man stating a floor beneath which nothing goes. No. Disease had no signature. Not a faint one, not a hidden one, none. The whole apparatus of everything they had spent their lives mastering, every rune and every stone and every mode of the delve, was built on force and geometry and the movement of mana, and life, the actual living machinery of the seething drop, threw no mana shadow at all and never had. Their magic was blind to it. Stone blind, root and branch. The lens saw precisely and only what the magic could not, and that, Armand said, was the lesson worth carrying out of the room, that in the country of living things they were to trust the glass and distrust the delve, because the delve would render them a confident answer where it had no business having one, would feel true and be wrong, being built to describe a world of dead force and asked, in that country, to describe a world of life it could not sense.

It was the right lesson and it was true and Emeric watched it land on the young sharp minds as a comfort, and did not say the thing that made it also a trap, because he had not, yet, fully said it to himself. The glass saw truly. But the glass saw only where a hand thought to point it, and a delve in the country of the living would render confidently and lie, and between those two facts sat a darkness neither the glass nor the delve would ever light on its own, which was the whole vast field of the things no one had yet had any reason to examine. They had learned, this afternoon, exactly where to look and exactly what to distrust, and the learning would make them careful in every place they thought to be careful, and would leave untouched, because a lesson about where to look is silent about where no one has thought to look, the one bench in his whole enterprise where a running current of shaped mana passed hour on hour through the bodies of ordinary men, and walked out of the works each evening in those bodies, into their homes and their beds and their confessions, carrying a change no rune could sense and no glass was aimed at and no man in the room, sharp as they all were, had been given a single reason to suspect. He had just taught the finest young minds in reach to trust the lens where the magic was blind. He had not taught them, because he did not know it himself, that the lens was only ever as wise as the hand that chose where to aim it, and that the most dangerous blindness a careful man carries is the one he has just congratulated himself on curing.

He counted the afternoon's gains that evening the way he counted everything, and they were real. He had acquired, in a season, a research faculty he could not have bought, a dozen trained hands and one converted skeptic who would now defend the sanitation gospel harder than any man in Kaldo because he had ground the proof of it himself. And he had acquired, in the same faces, a dozen more minds that now held pieces of what he was, the edges of the crystal work, the shape of the delving, the outline of things that could never be un-taught out of a head once set in it, so that every convert was also a new place the secret now lived and a new man who could, someday, under the right pressure, say it aloud. And it had cost Armand, though Armand had spent the coin himself and knew to the penny what he was spending. A head mage who leaves a tower spends his standing; that was the old and understood tax, and Armand had paid it in Kaldo already. But a head mage who leaves and then draws the tower's best young hands down the road after him is not spending his standing, he is hollowing the house he built, pulling the load-bearing timber out of the thing he gave his life to raise, and the Circle would read it as exactly that and would never forgive it, and Armand had known that when he let the first of them come and had let them come anyway. He had done it for the reason Fletcher had wanted the standard measure and Toller had wanted the mill to be his own, the reason that ran under everything Emeric had touched since a plague barony first refused to believe him: because here, and only here, the work could be given to hands that would carry it past the man who taught it. Emeric had gained a faculty and a set of enemies in the same afternoon and could not have had the one without the other, and was, as he always was, faster and surer at the counting of the faculty than the enemies, and closed the ledger on the season pleased, and did not turn the glass, that night or for a long time after, on the one drop of water he had every reason in the world not to think to examine.
