Book 2, Batch 13: The Returning Current

Chapters 94–98 | Tuesday 3 November – Tuesday 10 November, Year 17

The fleet is still at the Guano Isles after the storm. The dug face of the Old Stack has fallen, seven people are dead, and Kaldo has answered the Nestwardens' bill by voiding its own guano contract, paying a levy and agreeing in principle to a bond, inspectors and a share of the restoration, all still to be signed before it sails. Emeric's promise to Saldis still holds: no climb to the Band, no drum, nothing new in it until he has been to her house. And a tall man with a staff, Duncan, a warden of the Westgone march, has been watching the fleet from the guano wharves, breathing easily in the middle of a storm haul while everyone else gasped.

When Emeric finally asks how he does it, the man answers with his name: Duncan Hale. Emeric has a steward called Hale. Two thousand years ago part of a family walked away from the war the old races lost and part of it stayed, and nobody can prove a thing about it. Duncan will not teach a man he has not measured, so he asks for a spar and loses three falls without landing a clean blow, then tells the king that he bled mana the whole time. On the night watch he teaches Emeric the Returning Current, a breath count found on a thin march. And Emeric, who already has padding, cast stasis, the delve's slow time and a memory of a whole shelf of stories from another world about people who grow power like a crop, puts the pieces together.

The winged ones take Kaldo's bond, its signature on their inspectors' terms and its money for a restoration they choose, and agree to carry one letter at their full rate with their own commentary attached, a commentary that closes their sky to Kaldo's hulls. They sell one sample of cloudstone and refuse everything else. The fleet goes out of the lagoon on the water, with a Westgone warden at the rail.

Chapter 94: Kin by Name

On Tuesday 3 November, carrying Imani's bag on her round of the net stations, Emeric finds Duncan sitting on an upturned crate by the cot of Ari's brother, because Duncan brought him up off the lower cliff and a march-warden visits the men he carries. Close up he is older than he looked at the tally, somewhere in his fifties: long-armed and lean as a pole, his skin a shade darker than Emeric's and dulled by wind, his beard grey at the chin, his locs tied back at the crown and grey at the roots. His coat was green once and is now the colour of a hedge in March, thin at the elbows, with two straps across his chest, a satchel, a pouch and a flask, and his forearms wrapped to the elbow. Round his left wrist is a cord of knotted twine with a few wooden beads, and his thumb keeps moving over it. His staff leans against the cot post, plain and straight. Emeric has had his sense on him for ten days. He asks the question the storm left him: how does a man breathe like that during a haul? They speak orc trade speech, Emeric's renewed by Grau's delve and already thinning. Duncan's own tongue is a cousin of the old court tongue two thousand years apart, so about one word in five comes through. Duncan does not answer the breathing question. He says he came to watch, and gives his name because Emeric gave his: Duncan Hale. Emeric says he has a steward called Hale, who has kept his keep since before Emeric came to it.

Asked where his people came from, Duncan says: away from yours. His house left after the war, the War of the Groves, with the households that refused the war or the rite, and has lived past the wall ever since. Some of the house stayed behind in the old land, which is what his people call Ostrellin. The Westgone keep land and salvage rolls, and his house's roll goes back a long way. Neither man can prove anything. It is two thousand years, and Kaldo's Hale is just a man with the same name in a ledger. Duncan says so first. He wants to meet the steward and does not say it. Emeric sees him not say it.

On the landing ledge Ferra works with three councils on three papers, because each holds a different piece: the bond with the island council's keeper, Ruvo checking every line against her crack-marker book, Veska fighting the rewrite of Kaldo's contract clause. Ruvo begins choosing the restoration. Nothing is signed yet; the deadline is the leaving.

On Wednesday morning Duncan asks for a spar. He will not tell a man anything about breath until he has seen how he spends it, and a Westgone warden takes no gold-eyed king's word for anything. He asks in front of the crews on purpose, so the landing sees a spar and no council hears of a fight. On the flat behind the guano wharves, with no blades, Duncan carries his bo staff and Emeric fights bare-handed. Ward and Arthur stand at the edge without intervening, wanting to see the man rather than the king. The hired crews stop work to watch, as do two winged ones on the lowest launch ledge. Emeric holds back and wins all three falls: body enhancement turned inward, the pull on the staff so it goes heavy and off its line in Duncan's hands, set feet and a boxer's hand kept home at the cheek. Duncan lands one glancing touch on Emeric's forearm, and the staff stops dead on it like hitting a sack of wet sand, because Emeric padded the arm without thinking.

From the boards, picking himself up the third time, Duncan sees the truth of it. The king's mana poured off him like heat off a forge door the whole time. Every cast spent more than it moved, and by the third fall Emeric was the one breathing hard. Duncan, flat on his back, was not. "You bled the whole time." Emeric is not stupid about it. A year and a half ago he wrote "tuned for thin air?" in a notebook about trees, and he has felt his sense flood ever since Aumere. He had never measured it, and he says so. Walking off the flat, Duncan asks what his arm did when the staff touched it. Emeric says it is padding, the knights' trick, mana held in a shape. Duncan has no word for that. Then he asks Emeric up to the harbour watch tonight, with nothing in his hands.

Chapter 95: The Lowest Stack

The squat stack at the lagoon mouth carries the landing's harbour lookout, a ledge with a signal mast and a stone hut, reached from the landing by the walk. It has no homes and no nurseries, belongs to the landing rather than the nesting council, and is kept by the hired crews. Since the storm its watch has been short of hands, and Duncan stands it. With the island council's leave through Tiel, Emeric walks up on foot. On Wednesday, Thursday and Friday nights Duncan teaches him the count: gather mana to the centre of the chest on the inhale; hold it there briefly, and only briefly, because holding it long is forcing; circulate it on the exhale, out through the channels and home; rest. Breathing does not make mana; it teaches you to move it. On the count, Emeric's sense stops flooding. By the second night he spends a fraction of what he spent on Wednesday morning to hold the same small pull on a pebble. Duncan watches and says nothing, which Emeric is learning is how he says yes. On the first night Duncan slips the knotted cord off his wrist and hands it over: a knot for the gather, a bead for the hold, a run of knots for the circulate. March children learn the count on one before they can be trusted to keep it in their heads. Emeric does not need it and keeps it a night anyway. In pieces, on watch, Duncan tells his history: the thin march where dense-raised wardens grow groggy and ride in short shifts; years spent working out why one warden lasted a shift and another did not with the same reserves; the breath, the posture, the repetition; the year he noticed his own ceiling rising and did not believe it. Thirty years, and nobody ever measured anything.

On Thursday morning Duncan offers to teach the staff. He has seen a man who wins with his hands and cannot stop at a finger's width on a friend, and a staff can: it bruises instead of cutting, and it moves with the breath. He does not know about Emeric's restraint; he says only that a warden who cannot stop a man without killing him is no use on a march full of frightened people. He teaches the bo staff and quarterstaff: footwork, the two-handed grip, blocks, sweeps, the strike that stops a man without opening him, with the count kept in time. Emeric is bad at it, visibly, in front of the crews, and Duncan enjoys every minute. On Friday Bram turns a staff on the K Discovery's lathe from Emeric's drawing, tapered at both ends, six feet long. It is wrong. A march staff is a walking pole first, a man's height and a hand more, with no taper and no iron anywhere, because iron rings and the march is big-animal country. Bram takes Duncan's measure for a second. Mira watches the Thursday lesson from a bollard with her spear across her knees and says nothing.

On Thursday in the K Endeavor's ward, Imani reads Duncan with Kaldo's register card and her sight. He sits well above the register's line for a dense-raised body of his years, the first measurement anyone has ever taken of the Current at work. Duncan is delighted and furious at once: delighted he was right, furious it took a human king's doctor to show it. Imani asks whether the count would help the deckhands who have had headaches since Aumere. Duncan says, "On your ship. If I'm on it." She notes the if.

Ferra agrees the letter on Thursday: Kaldo's answer to the Leaving Terms charge, which travelled on the couriers' wings. It names what Kaldo's side did first, then gives conduct instead of argument: the promise to Saldis, the climb not made through the storm, the contract voided and the levy paid. The couriers will copy it and carry it to every door they serve this season at their quoted rate for a circular, paid in cloth and bar iron. It is revocable if Kaldo breaks the inspection agreement or the condition the commentary will carry. The commentary is theirs, and Kaldo will not see it until it is written. Kaldo's other offers are made once and refused. To hiring their eyes and wind-reading, the elder says no one of theirs rides a human ship, and Tiel, behind Ruvo, looks at the floor. To relay posts on the stack tops, Ruvo says nothing of Kaldo's goes on their tops, without needing a reason. At the lift's foot the lift-keeper refuses the harness Bram drew to carry his torn wing's load, for the same reason he gave back the wire rope: what happens when it breaks and he cannot mend it. He does correct Sorrel's drawing of his wing, a break lower and primaries lying the other way, and says the page may be shown, so people know what a fall does. On Friday Ferra buys one hand-length block of cloudstone from Veska's council at their steep price, for Bram's bench, without arguing, since Veska has lost his best buyer this month. Neris turns it in the light and says it is glass that is mostly air. The storekeeper will not say how it is made. Armand asks the storm readers once more and is told again he may watch, so he takes home two columns, the fleet's barometer against their sky.

Before dawn on Saturday, on the count, Emeric sends the current out through his hand and back without a break, which he can do only because the delve gives him its slow time. Duncan, who has taught the Current for years, sees for the first time that it goes somewhere he never imagined, and walks the ledge twice. Emeric sees the other half: the breath might teach this to people who will never have a delve. Neither says it.

On Saturday night's call, Harrold reports in his flat way that Weekes's sewer interceptor under the old capital is half done, a lord has sued over a trench at his door before Keller, who heard it and did not stop the works, and the first lamps on the market hall are lit from the ridge towers. He is visibly proud of the lamps. Then Corbin says he made Corrick the offer of a desk and paid authority and read Emeric's letter aloud, and Corrick said: "If my complaint becomes your wages, what happens to the complaint? You're offering me a desk." Corbin asks what he meant. Emeric does not know, and gives a reasonable answer that is wrong: ask him again when the Private Arms Act is before the Assembly; he will see its use then. His own word, "for," has been handed back to him, and he does not see it. Saban reports that he has Ferra's message: no guano this year, Milford's trial and the powder master's beds go without, and the bread-price argument has already started in the Port Town. "You'll want to be the one who tells Milford." After the call, Emeric sits with the set in his lap. Ferra sees him sit with it and knows why, and nobody else does.

Chapter 96: Within the Day

That Thursday at dusk, Liora comes to the ward with Tiel as her tongue. Her slate asks: what did you see? Emeric tells her: a lip and a mouth that did not close in the womb, early; not a curse, a seam that did not meet. We can close it, within the day. Imani says, "I can close it; he'll help," and Tiel laughs translating that. Liora writes that they said that three times. Imani explains why those healings failed, because the body rebuilt what it knew, and what is different: cut it right, then mend to the new shape. "And if it fails?" "Then you've lost a day." Liora wipes the slate and does not answer. In the same hour, at the landing ledge, the elders are refusing every one of Kaldo's offers. That night Dovan, the island council's keeper and her father, comes down himself and says no through Tiel: three times was enough, and not by gold eyes. Imani says it is Liora's decision, Kaldo will not ask again, and the door stays open. He does not argue.

On Friday, from first light to dusk, in the K Endeavor's ward under the shadowless lamp and ether, Liora lies at the door with Halle, her sister and the heir in her place, who came without asking and lies on the next cot as the template for the midline. The islands' senior healer stands gowned and watching while Imani explains every step through Tiel. Emeric works his lane, the Earth repair: the lip rotated and advanced, the ring muscle rejoined, the palate closed in layers with its muscle sling rebuilt, the wide map held and the mana fed and steadied. Imani works hers: bone grown into the gap in the gum, the nose rebuilt, the left ear drained and opened, the mirror of the right side, and the mending last, without scars. It takes about ten hours. She wakes to sound in her left ear before anything else. She looks in the mirror, touches one finger to her lip, then to her ear, asks for the slate, and leaves it blank. Her voice is not fixed in a day; the old habits remain. That night she flies up the lee face to her family's lodging. For a breath her father does not know her and takes her for Halle with a stranger. Her mother knows her by the laugh, still the old wet one. On the lowest stack, Duncan tells Emeric he is bleeding worse than at the spar, and does not ask why. On Saturday the councils' table half-watches the lee face, where the keeper's elder daughter sits on a launch ledge in daylight for the first time in seven years. Late, standing, Dovan asks Ferra what Kaldo charges. Ferra gives Imani's rule: Kaldo's own are covered, outsiders pay on a scale, and those who cannot pay are treated free. Dovan says then they will pay what Kaldo would charge a king. Ferra writes it down for Tuesday.

Chapter 97: Pressed

Before dawn on Sunday 8 November, on the watch, Emeric has every piece. Padding is mana held fixed in a shape to carry load; it stopped Duncan's staff on Wednesday. Cast stasis grips mana and holds it. The Current gathers held mana to the chest on the inhale. And from another world he remembers a shelf of stories about people who grow power like a crop. Pad mana with mana: close a seed of his own held mana inside a shell of more of it and press inward. He says it to Duncan in bad orc. Duncan, who has the breath but not stasis, says he cannot do the half that matters, and that he will sit there while Emeric tries.

At first light on the K Discovery's deck, Ferra asks whether pressing in this air breaks the promise to Saldis. Armand answers no. The promise covers the Band, the drums and anything new placed there. This is the air at the waterline that every one of them breathes, and no dragon keeps this sky. Ferra writes it into the promise's column with the reason.

On the lookout ledge that morning, Imani watches with her sight. The first seed fails. Emeric closes the seed and presses before the shell is round, and it slips out of his grip the way a wet seed slips between two fingers, back into his circulating mana, with a jolt he feels in his teeth and a nosebleed, the first sign of the road that leads to tracery. Imani looks for silver around the pit of his stomach and finds none. Her terms, said once: if she sees tracery, he stops, and he does not argue. He agrees. The second time he spins the flow as he presses, the way rolling dough between the palms makes it round, and the shell closes and stays. To his inner sense it is the size of a pea and as heavy as a stone, sitting in the pit of his stomach below where the Current gathers. To Imani's sight it is a bright weight that does not leak.

Then comes the day of the dough ball. He presses and takes in at the same time, all day, the ball growing denser and bigger together while the dense air fills the room each press leaves. It is mentally exhausting, and by noon he cannot hold a conversation. Duncan keeps the count for him when his own breath goes ragged, and Imani reads him every hour and writes it down. At dusk he makes the last press, and the shell folds the day's layers into itself and locks. He holds his full level as before, plus a core about equal to it, which gives back slowly through its gate at the rate his channels can take. It is storage, not output. He says it is a golem's heart, grown instead of cut. Armand, who has keyed a dozen golem hearts, sits down on the ledge.

At dusk Ferra watches him cast gravity on the core, and the envelope around him carries his body with it. He hangs a few feet off the lookout ledge and says nothing for a moment. Then he goes out over the open water beyond the ring, on the seaward side away from every stack, low, a few dozen feet off the swell, a slow mile out and back, gold-eyed in plain sight. Flight has always been gravity plus air for him, and air costs him three times over. This costs the weight and nothing else, and coming back he understands that the envelope is what the suit was for. A mile out, as the trade wind carries him gently downwind, he casts stasis on the field around the envelope and stops dead in the wind, the core holding his height, for the space of ten breaths while the wind goes round him. It thumps, and Armand, on the ledge, says "the pin" aloud. A man who can do that can stand in a storm. By Ward's order, a knight in a stealth helm keeps Emeric's heat in the grid from the K Discovery's rail as dusk greys the water, so a boat would know where to go if anything failed. It is not needed. For the first time on the voyage a flight costs nothing from the racks, and Ferra writes that down instead. Sorrel watches from the rail, grounded since October, and later asks whether a pilot could learn this. She is told the truth: years.

He does not fly back to the K Discovery, since the lagoon lies inside the ring among the stacks, too close to the terms, and Imani will not let a man who pressed all day fly twice. So he walks down between Ferra and Imani in the dark and, low, tells them where the idea came from: stories on his other world, a whole shelf of them, about people growing power like a crop. Imani says, "You read those?" Ferra laughs at him all the way down. He sleeps fourteen hours.

Meanwhile the elders leave Liora's healing out of their commentary on purpose. Tiel notices the absence, and when he asks, Ruvo says the bill is about a cliff. That night on the courier ledge the young say "the eyes that finish" for the first time. Tiel does not write it down.

At dusk, from the courier ledges on the outer face, Tiel watched the gold-eyed man go out over the sea. He has the winged ones' air sense, and what he felt was that the air did not move. At Corvan the man came out of the sky on wind; this time nothing in the air was pushed aside. Then he stopped in the wind, and the wind went round him, and nothing a winged one knows can do that without beating wings. It frightens him more than the valley did, and he cannot say why it should. He carries it to the elders that night. They do not charge a breach, because there was none; he kept their terms, beyond the ring and low, and Ruvo says so before the elder can say otherwise. They do not send the fleet away early, because the bill is two days from closed and they want it closed. Instead they add two sentences to the commentary going with Kaldo's letter: the gold-eyed king flew past our ring on the eighth day of the month, and the air did not move; he kept our terms. And: Kaldo's hulls and fliers pass our islands on the water, or not at all; the day one crosses our sky, every route we fly is closed to Kaldo's letters.

Chapter 98: One Letter

On Monday afternoon, 9 November, on the landing ledge, Tiel delivers the commentary and renders the attested copy into orc line by line, with Grau and Siv checking his orc beside him and Grau, whose Kaldo is now workable after four months aboard, carrying it to the room. It is hostile and careful. It names the falling sky and the Old Stack dug for Kaldo's sacks, and calls the airship at a nesting cliff useful and still unacceptable, counting first the forty the rain lift carried and then the nine the airship lifted. Then come the two sentences of Sunday night. Armand, keeper of the museum's list, thinks about where this paper will hang and who will read it. It goes home in Ferra's box, will be read in Kaldo after the king's word to his people, and the sentence about the gold-eyed king who flew without moving the air will be in every pulpit in Ostrel the week after. Through Grau he asks whether the home copy could carry the meaning without that sentence and the one after it. It is a fair worry from a decent man, and the wrong ask. Tiel refuses. The second sentence is the condition. A home copy without it would tell Kaldo's own people, who will build and fly Kaldo's airships, that they may cross the winged ones' sky. He will not render a copy that hides a condition of safe passage from the people it binds. He says it once, in orc, and Grau gives it to the room word for word. Ferra says it goes in whole. Armand writes in his register, under the letter's entry: asked to soften; refused; rightly. It is the first correction in his own hand of something he asked for. The paper goes into Ferra's box with the sealed diaries, commentary attached.

That evening in the K Endeavor's chart room, Ward, Arthur, Mira, Bren in a chair on light duty since that morning, two of the Crestless, Maeve, Armand, Duncan, Ferra and Imani gather, with Runa at the door where she has been told to stay and does not. Ward asks exactly what happened on that ledge. Emeric says, "It's a cultivation world." Ward asks what that is supposed to mean. Ferra and Imani look at each other, and Ferra has to look at the chart table to keep her face. Emeric cannot cite anything, so he uses the word the way Kaldo already uses it for Milford's fields: you do not grow mana by having it; you cultivate it like a field. Work it every day, take in what the air gives, press it, store it, and one day spend the harvest. Then he explains what a man with a day-old core can honestly say. It is storage, not pipes; it does not make anyone cast harder, it makes them last longer. Padding was stasis all along, so every knight who pads has done the first step without the name. There is the dough ball. The mind is the brake; one full day of it put him to bed for fourteen hours. The core is shut. He guesses, and says it is a guess, that a deep enough core might feed a bodily rebuild, the way the dome fed the stone-eaters' sleep. Imani says it stays a guess until she has watched one for longer than a day, and that children wait. Ward asks the cost first: a day for Emeric and fourteen hours' sleep, and attention every day for years for anyone who wants a core worth having. Only then does Ward ask what it gives.

Arthur's group wants to start that night, Bren loudest from his chair. Three people say no for their own reasons. Imani: nobody presses a first core without a reader beside them, she is the only reader, and she has read exactly one core for exactly one day, so she will watch his for a week before reading anyone else's. Emeric: he will not teach what he cannot yet name the risks of. Duncan: the Current is his to teach, and he has not said to whom. Arthur sulks; Mira says she has waited longer for worse. The class waits until the fleet climbs out of Saldis's sky. Maeve is quiet through all of it, and then asks, "Can theirs learn it?" She means the Church's knights. Emeric says anyone with a teacher can. Silence. Ward says, "Then nobody teaches them." Duncan, most shaken of all, having found the Current thirty years ago without ever knowing where it went, only to watch a gold-eyed king from the old land take it further in a week, says something short and unkind in Westgone, which Armand understands and does not translate. From the door, Runa asks, "Can I?" Emeric says, "Later. I'll teach you myself."

That night at the K Discovery's rail, with Ward and Ferra sent for before Emeric answers, Duncan asks for a berth home with the fleet. He says plainly what makes him go. His house's stories of the old land are the rite and the war, and the Westgone have kept their distance for two thousand years on the strength of them; a march-warden walks toward what frightens him until he knows its size, which is why his council sent him. And then the disagreement that decides it: "You'll teach it wrong." Emeric is going to teach the Current to a kingdom of thin-born people, and Duncan is the only one alive who has seen what it does to a warden who overdoes it, so he does not trust Emeric to know when to stop other people. He also says what he gets, because it sounds like self-interest and is: stasis, which only Emeric can teach, and in time a core of his own, filled on the dense side and carried into the thin march to answer his patrols' short shifts. He does not mention the steward. Ferra writes it in her own book anyway. The cost is real: his march short a seasoned warden through the winter, his tally wages lost, months among the people his house left, and the risk that his council reads the berth as going over to them. That night he writes to his council, carried north by the couriers at his own coin.

Ferra writes his terms, Ward argues one, and Emeric decides it. Duncan is a guest, not crew, with no watch bill, no oath, no line room and no keyed rooms. His letters go unopened. Ward wants to read them under his secrecy rule; Ferra says they are a sworn man's report to his own council and reading them would make him a prisoner. Emeric decides for Ferra, with Ward's rule kept the other way round: what he is not shown, he cannot write. Ward accepts it and does not like it. Duncan has a way back at Kaldo's cost, the right to leave any day, and the right to say what he likes about Kaldo in public. The Current stays his: it is taught under his name and the Westgone's wherever Kaldo teaches it, and he decides whom he teaches. Kaldo teaches him stasis freely. Ferra offers him a numbered diary, and he says he keeps a warden's log and will go on keeping it. She writes the refusal down. After the others leave, Ward talks with him at the rail, one old soldier to another, about nothing much: the weather, the crossing, how a staff does in a ship's passage. He has decided to like him and will not say so for a month.

On Tuesday morning the bill is signed on the landing ledge, Emeric signing for Kaldo, Ferra witnessing, Tiel reading each paper aloud in orc, and Dovan as the island council's keeper, Ruvo and Veska signing for theirs. The bond, about the lot's full price in bar iron and cloth from the expedition chest, is held in a sealed chest under the keeper, and the Nestwardens' inspectors draw on it for their costs, so a forfeited half and a bond together cost Kaldo more than the guano would have. The provisional inspection agreement means Kaldo buys guano only in lots carrying the Nestwardens' mark, set on the cliff by their inspector; their inspectors may refuse any lot without appeal; Kaldo's clause is rewritten in every contract from "ledges open under the Nestwardens' law" to "carrying the Nestwardens' mark"; Ferra's clerks will check the mark at home against the Nestwardens' seal register; and the agreement stands until Kaldo's Assembly ratifies it or either side ends it. For the restoration, Ruvo has chosen: the Old Stack will be rested for good, no digging and no carving, its north face left as the place of the dead, with a new walk and catch-nets cut on the lee face of the stack beside it so that the households who can move have somewhere to go. Kaldo pays the climbers' dry-season wages as far as the chest allows, with no Kaldo steel and no dust grout in the work, by Ruvo's word; Bram does not offer. Ruvo asks, "And the rest?" Emeric says that is his Assembly's; he will ask, and he may lose. Ruvo says then they will know what his Assembly is worth. The dug lot stays on the wharf under a Nestwarden's seal while the councils argue whose it is, Kaldo's mark struck through by Ferra's own brush. Ari, as the agreement's first inspector, marks one sack from a ledge open under her people's law, the first lot ever to carry the Nestwardens' mark, bought at the traders' price with the levy on top for Milford's soil test of the agronomist's point. She does not smile. It is her family's ledge's work she is doing.

Dovan's name is on the bond, and the keeper's payment for Liora's surgery at a king's rate goes on its own line beside it, not inside it, so nobody can say the minimum was bought; Ruvo checks. Liora chooses to go to Kaldo to learn Imani's work, but not from here, because the elders say no one of theirs rides a human ship. She will fly to Aumere on her own wings and board at the gate. Her father writes on her slate that she has lived seven years under a roof and he will not put her under his. Halle stays heir, and nothing resolves it. At the leaving Liora is on the Old Stack's crown in daylight, not at the rail.

Ferra asks for a landing field, as at every stop, and the elder answers, with Ruvo not contradicting: no. Their sky was the one that fell. It is recorded as an answer: ask again only if the young ever have the say. The crates take one line: sealed storm-honey comb for Maren and the museum but no live hives, by Imani's quarantine; the landing gardens' wind-dwarfed bean seed; down-felt for Bram's suit liners; and the one marked sack. The seed hand labels the bean packets and shows Emeric the label without being asked, the second thing he has said to him since the farm, if it counts as saying. Before dawn Zale goes forty feet down to the lagoon's rock shelf, frees the twelve gravity anchors, dials each to neutral, and the K Endeavor's crane brings them up while Bram counts them into the hold. Zale has Aumere on his mind and does not hide it.

The seventh gift set goes to Tiel, as a person and not the council, since the elders would refuse it as more of Kaldo's "everything else," and Ruvo or Ari could not take it without making their roles look bought. Ferra flags it once: giving a telephone to a courier is giving him the thing that ends his people's trade. Emeric gives it anyway, because this one rings only the fleet, and because Tiel wrote both accounts. It gives the inspection agreement a fast channel: when an inspector refuses a lot, Tiel can ring Ferra the same day instead of sending a courier for three weeks. Ruvo allows it, because it serves her inspectors and not Kaldo. Sealed brass is too heavy to fly with, so Tiel carries it up the walk on foot, the long way, the way the young and the hurt go. Thirteen sets remain.

The hulls go out of the lagoon mouth on the water. Sefa and a handful of the young fly beside them, unarmed, low over the swell, to the edge of the ring, and turn back. The elders do not come out. On 24 October forty came out with javelins. Duncan stands at the K Discovery's rail with his staff across his shoulders, breathing the count, watching the stacks drop astern. He has never been aboard anything that can lift, and it is not lifting. As the stacks fall behind, Ferra rings the set from the K Discovery's deck, and it is answered, in orc, from a ledge on the outer face of a stack none of them is allowed to fly over.

In the chart room that Monday evening, Emeric slips the cuff off and sets it on the table. It lifts and goes round the lamp sideways and back to his wrist, on the same cast that moves his core, not along the line from his hand as any pull must. At that range it costs him almost nothing; further away costs more. Lit, he says, it is the loudest thing on the ship to him and Armand would feel it from the deck; quiet, it reads like dead metal and he has to be closer to find it. Ward calls it a knife that comes round corners. Emeric tells him what stops it: a stranger's shell, a dead zone, anyone with a sense who feels the draft, stacked stasis, and iron between them. Two at once is a dual hold, three is high mage work. Ward writes nothing down.

Emeric has a mana core. Taught the Returning Current by Duncan Hale and combining it with padding, stasis and the delve, he pressed a seed of his own mana inside a shell of more of it, failed once with a nosebleed, then spun it and locked it after a full day of pressing. It is storage, not output, roughly doubling what he holds. His first core flight went out over open water low and cost nothing from the racks, and he learned to pin himself in place in the wind. He has told the crew "It's a cultivation world," Ward has ruled that nobody teaches it to the Church's knights, and the class waits until Imani has watched his core for a week and the fleet has climbed out of Saldis's sky. Runa has been promised he will teach her himself.

Stop 5 is closed with a bill paid, not a friendship made. The bond, the inspection agreement with its new mark clause, and the restoration share are signed; anything larger waits on Kaldo's Assembly, which may refuse. One letter answering the Leaving Terms charge will go to every door the couriers serve, with the elders' hostile commentary attached, including the condition that Kaldo's hulls and fliers pass the islands on the water or not at all. Tiel refused to soften it, and it is in Ferra's box whole. The field was refused, everything else was refused, and one cloudstone sample was bought. The seventh gift set is with Tiel.

Duncan Hale is aboard as a guest, his letters unopened, his Current his own to teach, a man who fears Ostrellin's humans and has decided Emeric will teach his breath wrong without him; he and the steward Hale have yet to meet. Liora, healed, will fly to Aumere to board at the gate and learn Imani's work, while her elders keep her healing out of their record and the young call Kaldo "the eyes that finish." At home, Corrick's refusal of a desk has reached Emeric, who does not yet understand it, and the bread-price argument has begun over the lost guano. The fleet sails for Aumere on the water with seven unlaunched drums, thirteen gift sets and about 78 tonnes of food, the promise to Saldis still kept and her summons next.

This batch gives Emeric a new kind of strength and, in the same week, a people who will not let that strength cross their sky, and it puts beside him the one man who found the root of it and does not trust him to use it well.
