Chapter 7 - The Discharged Debt

Keep whatever conclusions you draw from that to yourself, until you've understood why. Emeric turned the thought over, and the crystal with it. He'd had the thing out of his pocket for an hour now, more habit than intent. Either the baron genuinely has a reason for a barony this thin on its margins, or he's hoping I'll read guilt as incompetence. What are you hiding, Baron? He started sorting the same way he'd sorted the parish ledgers months back. Category first, structure before content. Revenue in one pile, expenditure in another, then finer: tithe remittance, garrison pay, castle upkeep, procurement. And one small pile that kept growing faster than it had any business growing. Debts forgiven. Obligations discharged.

Debt didn't usually just evaporate. Most of it got collected. What didn't get collected got written down as a loss, on the books, traceable. A cluster of quiet write-offs stood out precisely because it was built not to. The Control Rune warmed against his fingers without him deciding to hold it tighter, and the ledger data in front of him resolved into something cleaner. The same layering sensation from the nights alone with it, structure surfacing underneath what his eyes were already reading.

It didn't tell him anything the pages didn't already say. It let him hold all of it at once, sorted and filtered and searchable in a way his hands couldn't manage fast enough on their own. Discharged debts by date instead of by page number. That was the entire trick. Speed, not insight. Ferra's head for numbers wasn't magic either. It was just faster than the method everyone else was stuck with.

Sorted by date, one entry landed exactly where he didn't want it.

A debt, discharged in full, dated to the same week the carriage had been ambushed on the road into Kaldo.

"No fucking way." He said it out loud, to an empty room, and read the line again in case the date changed on a second pass. It didn't. "Baron. Baron, baron, baron. What are the odds you're just unlucky?"

He had no name on the other end of that debt. Only a date that lined up too well to be nothing. That wasn't proof. It wasn't even a lead he could hand to anyone. It was a coincidence with a shape, and a coincidence with a shape means nothing until there's a second one standing next to it to lean on. He went to Bram's with the documents in tow. By now the whole thing had turned ritual. Emeric left with Rylan, Ward followed without being seen, and everyone pretended the arrangement was still a secret.

Ferra took the ledgers off him without comment, the way Bram used to take a cracked blade, like the problem was the only thing in the room worth acknowledging. She didn't sort by category the way he had. She sorted by hand. The actual handwriting, entry cross-referenced against entry, until she found what she was after.

"This one." She turned the page toward him and tapped the line he'd already flagged. "Whoever wrote this wasn't hiding the amount. They were hiding who it went to."

The name on the entry was a merchant house, plain as anything, except the ink sat a shade off from the entries around it. Added later. Over something scraped away first.

"Somebody wanted a name here badly enough to write one in after the fact. That's not sloppy bookkeeping. That's covering a gap."

"Can you get under it? What was there before?"

"Given time." She didn't look up. "This isn't the only page that's been touched. Give me the rest of the quarter, not just this line."

It wasn't a name yet. It was a shape with a name-sized hole in it, which still beat what he'd had that morning. Tam was on the door when they came for Petram, same as every rotation since Ward set the schedule. He didn't ask why twice. He stepped aside the way he had the first time, the asking having been settled somewhere Emeric wasn't present for. Petram looked worse than he had a week ago and better than he had any right to. Imani had been in to see him twice, which was more than the man had earned and exactly as much as she gave anybody. He didn't waste breath pretending not to know why they'd come.

"You're not here to kill me. You'd have done that already."

"No."

"Then you want a name." Flat, working out the shape of the trade before either of them put terms on it. "You know I can't give you the one you actually want. I never met the man. I met the one he sent."

"Give me that one, then."

"Cobb." He said it like a man handing over the last coin in his pocket.

A courier's name. Somebody who moved between towns often enough that nobody thought twice about him turning up in any of them. Tam, close enough to the bars to be doing his job and not close enough to be obviously listening, went very still on the last part of it. Emeric caught it and said nothing. Neither did Petram, though the look he gave the guard said he'd caught it too, and had filed it as a problem for later, the way a man in a cell files everything as a problem for later.

Word came before dinner. Tam hadn't shown up to relieve the evening rotation. They found him near the mill road, close enough to town that it read on paper like exactly the kind of opportunistic violence a barony this poor and this desperate produced with grim regularity. Nothing missing that a robbery wouldn't have taken. Nothing pointing anywhere.

Imani looked at him because somebody had to. One strike, from behind, by a hand that knew where to put it. She said that much and then said she wasn't going to say more, because more than that wasn't hers to say and she'd watched what happened in this town to people who guessed out loud.

Except the timing, which pointed everywhere. Emeric stood out in the cold by the guardhouse a long moment before he trusted himself to speak.

"He's gonna regret that."

He didn't say Wexler's name. There was no need. Underneath the anger, cold and separate from it, the engineer's part of his mind was already doing what it always did, sorting the loss into what it cost and what it proved. Tam had heard enough to be worth killing for. Which meant the name Petram gave up was real, and worth more than Emeric had let himself hope an hour ago. None of that made it easier to walk past the well near the burial ground afterward. Rylan slowed without being asked, looking down the same street Emeric was.

"Sela's boy first," he said, more to the street than to Emeric. "Now this."

He didn't explain and didn't need to. Emeric filed the name next to everything else that had cost the same family twice, for a war that had never technically declared her the enemy either time. The rage came back sharper on the walk up, and it took him a second to place where it was actually aimed. Not at Wexler. At the version of himself from that morning who'd walked past Tam without once asking his name, without once thinking to ask who'd been posted over the only piece of leverage that mattered. He noticed the blood on his palm at the gatehouse, and couldn't say when he'd made a fist.
