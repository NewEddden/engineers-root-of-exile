Book 3, Batch 7: The Year They Have

Chapters 61–72 | Friday 1 September – Sunday 17 December, Year 19

The courts have spoken. Pryor, Sayer and Sir Edwin Hartley's killer were hanged at Kestle on the last day of August, while Imani signed passes at the foot of the pass road. Corrick and Kezia are at Turnstone until next July and Gethin until January. The Rule on Royal Force is standing law, the Isle's hollow is sealed under two keys, and the king refused a pardon to the man he admires. The bridge machine has run its first program and the Mark 1's engine its first four minutes. The young of the Isle mother have not grown past the first stage, and the beast line lapses in October unless the Assembly renews it.

This batch is the year the old order is given by the king's own charter. The Titles Act, which ends hereditary right to command, try, toll and tithe, passes its first vote, and the hereditary council takes the one year's delay that Emeric himself wrote in to win two hundred and twelve oaths. He cannot hurry it. Around that slow year, Kaldo builds: warning bands on the coast, rescue golems in a mine, an airfield lit, a jet named, relay drums over the kingdom, a class of nurses learning to feel each other's pulse, and a cap that can hear a thought of a closed hand.

The beasts and the Church keep pressing at the edges. A young follows a barge into Kaldo's bay and is pinned alive, then killed by the law because the law says so. A man who drank the beasts' blood for months dies under a shut case and rises on the third day. A grove close is burned by a dead zone in the night. And an old sickness from badly sealed jars puts seven people in Brask into stillness, breathing only by other people's hands, for weeks.

Chapter 61: The Bill

On Friday 1 September, the morning after the hanging, through Ferra's eyes, it is day 343 of the count, and her column under Kaldo's own sky gets its twenty-first entry, with a new line in the air column: higher inside the closes. On Monday 4 September Halverne's member tables the Titles Act in the Assembly, and what it does is read into the record. It ends hereditary legal title to command, try, toll and tithe and closes the Arms Act's remaining exceptions. The town watches' public licences run their full term, so no security is pulled. Hereditary civil courts sit until each district's elected court sits. Lawful tolls are bought out over twenty years. The hearing is set.

From that Monday, through Mother Sabbe's eyes, the warning band goes onto wrists, offered to the Islanders under the Compact, her people first. It is the pager band's receiver in volume, receive-only, with no shield, no identity and no payment, and three buzz patterns: long, long for a beast ashore; short, short, short for fire; and one long for the Shell. It is free to the wearer, paid from the Assembly's coast line. She takes hers from the knights' clerk and asks what it says when it is not buzzing. Nothing, she is told, which she approves of. Then the Claim's crews, the March's tower watches and Kaldo's harbour and coast get theirs. Ten shore sentinels go up, low stone-and-steel posts the height of a man, each with a works stone that decides one thing, whether that is a beast, and rings the warning bands on its stretch: six on the Isle's grey beaches, four of them on her coast, and four on Kaldo's coast, two at the bay mouth. Mother Sabbe still carries her key to the hollow.

On Saturday 9 September, on Stannary's east stope, the one that killed nine, through Ward's eyes, four of the twelve rescue golems walk into a staged fall made of the old stope's own rubble, re-propped. A crew of three drives them by hand on cables from the drift mouth first, then lets them work on their stones, each deciding where to dig, what to brace and when to stop. They stop twice where the crew would have kept going. The second stop is right: a slab is hanging that the crew had not seen. The miners watching are silent. Ward writes "not yet a rescue" on the drill sheet, because nobody was under it.

Chapter 62: Whose Coin

From Monday 18 to Wednesday 20 September the Assembly's long room hears the Titles Act: tenant delegates; a Penhallow steward against his own overlord; a Tarrowmede advocate for the hereditary courts, which are cheap, local and fast, and he is right about fast; and the March reform committee, Agatha Vell among them, asking for guaranteed seats and a referendum on the March's civil franchises, which the bill does not give them yet. On Tuesday, through Tom Ashlock's eyes, he is called because the hearing wants to know what the country thinks of the note. He is for it, because it is the same at every counter and no lord prints it, and against the slow country redemption, because a county office open one day in four made him choose in February between his hands and a stranger's paper. Asked what saved his Friday, he says a bank bought his invoice, and the member asks which. Vantic's House. He had not known it was hers. He says on the record that he would still rather the counties paid on time than have to be saved, and that is the sentence Kaldo News prints.

On Friday 22 September, through Vantic's eyes, the Issue Board answers the hearing by moving every county office to weekly redemption from Monday 2 October, on the bridge machine's roster, each office a fixed day a week with the coin carriage between them drawn up once and checked by hand. Every county office gets one of Ottoline's desk calculators for the week's tally, so the clerks stop adding on paper. Vantic thinks the House is better off without being needed. That evening, through Corbin's eyes, the Titles Act passes its first vote, printed by name, with Tarrowmede's members and the March's against, the March because its concession is not in it. Corbin says for the crown that the crown supports the bill and will say nothing more until the council has sat, because anything it says now will be read as pressure on the council.

Chapter 63: The Year They Have

From Saturday 23 to Thursday 28 September, through Hester's eyes, Bram lights the Grayrock Forge on a works stone of its own, and its first job is the farm line's cold-car frames for Milford. At Milford the sun house is started, an arched glass hall with its footings and first three ribs, carousel towers to go down its length, with Hollis in charge. Every water board on the bench fields has its controller now, Linus's programmable box that opens the drip valves on the soil blocks' readings and logs them by radio to the farm office, while the floats still shut the valves if the box goes quiet. The meat ration's first autumn brings a culling glut bigger than the cold store, and Hester solves it with a salting and tinning shed in a week. Emeric's only contribution is a letter saying the ammonia plant will take a second compressor, which Linus already knew.

On Thursday 28 September, in the lords' hall in Randillia City, the hereditary council sits. It is the old council of lords and heirs, kept by the Charter with one power: it may delay any bill touching hereditary right by one year from its resolution, and cannot amend or veto. Lord Cassel speaks for the court families and the Duke of Tarrowmede moves the delay. It carries. The Titles Act cannot pass its final vote before Thursday 27 September next year. On Friday, at the castle, through Emeric's eyes, he asks Ferra whether the year can be shortened. She asks who wrote it. He did, years ago, as the price of two hundred and twelve lords swearing in one afternoon, and called it cheap at the time. Keller, asked by Corbin's letter the same afternoon, answers in two lines that the Charter means what it says. Emeric names the cost once to Ferra: a year of the old courts trying tenants in districts that have already voted to end them. Ferra adds: and a year for the March to earn its seats. He cannot hurry it and does not try. The council spends its year on lawsuits in Keller's court, the first, filed on 9 October, arguing that twenty years' compensation is a taking at a discount and quoting Pryor's Charter brief by the page, and on custom ordinances, the first being Tarrowmede's, confirming its hereditary courts' fees as ancient custom. Keller lists them for the spring. Wystan says he will stand for the eastern hills when the widening comes, which is now a year off.

That Friday, in the museum's winged hall, through Liora's eyes, two letters come from the isles by Aumere's couriers. Her sister Halle's, in their mother's dictation and her own hand, says the naming elders read Liora's Hollow Night letter at the naming ledge and said that he finishes things, and now they have seen what he finishes. They quoted her honesty; they did not need to lie. The foreign name, the gold-eyed demon king of Kaldo, has reached the stacks by orc traders. The young are split between "the eyes that finish" and "the falling sky." There is changeling talk: some say the girl who came home was not her, since nobody knew her face, not even her father, and Halle answers it, and their mother answers it with a laugh, and it does not go away. Dovan's seat on the island council is challenged over his daughter and his king's-rate payment. The senior healer's own letter says she has opened a lip on a fisher's boy at the net station on the Kaldo pattern, with her own hands at her own table, and the elders cannot forbid it because it is hers. Liora reads her sister's letter twice, and the second time she reads it aloud to the empty hall.

At Milford, through Hester's eyes, the first forced cotton field from the desert's long-staple seed is picked in September. At the castle Emeric wants the seed pressed for oil, and Imani says no and why; he argues, she is right, the gin goes under the food line, and Ferra writes the cost. He says once that he did not see it.

Chapter 64: Long, Long

On Tuesday 3 October, about eleven at night, through Ward's eyes, a young of about one point eight metres comes into Kaldo's bay on the flood behind a shard barge from the Claim, riding the barge's wake the way gulls ride a plough, after the shard in the hold. The bay-mouth sentinel rings long, long, and two thousand wrists along the coast buzz at once; the harbour's knights, the fishing crews and the bay's houses all wake, and the watchman's radio confirms it in the same minute. The knights put night glass in their visors. In the harbour fort's control room the Harbor Board's two operators fire two whirlpool mines in the pattern the chart calls shoal, impellers first, then the stones. The eddies do not touch the young; they turn the water under it and shove it onto the sand off the north beach, away from the moored fishing boats. It is the first use, and nobody is hurt.

Before dawn on Wednesday, on the shoal, through Tess's eyes, it is stranded and alive, thrashing at the knights' lamps. Tess wants it kept: a live young in a shut pen is the only way to learn what its own kind's crystal does to a wound, and nobody has ever had one. Ward sends to the Inspector of Sealing, who is on the coast that week. The Inspector reads the beast line's nest clause as covering any living beast held by anyone, since a nest is wherever one is kept alive, the crown's knights included, because that is how he would read it for a fisherman. Ward kills it on the shoal at first light, by the book, and the carcass goes to the harbour's iron store under the Inspector's tag. Emeric agrees with the reading and tells Tess so, and she does not forgive him till lunch. It was one of the young nobody had ever seen.

That morning, on Kaldo's quay, through Liora's eyes, the case in the iron store is open for the Inspector's tag. Two gold-veined eyes, gold cracking through grey and stopping short. Her hand goes to her lip before she can stop it. She does not write home that night.

Chapter 65: Still

On Saturday 30 September, at the harvest fair in Brask, a widow's stall sells home-sealed jars of beans and pork in brine, put up in August without boiling long enough, and fourteen people eat from the same batch. From Monday to Wednesday the first ones go still: drooping lids, double sight, a tongue that will not work, then the arms. The families call it a curse or a sleeping sickness and keep them home, because they plainly breathe and look. Three die at home when their breathing fails. On Wednesday Brask's reeve radios Kaldo's hospital, because a third family has the same thing.

On Thursday 5 October, in Brask's guildhall cleared for beds, through Emeric's eyes, he recognises it from what he knows privately: a poison a soil germ makes in badly sealed jars. Aloud he says only what a doctor would say: it came from one batch of food, everyone ate the same thing, it is not catching, and they can hear every word. He tells the families to talk to them. One man stops crying over his wife, Jenna Kitto, and starts telling her the price of oats, which is the first good thing in the room.

Through Imani's eyes, seven of the eleven living cannot breathe enough on their own. A tube goes into each windpipe, and Kaldo's medics breathe for them on hand bellows in shifts, a breath every five seconds, for weeks. Once each is steady, air stones move the air gently, with the bellows kept at every bed as the fallback. Imani speeds the mending of the nerve endings and says plainly she cannot make weeks into days. Bettany starts an antitoxin in two horses from the jars' poison made harmless first; it will not be ready for these patients, and will be for the next.

On Monday 16 October, in the March reeve's hall at the foot of the pass, through Mother Brannagh's eyes, the Medical Pass Office issues its first notice that is not about a pass: boil home-sealed beans, meat and fish for a full ten minutes before eating, and seal low-acid food only by pressure at the works or by the Office's printed method. Brannagh asks why it is theirs, and Imani says because the Office is the only body in the kingdom with a March midwife and a crown knight at the same table, and this is a rule both have to carry home. Kaldo News prints the jar rule under the Office's five names, and not the king's.

To prove the jars, Bettany grows their contents on plates of seaweed jelly from the Rim's red weed, sealed and warm beside the bellows rota. The plates from the badly sealed jars grow and the good ones do not, so the jar rule goes out on the Office's name with a reason anyone can check.

Chapter 66: The Line Renewed

On Monday 9 October the hereditary council's first lawsuit and first custom ordinance are listed in Keller's court. On Wednesday 11 October, through Corbin's eyes, the Assembly asks the question the beast line asked of itself a year ago: did the open licence work? The Inspector of Sealing, the Saltreach harbourmaster, reads the record: three sealing houses, the Claim's iron shed, the crown's Saltreach house, and the Saltreach chandlers' guild with Wat Greave its sealer; no part missing from any licensed house's custody book; the formula price reviewed four times; the families' fund and its one claim. Then the coast's evidence: two thousand warning bands, the sentinels, and eight days ago the bay, where the bands rang, the mines turned the water, and nobody was hurt. The king's letter, read by Corbin, asks for a renewal for three years and a standing licence for the crown to keep live beasts for study.

Through Merryn Pellow's eyes from the gallery, she hears her own name read in the Register's line and counts the young she has not found. The Assembly amends both of the king's asks: renewed for one year, to Saturday 13 October next year; a report to the Assembly every quarter by the Inspector; and instead of a standing licence, one licensed research pen, a single live beast at a time, at the Claim, in a shut pen under the Inspector's seal, its existence printed. Corbin votes for it. The king obeys it.

On Saturday 14 October, at the Sky Yard, through Sorrel's eyes, the Field is finished: the last stretch of concrete cured, the lamp line lit along both edges and the thresholds, a lit windsock, the brick tower finished with Ambrose's radio set in it, a fire tender parked by the apron, and the fuel store bunded and set well away from the sheds. Every approach comes in over the farms and the Mere. Sorrel walks the whole two kilometres of lit concrete at dusk with a lamp and will not let anyone call it open until she is done. On the line room's board, the site station gives its first evening check from the dark.

Chapter 67: Whole

On Tuesday 17 October, through Vantic's eyes, her correspondent in Velmoor's court writes that a court jeweller is offering whole outside shard to the treasury's buyers, and that the price of a fist has dropped by a third in a week. She sees it first and sends it to Saban the same hour. On Friday, through Saban's eyes, his clerks at Kaldo's customs shed match the Aumere relief hull's manifest and leave roster to the jeweller's dates, and the hand who was on leave at the border on the right days is Elwin Gorran, the Brask miller's younger brother. The receiver Keller's court appointed over Tullio's seized crown book called the miller's loan at thirty days in September, lawfully, for the book's creditors, and the wheel-house goes at the end of October unless the miller pays. Elwin bought a whole fist of outside shard at Aumere's market for the price of a good tool, carried it home in his sea chest past the manifest, and sold it in Velmoor. He is found, not caught: he hands over the rest of the money and the jeweller's receipt and asks whether it is enough for the wheel. It is, almost. He is charged with an undeclared import and a sale abroad without the Assembly's leave, under the ordinary customs law, and a shard schedule is published. Short of eyes in Velmoor's court, Saban asks the king whether he could put the squirrel in a window. Emeric gives the limits, a sentence each: only an animal he has read by touch and calmed, a few hundred yards at most, his own body blind and deaf the whole time, and a squirrel does not know a ledger from a leaf. Saban says he will keep his clerks. On Wednesday 25 October, from the gallery, Saban watches the Assembly vote the Mage Corps as a budget line, not a royal order: a paid service of working mages, hired, never conscripted, its first intake forty-one of the capital's lift and utility mages, freed as Weekes's stone cradles take the lift hulls' weight. Lord Cassel objects that the crown is buying away the houses' mages with the realm's money.

On Monday 23 October Vantic buys the miller's note from the receiver at the market price and writes it to ten years, with her own money, not the House's, because it was her brother's loan and her correspondent's letter that put Elwin in a dock. It is not a favour from the crown; it is a claim bought, like Tom's. She tells nobody. The miller finds out from the receiver's clerk and writes her a letter he rewrites four times.

On Wednesday 18 October, on Tess's floor, the first lot of the whole-land relay drums stands ready: twelve drums, counted on the floor's board in a separate column, "build-out 12", apart from the voyage spares. Each has a silvered mirror, two trim vanes, a decider written to drain itself to zero if the pin is lost or the line room orders it, and a glass hammer, a spring hammer that smashes the mirror if the pin goes. To everyone outside the six and the line room, the drums are talk and a fix and nothing more. Tess hits a scrap mirror with the glass hammer for the third time because Dov asked her to, and Dov was right.

Elder Vosse sits in the gallery with two of the tower's masters. The tower does not object. She asks the clerk for the Corps' pay scale, and the next week twelve of the tower's own apply to the first intake. Saban writes that the oldest school of mages in Ostrellin has just voted with its feet, and that nobody on the floor noticed.

Chapter 68: Pity

In early October Rowena gets three letters at Emberfell: Harrold's, asking her properly for Hollow Night; Ward's, telling her to come and see what they have been doing on Saturdays; and Maeve's, careful about Saturdays, which Rowena notices before she notices Ward's. She comes in on Friday 27 October. On Saturday, at the Cradle on South Sentinel, through Rowena's eyes, she takes the oath first. Then she sees the Bulwark waist-deep in the bay with Wren in the crown, a year after its first walk, the proving floor, and on the berth wall the drawings for Ward's Bastion and another machine. She asks what it does on one leg, how fast it turns, and what a hit feels like. Wren says dull pressure, not pain. Rowena says that is a pity; pain teaches. She wants one. There is a line, she is told. Who is at the front? Ward is. Then she is behind him; that will be new.

On Monday 30 October, at the castle, Emeric says once why she will have one: she is the best knight he has crossed blades with, and the line has a wall, a champion, a gun and a shield, and nobody fast. Bram gives his estimate, Ferra says yes on the purse, the core will be bought at the beast line's formula price like anyone's, and the sworn panel takes note. Not this year. She has waited longer for less. Her name goes on the build list after Ward's, and Bram's office starts the frame's drawings. Nobody names the machine aloud.

On Tuesday 31 October, Hollow Night, through Imani's eyes, there are fewer grey-pup costumes in Kaldo's lanes than last year, because the young have killed people this year and the mothers have views. There are more slimes, and one child goes as a rescue golem with a cardboard head who stops twice at every door to decide. Ward has a bowl. Rowena, at Harrold's elbow, is given a turnip to carry and carries it. In the early evening the squirrel September comes down the castle pear while Runa is out with a bag, and Imani says it is the oldest squirrel she has heard of, with no grey in it, and asks to enter it in Bettany's register beside the dense-fed rats. Emeric asks how long it will live. She does not give a number; it is not immortal, and the register will say when. Late in the castle hall, Harrold brings three dukes' letters asking for the citizen's band for their miners and coasts, after the Stannary miners went first and the warning bands rang in Kaldo's bay. The answer is year three, next year, in the queue.

That night in the museum's winged hall, two years to the night from the lagoon's lanterns, through Liora's eyes, she writes home her answer. It was not the eyes. It was a woman with a knife who knew where everything goes, and a man who held the whole of her face in his head so the woman could cut. She does not know what the beasts are and will not say she does. It is not a defence of the king; it is a refusal to let her face be used by either side. She writes it on her slate first, and then says the sentence aloud, once, in the empty hall, to hear her own voice say it.

On Saturday 28 October, in the knights' yard, Ward asks Rowena to spar the girl, because he can't make her change her favourite move and Rowena once made his king change his. The same opening works on Runa three times. On the fourth she counters it. On the fifth Rowena changes it and Runa is on the sand. She never lands a winning touch, and Rowena calls it the best session she's watched in a year. She leaves Runa a drill by letter: expect the trick to change.

Chapter 69: The Third Day

On Thursday 2 November, at the Claim's sealing shed on the Isle, a shut case comes off its sling and Gil Haverty is under it. He dies at once. His sister is sent for from Saltreach, and his body goes into the Claim's cold store to wait for her, as the Claim does for every death. At dawn on Sunday 5 November, the third day, the store's watchman hears something. The body moves. What rises is small and wrong, a hatchling grown out of a man everyone knew, grey crystal laid through him, its eyes gold-veined over grey with a thin dull ring of brown at the rim, Gil's own colour. The Claim's watch kills it with rifles at the door, and it crumbles toward dust within the hour. Ward flies over by mid-morning and burns what is left. Gil's sister arrives at noon.

On Sunday afternoon, through Imani's eyes, under his bunk she finds a pup flask from the sealing shed and a notebook of doses: a small cup every two weeks since the end of May, five months. That is why his readings rose when nobody else's did. He wanted his back to stop hurting and his strength to come back, and both had. On Tuesday, in the hospital's back room in Kaldo, she holds a sealed vial of Gil's blood near Mochi on Emeric's shoulder, and Mochi goes flat, cold-coloured and still, as at the guard's carcass. A vial of an ordinary patient's blood does nothing. It is a test, not proof, and she adds it to the register. From Monday she has dose-matched rats from Bettany's series killed at set loads and intervals to see which rise. The November couriers bring word that the island council kept Dovan in his seat by one vote, sending his payment to the elders' audit.

Chapter 70: Fast Enough

On Monday 6 November, in the hospital's teaching room, through the eyes of a nurse named Mabyn Hoskin, Imani's first readers' class meets: twelve nurses. First comes the Kindling, the whisper Emeric gives to each one at a time with Imani's hand on the other wrist. Then they sit in pairs with their hands on each other's wrists and try to feel the other's room. Most feel nothing the first day. Two were half-lit already from years of Kaldo air and mana food: Bettany, who has fed the rat shelves for years, and a Grayrock nurse. Mabyn feels it on the Wednesday, too loudly. Next door, the hospital's limb room fits an old Tarrowmede soldier with a fitted socket and a jointed knee, and a Stannary man with a body-powered hook on a shoulder harness.

On Wednesday 8 November, at the Motion Shop, through Tess's eyes, Emeric asks whether a valve amplifier can hear a nerve. Tess says it can hear a frog's leg; everybody has done the frog's leg. He means a person's head, from outside. Privately he remembers a rig that once read a donor's working method, and what writing into himself did. Aloud he sets the rule: the cap reads and never writes. Tess asks why the second half needs saying. We don't write into people. Imani backs it without asking why. On the same bench the promised lot of the Forge's fast chips sits in its tray beside an old page of Tess's about a floating train, pinned back up in August, and she looks at both and says "This is fast enough" to nobody. The first twelve build-out drums are launched over the bay on Thursday 9 November and walked to their pins over Randillia's north and west. A nickel-titanium coupon from an argon-shielded melt cracks near the clamp on its sixtieth cycle, and Tess writes "remake the grain." On Monday 13 November the Stone Hearing's autumn sitting grants nine stones in its ordinary rounds and refuses a works stone to dim the Field's landing lights by the weather, because relays do it; Sorrel, who asked, says she knew and wanted to hear Ferra say it. That afternoon Ada Lovell arrives off the Milford train with one bag and Tess's reply in her pocket. Tess shows her the amplifier's first useless scribble on paper tape and explains what they want, which signal means which move. Ada sees that a table of signals is a program, the way the cards on Ottoline's loom were, says so, and starts ruling columns before anyone has shown her where to sit.

Each of the nine granted at the Hearing's autumn sitting lived a built scene for up to a week, walled by consent and proctored by their own master, then watched the whole of it back and chose to keep it or box it. Tess, at the works table, has never proctored one, and notes who did.

Chapter 71: The Swift

On Saturday 18 November, at the Field, through Sorrel's eyes, the Mark 1 flies for the first time: straight wing, one turbojet burning kerosene, started from the ground cart, with no stone aboard. Bram is on the fuel truck, Tess at the tower window with the jet pipe temperature figures, and the fire tender runs its engine the whole time. The sky watch's first radar set, just bolted to the tower's roof, follows her as a bright mark out to about thirty miles. She is up twenty-two minutes, fast and long, and the yard stops again. A clerk asks what to write in the logbook. "The Swift," a bird that never lands if it can help it. Emeric, at the fence, says nothing at all, which Sorrel takes as the best thing he has ever said to her.

At about two in the morning on Saturday 25 November, at Wayford's river close, one of Wayford's four pairs planted in May, the newest post, farthest from a knights' base, with the river at its back, through Osric's eyes from the pickets' account and his own arrival, the close's stone lights go dark all at once, and the stone bell with them: a dead zone over the close. The electric lamps on the wall stay lit on their own battery, the hand bell rings, and the two pickets on watch see three men at the drop cage by lamplight; their visor glass went dark with the zone, and they see by the scouts' mundane monocular instead. The pickets and the post's two regulars work the close's hand pump and the water butts. The fire break saves the far tree. The near one burns to the stump before the Wayford brigade's pump arrives. Nobody is hurt. At dawn the post's dog takes the trail to the river, where there is a boat's keel mark in the mud, and in the reeds an empty padded stone case and a cracked rack stone, overloaded. Osric's report says only what he found: a forced-stone zone, the method the Gate's decoy and the Moss used, both with Church stones by the record. It points at the Church and proves nothing. The Letters' switch holds, and nobody says Ostrel.

On Saturday 2 December, through Ferra's eyes, Emeric brings Mochi out and leaves it on the burned close for the winter, to make the ash into soil so the replanted tree has ground in spring. The Watch logs Mochi as a carrier, because in spring Mochi will carry the replant pair from the soak racks to the close, and anything that will hold a seed goes on the chain of custody. Mochi resents being signed for and goes the colour of old milk at the pen. Osric writes "Mochi (it)" on the line, and Ferra writes "she" beside it. On Wednesday 6 December Corbin tables the crown's draft of the Seed Act: only the Stone Hearing grants a stone; every blank seed goes on the Roll from the drop; holding an unrolled blank, trading one, or writing a stone for hire without a licence becomes a crime in Keller's courts; selling a seed abroad without the Assembly's leave is the heaviest charge; and holding your own granted or eaten stone is never a crime. Wayford's member wants the burned close paid for before anything else. Halverne's member asks why the Watch answers to the crown at all; the crews' man says he will want the Watch under the Assembly's Inspector before he votes; Tarrowmede's member says the crown is writing a monopoly on miracles. It goes to committee for the winter. After the sitting Saban hands Ferra a page from his man north of the Cocan Range: iron bought in odd lots, and foundry smoke where there is no market for iron. Ferra opens a new page and heads it "what else are they hiding": the hush mines, the juggernauts, the viewer stones in the Gilded Eye. She writes the iron under them with a question mark, and the grove's cracked rack stone under that.

The same week Sorrel flies the opposite machine: her held-stone glider off the Field at dusk on the suit's stones, with no sound at all, the radar's mark the only proof it is up. She gives the drawings to Mira for the Crestless as a scout. Loud for the sky, quiet for the knights.

Chapter 72: Twenty-Three to Nineteen

On Friday 1 December, through Imani's eyes, the rats answer. The ones killed at Gil's load rise on the third day; the ones killed at the other crewmen's trace do not; the ones killed long after their last dose do not. The same day, in Brask, Jenna Kitto opens her eyes and says what she heard: her husband reading her the price of oats every morning for eight weeks. All seven on support live; three died before help came. On Monday 4 December Imani tells the other three flecked crewmen, with the numbers, that their flecks are old exposure, their trace load has decayed, and they will not rise. One of them cries, which surprises him. All three stay on the register.

On Monday 11 December, on Tess's floor, the first winter re-spacing comes: the westerlies have pushed four of the twelve drums off their marks, and the line room walks them back on coded orders, the westward legs taking three days. Tess grumbles about the westward legs. It is logged and nobody looks at it again, which is what logs are for. On Wednesday 13 December a scalp electrode on a valve amplifier draws a trace that changes when the volunteer, Tess herself, thinks about closing her right hand. Nothing moves yet.

On Friday 15 December Keller orders, by the families' leave of the four on Imani's register and any who join it, that the dead of anyone on the register are burned. It collides with burial custom the first day. Gil's sister signs. One crewman's wife will not until Doran comes and sits with her for an hour. There is no cure yet; Gil could not have been saved, and the page says so plainly.

On Monday 4 December Corrick writes from Turnstone proposing that the League boycott the circuit, filing nothing and serving on no jury, while the hereditary council's lawsuits are listed there, so the lords cannot say the League takes their courts' word when it suits it. It is not a stupid letter. On Saturday 16 December, in the League's hall in Cinderhale, with Maudie keeping the minute and neither the king nor Corrick in the room, the League votes it down, twenty-three to nineteen. On Sunday 17 December, in Turnstone's cold visitors' garden, through Pim Barrow's eyes, Pim reads him the result. Corrick begins: the majority does not understand what it is trusting. Pim says that is what the mill lords say when tenants vote against them. Corrick reaches for the minutes and reads the tally himself. He does not laugh and is not converted. He is ashamed of the resemblance.

Where the Story Stands After Chapter 72

The Titles Act passed its first vote on 22 September and the hereditary council has taken the year the king's own Charter gives it, so the final vote cannot come before 27 September next year. The council is spending the year on lawsuits that quote Pryor's brief and on custom ordinances, listed in Keller's court for the spring. Weekly redemption has run since 2 October on the bridge machine's roster. The beast line is renewed for one year, to 13 October next year, with quarterly reports and one empty research pen at the Claim instead of the standing licence the king asked for. The jar rule went out under the Medical Pass Office's names. The Mage Corps is voted, and the Seed Act sits in committee. Keller has ordered the register's dead burned.

The young are fewer but not gone: sixteen killed, one starved, and the mother unseen, with none past the first stage. Warning bands ring on two thousand wrists and ten sentinels watch the shores, and the bay's first beast was pinned alive and then killed under the Inspector's reading of the law. Gil Haverty drank the beasts' blood for five months, died, and rose on the third day; the rats have confirmed why, the other three crewmen are safe, and there is no cure yet. Mochi knows the beasts' kind in a man's blood. A grove close at Wayford was burned by a forced dead zone and a cracked rack stone, pointing at the Church and proving nothing; Mochi is on the ash for the winter. Ferra has begun a list of what else the Church is hiding, with iron and foundry smoke north of the Cocan Range at the bottom.

Kaldo is building fast. The Field is lit and the Swift has flown twenty-two minutes under radar. Twelve relay drums hang over the north and west. Rescue golems have walked a staged fall. The Grayrock Forge is lit, the sun house is rising, and the first cold cars have come off the line. Twelve nurses are learning to read each other's rooms, Ada Lovell is turning signals into tables, and a cap has drawn its first trace of a thought. Rowena is promised a machine after Ward's, and Vantic quietly holds a miller's note. Liora has answered the isles that it was not the eyes. Corrick proposed a boycott, lost by four votes, and recognised himself in the lords he opposes.

This batch is about living inside a delay the king chose. He wrote the year into the Charter himself, and now he spends it the only ways open to him: building, and obeying rulings he would have written differently, while the people he governs sometimes find his own faults in themselves.
