Book 3, Batch 1: Middle Ground

Chapters 1–10 | Monday 21 June – Monday 30 August, Year 18

Book 3 opens a week after the king sat in a plain chair below Keller's bench and answered the review's question with one word: mine. The Isle Works Review is in session, with the mill lord on it, Mother Sabbe in her own chair, a chair kept empty for the orc doors' families, and the families of the fourteen dead free to question anyone. Under its terms the king is bound in writing to its finding. A frightened letter from Ostrel told him there is more than one room like the Isle's in the world, and that letter is under seal with Keller. The Private Arms Act's deadline is days away, the beast's core sits in Kaldo's case of record on behalf of its three owners, and Waylon is to marry Alina of Duvrenne at the end of August.

This batch is about the institutions Emeric helped build starting to work without him, and sometimes against him. The review refuses him twice, on things he thinks matter, and he obeys both times. The money houses dissolve their private armies on the deadline and rehire the same men under new names the next morning, and the king will not sign the decree that would make the cases quick. In Cinderhale a good man makes a lone decision that costs a case months. Nobody needs the king for any of it, and nobody asks him.

Then comes the wedding on the Isle, planned against enemies by Ward to the last inducer, and the plan holds where it was built to hold and fails at the one gap it chose to leave. People die in both places. A brother becomes a dragon in the sand, and a princess holds a broken wagon line herself. Under all of it, small signs keep turning up on the Isle's shore, and nobody puts them together.

Chapter 1: The Second Week

On Monday 21 June, in the records hall's long hearing room, Keller gives the families' bench the morning. Most of the families ask about money and burial. Dacey asks one thing: did the king read her brother's first warning when it was written? He answers it straight. No. It was written in November, while he was at the Guano Isles, and the works office filed it as not enough to halt the work under the exception. He heard it named in February, in the engineer's finding at the Assembly, did not ask to see it, and the survey was put off. He read it for the first time on 6 May. He does not add that he was a thousand miles away in November. The mill lord adds it for him, and then says it does not change February. The mill lord asks whether the king would have stopped the shaft in February if he had read it then. The king says he does not know. That goes in the record too, and it is the line the mill lord underlines.

On Wednesday 23 June, in writing, through the review's clerk as any party must, the king asks for leave to set a listening post on the cap of the filled shaft: a sealed iron pipe into the fill, a stone on it and Neris's ear, nothing dug and nothing opened. The Ostrel letter, under seal, says there is more than one room, and Neris heard an echo in April with her palms on the shaft frame's footing, which is now inside the fence. He wants to know whether anything else in the hollow is still beating, or could come out the way the guard did. Sound crosses a uniform fill far better than broken rock, which is why he asks for the pipe. As a fallback he asks to lay Tess's listening coil on the rock outside the fence. The Ostrel letter's words are kept to the chair.

On Friday Keller rules flatly. The pipe is refused. The shaft broke into the hollow's outer chamber in April, so its fill sits in the hollow's mouth, and a pipe into it touches the hollow, physically and under the words of the orc families' condition. The review agreed to that condition in June and cannot keep the promise and break it for the party it is examining. The king may renew the request when their member sits. The coil needs no ruling at all: anyone may listen from outside the fence, and the review will receive what is heard. The king puts his reasons in the record in full, without the sealed letter's words, says he accepts the ruling, and leaves. He thinks it is wrong and dangerous, and he obeys it. On the steps Ferra asks what he will do. He says he is going to ask the Islanders to run the coil and pay them for it. Mother Sabbe says they would have done it for nothing, and he can pay them anyway. All summer the coil hears nothing, which proves nothing either way, and he knows it. It is the first ruling against him: small, and costing him something he thinks matters.

On the records hall steps, before he says the word coil, he asks Ferra how far outside the fence a bore would have to start to come in under the cap. She says that is the hollow by another door, and Keller has just told him he cannot have the hollow. He says he knows. Then he says the coil. He has not said aloud what he believes, that the institutions he built are independent and he will keep every line, and that when the danger is real they will see he is the one who can stop it. This is the first time someone with the standing to say no says it to him.

On Saturday 26 June, through Saban's eyes, a broadsheet goes up on the Heirs' wall by the Works Town. It holds one word a foot high, MINE, then his answer to Keller word for word from the public record, then "beasts of the crown," then a gold eye drawn badly. It is all true and all lawful, and the quotation is exact. The review's fair copy is Honor Linden's, so the Heirs' angrier half assumes she gave it to them. She did not; the sittings are public and the printer had a man in the seats. She says nothing either way, and her silence reads as a yes to both sides, which Saban notes. The king reads the broadsheet and tells Saban to leave it up. His own word against him is the price of having said it, and tearing it down would make it a different word.

Chapter 2: New Names

On Wednesday 30 June, through Corbin's eyes, every private command in the kingdom dissolves under the Arms Act. The surrendered guns are in the crown stores at the Works Town, and the buyback has paid. On Thursday 1 July the men are back at work. Stannary hires its men as mine wardens. Fairhythe's are quay porters on Sayer's weigh-house roll. The Cocan March's are pass guards on the pilgrim road. Tullio's book pays forty watchmen's pensions to men who are not old. There are no guns, because the Act holds: only clubs, lanterns and numbers.

Hiring porters, wardens and guards is lawful. Four things are not: a command under arms, which the Act forbids if a continuing command and arms can be proved; coercion, threats, blocking a road or a gate, keeping workers off a quay, which the ordinary criminal law already forbids; playing the watch, stopping, searching or holding anyone without a public office; and holding arms at all outside tracked issue. So the gap is not in the law but in the proof. The new coats are careful never to stop anyone, a porter who leans on a fisherman does it with nobody else on the quay, and every case needs a witness who will swear in front of the men he is swearing against. The Inspectorate opens thirty-one cases and builds every one by hand, and they crawl.

At Stannary's mine office Treloar, the house's head, is told the stope timbers need replacing. He signs for the wardens' coats the same afternoon, and the timbers wait for next quarter.

On Monday 5 July Tullio's counsel asks the court to stay the Act's emergency schedule. The argument is that it rests on the same kind of emergency prerogative now under review, and a crown whose clause is in the dock should not run its own emergencies in the meantime. Keller cannot hear it, because he chairs the review, so the southern-circuit judge does. He refuses the stay: the Act was passed by the Assembly, not ordered by the crown. The argument is printed and read anyway, and it is a good argument. That day in the long room Corbin asks the king for a decree, a crown order naming the four houses' new companies as commands in all but name, so the Inspectorate need not prove the command man by man. The king refuses. He is in a witness chair over one emergency clause and will not sign another the same summer; if it is a command the Inspectorate proves it, and if it cannot, it is not one yet. Corbin says that is easy to say for a man with a dragon in him and harder for the porter on Sayer's quay. The king does not argue with that. Corbin goes back to building cases by hand, angry and good at it.

Chapter 3: One False Line

From Saturday 3 to Thursday 8 July, in Cinderhale, through Kezia's eyes, the ex-watchmen of the Hearth League trace guard payments to Tullio-linked books. They find a chit naming a poor guard as paid when he was not: one false line, and the only sample of the steward's own hand on a false entry. To prove the books were falsified at the steward's desk, the Inspectorate needs exactly that. Corrick decides alone not to publish it. He is right about the man and wrong about the procedure. One ex-watchman who carried the steward's counting-house sacks for six years, and knew which other ledgers the steward wrote himself, leaves for a season. With the chit buried and him gone, nobody searches for a second sample, and the case can only charge the clerks below the steward. It is one of the thirty-one, and it slows by months.

Kezia tells Corrick he was right about the man. What he got wrong was that he did not let anyone else be right with him. Her argument goes past procedure: he decided for everyone else what evidence they would be allowed to use, which is exactly what the Hearth says the houses and the crown do to the south. He has no answer to that one.

On Saturday 10 July, through Maudie's eyes, the Assembly's states committee defers her petition for elected representation on the south road until the state map and the Charter amendment are ready. She publishes the exact minute, not an embellished charge, and pins it to the League's wall as written. The people reading it understand that lawful delay is not neutral. Nobody sends for the king.

Chapter 4: Recall and Halt

The guard's core belongs to the Isle Compact, not to Kaldo, so reading it needs its three owners. On Monday 12 July Mother Sabbe signs in Kaldo, Oskel signs by Waylon's set, and Duvrenne's observer witnesses and carries the request to Kesrath. The leave asked is to read it and make it safe, and it is given on that wording. Keller's leave is needed too, because the steel-and-glass case is the case of record, the review's evidence. He gives it that afternoon on two conditions: the case is resealed in front of the clerk, and Tess's finding goes into the record as a sworn paper.

On Tuesday and Wednesday, in Tess's office, through Tess's eyes, she and Emeric take turns reading the core through a sealed reader stone, never bare, with Armand watching the clock. They find two commands in it, recall, which would make it walk home when its cradle calls, and halt, which would let the cradle stop it, and strip both, Tess checking every line he writes. Tess says the core's language is not like any delving she knows. On Thursday 15 July she files her finding under oath as paper fourteen: two notes in the makers' hand found and removed, recall and halt, described. She cannot say there is nothing else in it. She can read the shape of most of it and not the sense, and she will not sign it as safe. She writes the same sentence in Kaldo's own file, and it stays unsigned.

On Wednesday 14 July, in her ward, through Imani's eyes, she looks at her register of the crews who bled the carcass under the condenser, about three months in. Their old injuries are easing, they recover quickly, their density is up on Armand's kind of readings, and the four who worked closest have grey-violet flecks under their nails. She names the probable cause and no more: the beast's blood, on skin and breathed off the carcass, doing a little of what an eaten stone does, with the wrong pattern. Her register keeps two columns, measured and suspected. She asks the four one by one and puts their answers in writing: what she knows, what she does not, and that the change might be a gift or the start of an illness nobody has named. All four agree to stay on her register monthly for years. One asks whether he can keep the strength. She says she does not know, and that is the honest answer. She stops the work, writes it to the review as paper fifteen, and Keller orders the samples kept sealed as evidence. Nobody outside Kaldo has the warning yet.

On Thursday the king asks the Compact, through Vantic's escrow, to sell Kaldo the core outright at a price the other two owners set, and does not say what for. Mother Sabbe says it to his face: he asked to read it to make it safe, and now he wants it, and a review would call that using the leave. He agrees that is what it looks like, and says that is why he is asking to buy it instead of keeping it, and why nothing goes into it until it is sold. She says yes the same day: it is a thing that kills people, so keep it. Duvrenne's answer is slow, with Kesrath's court busy and the wedding coming.

On Friday 16 July, under the covered berth of the Cradle at South Sentinel, Bram's first castings come down the barge tunnel from the Sentinel Forge and are laid out on the dock: a frame for a thirty-metre body with nothing in its chest. Only sworn staff see it. Ferra puts it on the Roll as a sealed works at South Sentinel, true and nothing more, with its real account going to a sworn audit panel of three people who are not the six and did not spend the money; their finding on the Roll says audited, without the content. Nothing is booked as a hull refit, because a false line would be the one thing Ferra's censure could not survive. The core will go in only after the sale. Wren is told, and stands on the floating dock beside the empty-chested frame and will not stop talking, happily.

On Tuesday 13 July, through Emeric's eyes, the all-ages school sends for him: a boy called Runa's tail a pelt and she bit him. He disciplines her and means it, a week with no yard and an apology written in the letters she has, and Ferra catches him grinning on the stair afterwards. That evening he goes to the boy's house and tells the parents what the word means past the wall. The father had no idea, and says so.

Chapter 5: The Site Visit

On Saturday 17 July the review travels to the Isle on the K Endeavor's July run: Keller, the three delegates, Mother Sabbe going home, Duvrenne's observer, the families' bench by choice, Dacey among them, and the clerk with Honor Linden carrying the fair copy. The king comes as a witness on the review's summons, not as the island's host, and Kesrath's leave is used for Duvrenne's half. From Monday to Wednesday he walks the ground he is answering for: the filled shaft under its fence, the hollow's fence that nobody opens, the grey dust under the condenser that was the guard, the gallery where Bryn died, and the buried city's landward edge where the zone held. Mother Sabbe hosts on her own ground. Waylon walks the review through the filling and Oskel through the night of the charges, both under oath. The king answers what he is asked and offers nothing else. Walking back past the cove on Wednesday, Mother Sabbe mentions that the fish have gone thin there this summer, and that the fishers blame the works' noise and the filling. Nobody thinks about it again.

On Wednesday 21 July, through Waylon's eyes, Waylon shows the king the desert road Alina's convoy will take from Kesrath's tower to the pitch lake across Duvrenne's half. Duvrenne's king has already refused a Randillian escort on his own half, by letter, before the king could offer one. Emeric respects it and says so. Waylon agrees with the refusal and says he will ride with her guard himself, as a gesture to her people, and Emeric does not argue. On Thursday 22 July, the last night of the site visit, Alina and Waylon fix the date at Kesrath's tower, or rather Mathilde Aurenne, Alina's lady since they were girls, fixes it, picking Saturday 28 August off the tower's almanac after the two of them have politely deferred to each other for twenty minutes. She tells Waylon he will be on time or she will come and get him, and that she has held Alina's hand at every terrible dinner since they were nine and will not be stopping now.

On Thursday the king asks the review to let the crown's share of the Isle's older bores run again, not the shaft and nowhere near the hollow, because Kaldo's tanks are low, the fleet runs on diesel, and his own pause has cost the city a winter's fuel stock. The review refuses until its finding: the crown's part of the works is what it is deciding, and it will not let one part run while it decides the whole. He dislikes this more than the listening post, because this one costs every house in Kaldo. He obeys. On Friday 23 July, back in Kaldo, Ferra buys Duvrenne's oil from the Kesrath shore's houses at their price, signs for it, and says to nobody, "That's Alina's."

Chapter 6: The Plan

In early August the review's refusals send Emeric back to the one thing under the Isle he can plan for, the day somebody has to go down. A dead zone takes the masks' pumps and the casters' air, but a held breath does not care. In the bath he presses half a litre of air beside his core, scalds himself a little doing it too fast, and does it slower. Arthur learns it first after him, because he has a core and the Crestless are the ones who will go into dead ground at the wedding, and in two weeks he can hold about half a litre. Imani's rule is to vent before any fight you can see coming. Arthur asks what a fight you cannot see coming is. She says that is the one where it helps.

On Monday 2 August the cuff comes back from a Kaldo goldsmith plated in gold over the tungsten, with diamonds along the rims, a few sapphires and a star on the clasp. Tungsten weighs almost what gold does, so in anyone's hand it is a heavy gold cuff on a king, and a touchstone says gold. Ferra says it is the first jewellery he has ever bought himself. He says it is not jewellery.

On Monday 16 August, at the long table, Ward lays out the security plan whole: ships aloft, a sky watch, the dragon rifle on the lead hull; mana inducers at the venue and on every hull against a forced dead zone; card checks keyed to signatures, yellow cards for foreign staff, delvers at the gates and Ward's own eye for a juggernaut's tells; Imani's people in the kitchens; nothing that burns near the pitch; the merfolk of Ilenne's watch on the shore; every hull able to set down and float; and Amara on the relay sight sweeping the approaches, covertly. Ward names the gap aloud: the convoy on Duvrenne's half, where Duvrenne refused an escort. The hedge is Amara sweeping the route between venue checks, and a quick-reaction team sitting on a hull with its engines warm. The venue will be the pitch lake where the armistice was signed, each house walking in from its own end, Doran and a Duvrenne cleric officiating together. Envoys come from Velmoor, Solreth and the Imperial Core. Ostrel sends no legate, and people notice.

Saint Edwy's whole orchestra goes to the Isle by hull with its composer, who grumbles about rearranging without the organ. When the first tuning goes sour in the hold, the first cello thinks of the fix: the strings and the built piano will live in a tent with pans of water under them. On Saturday 14 August Alina comes to Kaldo for Abloh's last fitting with Mathilde, who argues with Abloh about the hem and wins. In the line room, while Wren rehearses the processional, Mathilde quietly asks her to sing one more piece at the vows as a surprise, an old Duvrenne cradle song the two of them sang as girls, and gives Wren the words in her own hand. Wren says yes. That month, on Fairhythe's quay, a widow named Ruth Penhale brings a feud to Sayer's weigh-house to be settled, and it is settled fairly, and she leaves sure she has had justice; then the weigh-house fee falls on both families.

The same Saturday Emeric is in Abloh's shop for his own coat, and the cut that burst at the side seams in January is finished. The two back vents are lapped and pressed so they read as seams. Box pleats run down both sides, under the arms and across the yoke. There is a lapped fly at the back of the trousers for the tail, the boots are split along the instep so they fall open and hang from an ankle strap, and every shirt opens down the front, because nothing goes over horns. He takes the half form once with the door shut. Everything opens and nothing tears. The trousers stop at mid-shin, and Abloh says that is a cut. The line's coats stand on forms along the wall, Corbin's with a third opening down the spine for the crystal. Mathilde asks what the second vent on the king's wedding coat is for. Abloh says riding.

On Saturday 14 August, through Ward's eyes, Runa tells him he lets her win. He agrees to show her the difference, padded and at an honest speed, and she can't get near him. Then he names three things she can fix: she squares up, she watches his hands, and she stops her feet when she's angry. She leaves with a plan instead of a grudge.

Ferra's August column under Kaldo's own sky has its one line, with no dragon yet to read it. On the Isle the orchestra tunes in a tent with pans of water under the cellos, and it holds.

Chapter 7: Asking Properly

Rowena arrives two weeks early for Waylon's wedding, and says so in one line at the quay: it is her answer to Harrold's letter. On Monday 23 August, at the hospital, through Rowena's eyes, she asks Imani, not Emeric, to fix the old scar on her face, because she watched Imani see the pull across a fire eleven years ago. Imani cuts the old line out, lays the skin back to the length it should have been, rejoins the small nerves the healers knitted wrong, and mends it to match the other cheek. It takes an afternoon. "In a day. Again." "An afternoon. Done right." Rowena keeps the scar on the back of her left hand, her own fight. Emeric, at the door, says he offered weeks once. She says she was not ready to be looked at for weeks.

On Wednesday 25 August Harrold, Amara and Rowena have supper at the keeper's table on the lighthouse island at dusk, Harrold's idea, which surprises both women. They talk about the court's three queens and three kinds of absence, now as a joke, and Waylon at three on the steps; Harrold names the carriage once and sets it down. Amara eats so she can taste it. Mid-supper Rowena, who has had seven months of letters about it and two children who are now dragons, asks Harrold straight out whether it really took a stone. He says it took the stone and the blood, and thick air to open it, and Ferra has the sheet. Then she asks the one she came with: would he give a bonded stone to a grandchild? Harrold has no answer. Amara does. She asked the same thing at the family table in January, nobody answered, and she has had seven months to find one. It will not be theirs to give. The hospital's rule is no stone for anyone under eighteen; after that a stone is asked for at the Stone Hearing by the one who would eat it, like anyone's, and nobody of the line gets one or is kept from one for being of the line. So the only thing the family owes a grandchild is the truth first: the sheet, both columns, before they ask. Rowena says then someone has to keep the sheet honest for fifty years. Harrold says Ferra will. At the end of the supper Rowena asks Amara how it is, with that, meaning the body. Amara says her stepson said to ask Bram. A sip. "So we asked Bram." "And?" "Bram asked if we wanted it load-rated." Harrold puts his face in his hand and keeps it there. Rowena laughs with her whole face, and for the first time in thirty-one years nothing pulls at the corner. She notices, and so does Harrold. He walks them both up from the boat, and at the gate Rowena tells him that was asking properly, and goes in.

Chapter 8: Processional

At dawn on Saturday 28 August, Bertil Quayle, the castle steward who has run the venue's staff for a month, walks the king round the stands. He complains that the east stands' lamp stones were set a day late and asks whether he can sit his daughter in the orchestra's tent for the processional. The king says yes. An hour before the processional, Ward flags a man at the staff gate: the tells of a juggernaut, how he stands, how he does not sweat. He is taken quietly, without a word about the Church in anyone's mouth. He was one of six in the guest staff.

Through Arthur's eyes, Wren sings the processional and the two houses walk in from their ends. The other five force stones under the stands for a dead zone. The inducers fire and the zone refills from liquid mana, but not evenly. The inducers sit on the hulls and the lake side, so the mana comes back from the water inward, and the east stands, farthest from the hulls, stay dead about a minute longer than the rest. The five knew where the inducers were, since the hulls are in plain sight, and centred their zone on the east stands. In that last minute, with the stand guards' lamp stones dark and only the Crestless carrying night glass, two guards and Bertil Quayle die in the dark at the stand's foot before the Crestless take the team with the night glass and the monoculars. The plan holds, and its seam is where the people died. Ward writes the inducer spacing on his table that night and does not forgive it.

A charge goes off in an empty stand near the orchestra, which the plan had kept guests out of, and the stand fills with smoke. The composer's copyist has gone back for the parts. Arthur goes in after her and breathes from the held breath in the smoke, breathing out into the smoke and in from the store beside his core, and brings her out. The orchestra will be a stand short with a broken chair. It is the first use of the held breath.

Amara, on the relay sight, flags the convoy road within minutes of a storm rising there: there's no wind for that. The quick-reaction team launches. Emeric stays at his post, because the venue holds the most people, the guests, the orchestra and Wren, and it is the right post for the king with the most force. He hates it. When the venue's zone clears, his sense comes back all at once, and for a moment he feels something in the sea off the cove, faint, alive and large. Then it is gone under everything else, and he puts it aside in the wreckage.

When the zone is forced, the cuff locks as what it is: a bracelet. A king cannot wear a weapon to his brother's wedding, and in the zone Emeric has three kilos of gold-plated metal on his wrist and nothing he can change. He stays at the post with it. Afterwards Ward makes it a rule, and Emeric agrees: on any ground where a zone could come, he sets a form before he goes, and at court he wears a second piece.

The coat does nothing at all in the zone, because nothing in it was ever cast: wool and steel strips. He notices only afterwards that the one thing on him the zone couldn't touch was his tailoring.

Chapter 9: The Convoy Road

In the same minutes, through Alina's eyes, her convoy crosses Duvrenne's half on her own guard, on her own ground, under her father's order, with Waylon riding with her guard in Duvrenne's colours over his own as a gesture to her people. Mathilde rides beside her and complains about the dust. The air goes still, then a wall of sand comes. The road opens under the lead wagons, and Duvrenne's escorts go into it in their armour. Mathilde goes under reaching back for Alina's hand. Alina watches her own guard fail on her own ground, and she does not wait to be saved. She is off the wagon and flat on the crust before anyone tells her to, the way the desert children are taught, and pulls a driver out of the sand by his collar. She gets the guards still standing to cut the traces and roll the rear wagons into a line on the hard ground, shouts the names she can see, and will not leave the line when her captain begs her to run for the tower. Her guard failed, and she holds what is left of it together.

Through Waylon's eyes, he feels the field holding the storm up, the way Emeric once told him a field feels, and overpowers it, and turns their own magic back on them, and changes for the first time before he decides to: a sand dragon. The change is frightening, not clean. He is suddenly a dragon's length of himself and cannot feel where it ends, and his earth sense is a hundred times louder, every beetle in the dune and every heartbeat on the road. His first lunge through the dune collapses the crust a body's length from Alina's line. He hears her shout his name and stops dead in the sand. He does not try to sort the bodies. The field runs back to its casters like strings to hands, so he follows the strings, and the casters are the only people on the dune standing still on hard rock and holding something. Then he moves through the dune like a sandworm and buries them. It is over before the quick-reaction team lands. He cannot find Mathilde under the sand in time. He finds her after, by the same sense, and carries her up to Alina in a claw he is afraid to close.

Emeric comes with the quick-reaction team's second lift, after the venue is secure, and lands on a road that is already quiet. Waylon solved it himself. The king looks at the sand dragon that is his brother for a long time and says nothing that would make it about himself.

Chapter 10: Sand in His Scales

On Saturday late afternoon, at Kesrath's tower, Alina has an hour alone. Her women have laid out the dress, and Mathilde's pins are on the table where she left them that morning. Wren comes up to ask whether she should still sing Mathilde's song, and that is how Alina learns there was one, in Mathilde's hand. She breaks, properly, for the first time since the road, with Wren holding the page and saying nothing useful. Then she asks Wren to sing it, pins her own hair with Mathilde's pins, and goes down. She makes the choice to marry tonight here, in grief, not in the council's language: she will not give the men who killed Mathilde the day, and she will not give them the song either.

That evening at the lake, through Ferra's eyes, Alina marries him as she had planned to, and sand is still falling out of his scales, because he cannot change back yet. Before the vows, so nobody can ask after, the Duvrenne cleric asks him, as the form requires, whether he speaks for himself, and Doran asks the same for Randillia's register. He says yes to both in his own voice, enormous, and the cleric rules that Duvrenne's law marries a person who consents in his own voice and says nothing about what shape he is in. Doran writes the same line in the register. Waylon says every word of the Duvrenne form aloud, so low the orchestra tent's canvas hums. Wren sings Mathilde's cradle song after the vows. The orchestra plays a stand short with a broken chair, Wren sings again, and it is bubbly, a bit ridiculous and earned. The envoys of Velmoor, Solreth and the Core sign as witnesses. Doran and the Duvrenne cleric note Ostrel's empty chair to each other and to nobody else. Like Emeric on the Isle, Waylon is empty from his first full change, and is a man again by the small hours. That night on the shore, at Ferra's side, Waylon asks Emeric whether he likes the desert because he is a sand dragon or is a sand dragon because he likes the desert. Emeric says he still does not know which way round it goes for himself. Ferra fills Waylon's row on the sheet: sand; his stone, eaten two years before; the air, the casters' own field over the dunes. Six rows, six stones, and nothing on the sheet that says why it took two thousand years.

That night Ferra writes full first in his door column, the same words as his father's. Waylon, reading it upside down from a dragon's height, says so big comes easy and coming back and the rest he has to be taught. Emeric tells him about the set on the sea stack and the first of the month, five weeks off, what a full change costs, and that coming back gets quicker with teaching. Waylon says he will wait for the first, since a sand dragon on the Isle every night in the first week of a peace is the last thing Kesrath's council needs. Alina agrees too quickly, and he notices.

The coat Abloh cut Waylon for the day is somewhere in the dune. Abloh says the whole dragon was never his business, and asks for Waylon's measurements in both shapes anyway.

On Sunday morning, through Waylon's eyes, Duvrenne's council arrives at Kesrath's tower with the convoy's dead counted, and its ministers ask why their own guard failed where Randillia's held.
