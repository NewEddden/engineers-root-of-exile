Chapter 77 - The Cost of a Hundred

Ward put the problem in front of him without dressing it, the way he put everything, laying it on the table between them as a fact and not a plea. "You have twenty-four men," he said, "and three towns and a road. In the last war I held a wall with them and it was enough, because there was one wall and one gate and one field to lose. There is no one field now. If Grayrock is attacked while I am at Wayford, I am a day away with every man I have, and the men I do not have, which is most of them, are a number you have been very careful never to make me say aloud."

Emeric made him say it aloud, because a number unsaid was a number Wexler had taught him to fear. Twenty-four enhanced men for a barony that had been eighteen hundred souls on one island, and the same twenty-four for a holding that was now eight thousand five hundred spread across three towns and a mine and a trade-junction and the road that stitched them, and twenty-four across all of that was not a garrison, it was a rumor of one, a force that could win any single fight it was present for and be present for almost none of them. The guard had not grown when the barony tripled. He had scaled the water and the streets and the school to the real number and left the thing that defended all of it sized to a town that no longer existed, the same error in a more dangerous place.

The scale had to move, and the question was how far and paid for how, and both answers had changed under him in the last year without his having stopped to add them up. The old ceiling on the guard had never really been men. Men he could find, drill, and feed. The ceiling had been the harness, the enhanced suit that made an elite man elite, and the harness had two costs that had each been a wall. The plate was one, the advanced steel that was the whole mundane floor of the thing, the part that kept a man alive when every rune on him failed, and the plate had been scarce when iron was scarce and was scarce no longer, because the foundry ran a surplus now and plate was the one thing the barony had learned to make in volume. That wall was down. He could armor a hundred men in good steel starting today and the foundry would not feel it past a season's schedule.

The other cost was the crystal, and the crystal was where a lesser plan would have lied to itself, and he made himself walk the truth of it slowly because the truth of it was a line he would not let anyone, including himself, blur. The suits used gravity crystal, a small amount in each, distributed through the harness to take the weight of the armor off the man so that a knight in full plate moved like a man in none, and gravity crystal was not iron. It was not a thing a barony brute-forced by opening a shaft. It was platinum-tier, geologically rare, a material of which the whole empire knew only a handful of deposits, and that rarity was not an inconvenience, it was a load-bearing fact of the world, the reason gravity-worked war had never flooded the field, the reason no king had ever fielded a legion of flying men, a scarcity the world was built on and that a single lord reopening a single mine did not get to repeal.

What had changed at Grayrock was not that fact. It was his relationship to it. The shaft they had reopened, the old worked-out vein that had turned out to run into air the dead had guarded, was one of those handful of deposits, and reopening it had given the barony, for the first time in its life, its own local supply of a material it had previously had none of. That was the whole of the change, and it was enormous and it was bounded, and the two things had to be held together or the whole military plan became a lie the way the prior reckoning had tried to make it one. Local access. Not abundance. Not common. He had a deposit where before he had a beggar's dependence on buying scraps at platinum prices from lords who had their own, and a deposit was a transformation, and a deposit was still a deposit, a finite thing with a bottom, one vein and not a world's worth.

He sat with Ferra and did the arithmetic of what the one deposit could and could not buy, because the difference between could and could not was the difference between a sound army and a fantasy that got men killed. The base suit and the flight suit both used the crystal only for the weight-offset, the small distributed amount, and at that rate the Grayrock vein covered a hundred base harnesses with margin to spare and the flight variant on top of them if the war ever called for flight. So the hundred-man guard was real, materially, honestly real, buildable now, not a wish. That was what the deposit cleared, and he let himself feel the size of it, a barony that could put a hundred enhanced men in the field on its own material, which a year ago had been unthinkable.

What the deposit did not clear was the thing he most wanted and would most have to deny himself, and denying himself was the discipline the whole plan rested on. The heavier suit, the one that did more than offset weight, the one that partitioned the crushing forces of true speed off a man's body so he could survive velocities that would otherwise pulp him, used a whole second gravity system, a body-worn harness heavy with crystal per unit, nothing like the small offset of the base suit. A squad of those would drink the Grayrock vein dry by itself, and the vein was already spoken for by threads that reached past the guard entirely, the larger works that drew on the same finite material, and a lord who spent his one deposit arming a handful of men to fly at the speed of sound had no crystal left for anything else and no more deposits to open when it ran out. So the heavy suit stayed rare. Not because he lacked the knowledge to build it, he had built it, but because the crystal to build it in numbers did not exist to spare, and a strategic material spent on a domestic quarrel was a material not there for the war that domestic quarrels were only the rehearsal for. He capped the plan at the base suit and the flight variant and shelved the heavy one, and shelving it while knowing exactly how to make it was its own small ongoing cost, the restraint of a man who could see the ceiling and chose to leave room under it on purpose.

The structure he locked with Ward across two evenings, and it had the shape of a thing sized to a real place rather than to an ambition. A hundred standing elite, full-time professionals in the base harness, recruited from all four baronies, Kaldo and Milford and Grayrock and Wayford, drawn deliberately wide so the guard belonged to the whole holding and not to one town, and loyal to Emeric directly, his own force and not a thing inherited or borrowed. Behind them a trained reserve of about two hundred and fifty, run through the same literacy-and-drill pipeline that had made the first men, not full-time and not armored to the same standard, farmers and craftsmen and clerks who drilled on a schedule and returned to their work and could be called up when a crisis needed more bodies than a hundred, a force that cost the barony almost nothing until the day it was needed and roughly tripled the guard the day it was. Wartime, the two together came to something past three hundred and fifty, which was a real army for a holding this size and no fantasy at all. And the garrisons he had inherited at Grayrock and Wayford stayed where they were, in their local posts, a separate structure holding local order, not folded into the elite line, because a mine and a junction each needed their own standing watch and it was wasteful to make elite men do a watchman's work.

Ward wanted more standing men, because a captain always wanted more standing men, and Emeric held the line at a hundred and made himself explain why to a man who had earned the right to the reasons. Past about a hundred and fifty full-time soldiers, he said, you stopped choosing how many men to arm and started choosing men over ore, because a full-time professional was a pair of hands not at a furnace or in a shaft or on a bench, and the barony was already short of hands at the shaft, and the ore the short-handed shaft produced was the exact thing that paid for and armed and fed the guard in the first place. An army too large ate the economy that sustained it and starved itself in the eating. A hundred standing men was a fraction of the working population small enough that the works did not feel it, and the reserve tier held the rest of the force in reserve where it cost no throughput at all until it was spent. It was not the largest army he could build. It was the largest army he could build without the army beginning to consume the barony, and that distinction was the whole of sound military accounting, and Ward, who had watched Wexler's kind arm past their means and rot from the inside, took the point and stopped pushing.

They built the armor and they did not build the arsenal, and the distinction mattered to him more than it would have to another lord. The hundred got the base suit as standard issue, the good steel floor and the small crystal offset and the reflex-assist, the suit that spent its whole magical budget on removing the cost of superior protection and sharpening what a man already had rather than on any edge or blast, a suit that made a man harder to kill and no easier to make him a killer. That he issued freely, now, in peacetime, because armor was defense and a well-armored guard was a thing even a nervous watcher could read as prudence. But the weapons, the grenades and the kinetic ordnance and the flight and the rest of the ladder that climbed above the base suit, those he did not dump into the men's hands cold. Each rung of that ladder carried a cost that a man only truly learned by paying it in a real fight, the dead zone a discharged crystal left behind it, the thrust a flying man lost the moment he drew a weapon, and gear issued without the fight that taught its cost was gear a man trusted wrongly and died behind. He would field each rung when a fight forced it and not before, when the necessity that justified it had shown its face, and he built the capability now, the trained crews and the plate and the crystal reserve, so that the day the necessity came he could climb the ladder fast, rung by rung, each answering a wound the last fight had opened. He did not yet know which fight that would be. He only knew, with the part of him that read the shape of things coming, that a barony did not build a hundred-man guard in a year of peace unless something in it already expected the peace to end.

He kept the whole of it quiet, and sequenced it after the streets and the school and the water on purpose, so that a man watching Kaldo from the outside saw a lord rebuilding a wrecked town and only later, if he looked closely, saw the guard grow inside the rebuilding, the army arriving as the last and quietest note under a year of visible civic works. That was the pacing, and Ferra approved it, and then Ferra said the thing that sat under the pacing and would not be paced away. "You can build the water in daylight and no one minds," she said. "You can build the school in daylight and they applaud. There is no hour of the day and no order of operations that makes a man building an army look like anything but a man building an army, to the men whose business it is to worry about armies. You have tripled your holdings and tripled your defense of them, and every reason for it is sound, and not one of the sound reasons will matter to a duke who reads a hundred enhanced men on the frontier as a schedule and not a shield. You are sizing a guard to a land. He is going to read it as sizing a guard to a war. And the terrible part is that from where he sits, with what he knows, he is not being stupid."

Emeric heard it, and half-filed it, the way he always half-filed the warnings that were about people rather than systems, because the systems half of him had done its work perfectly, had sized the guard to the holding with an accountant's honesty and a captain's judgment and left not one figure padded, and the systems half of him could not quite make the people half believe that a perfectly honest army was the single most frightening thing a nervous peer could be shown. He had built a guard that fit the land. He was certain of the arithmetic. He was, as ever, less certain of the room, and the room, this time, contained a duke already reading his rebuilding as a rearmament, already counting the hundred men Emeric had counted, and drawing from them the one conclusion the arithmetic could not argue away, which was that the exile on the coast had stopped being harmless in the only way that had ever kept him safe.
