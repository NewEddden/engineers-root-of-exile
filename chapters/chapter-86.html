Chapter 86 - What She Chose to Show Him

The quiet word happened in a small room off a gallery where the banquet's noise came through the wall as a blur, and Maeve stopped being nothing the moment the door closed, which was the only place in the whole capital she could afford to.

He had watched her wear the nothing all evening and he understood the cost of it now that he saw her put it down, the way a person's shoulders come down when a weight they have carried so long they forgot it was a weight is finally set aside for a minute. She was younger than her caution made her seem, and sharper than her blandness let the room believe, and she had built herself into the sister no one watched with the same deliberate craft he built everything, and it had cost her the thing it cost anyone who makes themselves invisible on purpose, which was that no one saw her, ever, including the people she would have liked to be seen by. In this room, for a few minutes, with the door shut and the banquet a blur through the wall, she let him see her, and it was the closest thing to rest her life allowed her.

They did the thing that was theirs, the thing no one else in the world knew was possible and that was the whole reason their alliance could exist at all. He had brought her into the shared delve once, a season and more ago, carefully, teaching her to be the willing second in a workspace he built and held, and it had become their channel, the one line of communication in the entire realm that no watcher could tap and no cipher could crack and no captured letter could betray, because it left no letter and made no sound and passed through a method the world did not know to guard against. It was why she was his most secure ally and his least suspected one at once, a castle-based sister who could reach him and be reached without a single physical trace, and here, in person, with their hands together over the modest keepsake that had been the cover for this meeting, they could go into it fuller than the distance had ever let them.

The workspace built around them, quiet and hers-and-his, and in it they did the thing that was not mind-reading and could never be, because he had built even this to obey the law that held the whole magic of the world together. He could not take a thing from her. That was the bedrock of it, the reason the channel was safe to use and the reason she trusted it with her life, that in the shared space each of them could only offer, deliberately, the thing they chose to show, and the other could not reach past the offering to anything held back. She showed him what she chose to show him. He could not have found a single thing she did not surface herself, no more than he could read the buried thing in any mind, and so the channel was not an intrusion they tolerated for the sake of the alliance, it was a giving, mutual and bounded and chosen, and the boundedness was exactly what made it possible for two people who could trust no one to trust each other in the one place their honesty cost them nothing.

She showed him the court. That was the intelligence, and it was the reason she had asked for the word, because a castle-based sister who watched everything and was watched by no one saw things a marcher lord on a frontier could not, and she had been saving them. She showed him Corbin's shape at court, the faction he ran and the men in it, the pattern she had watched for years and been certain of and had never once had proof of or anyone to tell, the deniable insults and the two-birds maneuvers and the warmth worn over the knife, and she showed him that she moved for him not out of a sister's blind love, which she was too clear-eyed to feel and too honest to fake, but out of the coldest and truest reason she had, that he was the only one of her brothers who had made a thing real and checkable, built something that existed and worked and could be counted, and that between a brother who built and a brother who only maneuvered she had made the rational bet, the same bet a careful baron on the frontier had made, on the sibling who had actually produced. He received it the way the channel gave it, as a thing she chose to hand him, and he understood that her clear-eyed reason was worth more than any warmth, because warmth could be flattered and a rational bet could only be held by keeping earning it, and he intended to keep earning it.

And she showed him the warning, which was the thing she had most needed the private word to carry, because it could not go in any letter and could not survive any courier and could barely survive being said aloud even in a shut room. She did not have the shape of it exact. That was the honest edge of what she gave, and she gave the honesty with the intelligence, that she could not tell him precisely what was planned or by whose hand, only that she had watched the room the way she watched every room and had felt the particular stillness that gathered around a thing being arranged, the quiet that Corbin's faction fell into when a piece was being moved, and that all of it was pointed at his leaving. Something was set for his departure. She did not know if it was Corbin's own hand or the careful men behind the duke or some third arrangement the capital had made for the man it had just raised, and she did not pretend to know, because pretending to know was how allies got each other killed. She knew only that the shape of a plan sat around his going, and that he should not leave the way they expected him to leave, and she handed him that certainty across the channel as the most useful and most frightening thing she owned.

He gave her back what he could give, and choosing what to give was its own discipline, because there was a thing in him he would not surface even here, the deepest thing, the fact of who actually stood inside this face, and the channel's own law protected it, because he could only offer what he chose and he did not choose to offer that, not to her, not to anyone, and the not-offering was safe in a way no secret guarded by trust alone could ever be. So he gave her the things he could. He gave her enough of what he was building to confirm her bet, the real shape of Kaldo, the school that would outlive him and the water that ran without a crystal and the town grown gold out of black ground, so that she would know the thing she had wagered on was as real as she had guessed and realer. He gave her the trust that ran the other way, that he saw her, the actual her under the nothing she wore, and valued the clear cold mind more than he would have valued a softer sister's love. And he gave her the one warmth that cost him nothing to give and meant the most for her to receive, which was that she was not alone in the knowing anymore, that the years of watching Corbin's pattern with no proof and no one to tell were over because now there was one person who knew what she knew and believed her without needing the proof, and he watched that land in her the way water lands in dry ground, the particular relief of a person who has carried a certain thing alone for years and finally set half its weight on someone else.

They came up out of it before the banquet could miss either of them long, because a sister and a brother gone from a hall at the same length of time was the exact kind of small fact the room's careful people collected, and neither of them would spend the channel's secrecy on a minute of comfort. She put the nothing back on before she opened the door, and he watched her do it, watched the sharpness go under the blandness and the weight come back onto shoulders that had been down for a few honest minutes, and he understood what it cost her to walk back into a hall that would forget her the instant she entered it, on purpose, because being forgotten was the whole of her power and the whole of her loneliness at once, and there was no version of the alliance that did not require her to keep paying that price. She would be read, if this ever surfaced, as a sister who had sided against her own full brother, and it would cost her everything a court could take, and she had chosen it anyway, coldly, rationally, for the brother who built, and the coldness of the choosing did not make it cost her less.

He held the door for her and she went back into the nothing, and he stood a moment in the small room with the blur of the banquet coming through the wall and turned the warning over in his hands like a stone. Something was set for his leaving. He did not know its shape and neither did she, and the not-knowing was not a reason to dismiss it, it was a reason to change how he left, because a plan aimed at a departure was a plan that assumed a departure it could predict, and the one thing he could always do to a plan built on a prediction was to make the prediction wrong. He would claim what was his in the morning, the thing he had come to the capital to claim beyond the title, the thing no one else in the realm wanted and only he understood the worth of. And then he would leave the capital in a way the capital had not arranged for, because his youngest sister, who was nothing to the room and everything to him, had walked into a shut room and set half the weight of her certainty on his shoulders, and he did not intend to waste it by climbing into whatever ride the plan expected him to climb into.
