Chapter 79 - The Grievance He Built

Aldane raised his banner on a grievance that was true, which was the part Emeric could not talk his way past and did not try to.

The reports that came up the new paired line and then, thicker, up the old relay chain had a shape to them by the third day, and the shape was a muster. Aldane was gathering men on the frontier of his own lands where they met the edge of what Emeric now held, and he was gathering them under a reason a herald could say aloud in a market square without shame, which was the most dangerous kind of reason a war could have. He was not calling Emeric a heretic, not yet, that was a later season's charge and a different institution's to make. He was calling Emeric a destroyer of the old and honest crafts, a pedigreeless third son raised up past his birth on the wealth of machines that put good men out of the trades their fathers had held, and he was gathering, to march behind that banner, the exact men the charge described.

The Inkhands. Emeric had known they existed before Aldane gave them a name and a patron, had known it the way a man knows a debt he has not yet been asked to pay, because he had made them. When his paper and his ink had taken the market on merit, fairly, without a predatory price or a dirty trick, the men who had made ink and paper the old way had lost first his administration and then the public and then their trade, and the ones who did not convert or sell had come, in the end, to the only place still hiring hands, and had taken work in his own works, and there was the wound. A master ink-maker who had held a guild rank his father held before him, who had been a man of standing in his own small world, now stood at a line in Emeric's factory doing a laborer's portion of a thing he had once done whole and been honored for, and the income was not even the worst of it. The income he could live on. It was the standing, the fall from a man who made a thing to a man who tended a step of a machine that made it, the demotion from craftsman to hand, and a man will forgive you for making him poorer far sooner than he will forgive you for making him small.

That was Aldane's rank and file and Aldane's justification both, and Emeric could not answer it by calling them dupes, because they were not dupes. They had a real injury, and he had dealt it, cleanly and legally and without malice and it was real all the same, and a man does not need malice done to him to want the man who unmade his life brought low. He had told himself through the whole market takeover that fair competition would survive later scrutiny, that his hands were clean because his margins were honest, and his margins were honest, and here was the thing the honest margins had built, a class of men with a true grievance and now a duke to carry it, marching on him behind a banner that was not a lie.

Aldane's own reason was a lie, or half of one, and Emeric knew that too, and knew it in a way that changed nothing about the war and everything about how he understood it. The status grievance the duke wore in public, the destroyer-of-crafts, the upstart-raised-past-his-blood, was real enough to fight behind and thin enough that Emeric had seen through it before he ever knew what lay under it. He knew what lay under it now. He had asked Ward, in a quieter season, why a man's hatred should run so far past what a mere insult to rank could feed, and Ward had told him the hallway, and the queen, and the thing Aldane had tried and been handled for, and so Emeric fought this war knowing that the banner of the honest crafts was a respectable coat thrown over a private and much older thing, a personal hatred that had found, at last, in the Inkhands, a grievance decent enough to march in daylight. The duke had not raised an army because Emeric built factories. He had raised it because he had hated Emeric's line since before Emeric wore this face, and the factories had finally given the hatred a flag the world would salute. Emeric did not say this to anyone. There was nothing in it that changed a single disposition of the coming fight, and there was a thing underneath it he looked at only sideways and never long, a shape his own exile had, the same shape as the sin the hallway held, that he did not have the stomach to hold up to the light and did not need to, because the war would not care whether he had.

What the war would care about was whether his men could fight it, and here he came to the decision he had been holding since the scale-up, the decision the whole gear ladder had been waiting on, and the reports coming up the line took it out of his hands and made it now.

He had built the capability and held the weapons, and he had held them for exactly this reason, that a weapon issued cold to a man who had never bled behind it was a weapon that betrayed him, and now the holding was over because the alternative to arming his men was watching them meet Aldane's numbers with steel and reflex alone. So he ordered the first rung built and issued, the small arms and the grenades, and he ordered it knowing three true things about them that a lesser commander would have let himself forget in the urgency, and made himself say all three aloud to Ward so that they were shared knowledge and not a comfort he kept privately from the men who would carry the risk.

The first was that they were tested and the testing was not enough. He had run them on a range, because he was not a man who sent an untried thing into a fight, and on the range they worked, the guns threw their shot true and the grenades emptied their charge where he aimed them, and he had the numbers to prove it and the numbers were good. And the numbers were a lie of a particular kind, not false but incomplete, because a range was a calm man and a cleaned weapon and a known distance and no one shooting back, and a battlefield was a frightened man and a fouled action and a wrong distance and death coming the other way, and the gap between those two was where every early weapon in the history he carried had gone to fail. Range-proven was not war-proven. He knew it. He fielded them anyway, because the war had not offered him the season it would take to close that gap, and accepting the gap was accepting that the first real fight would teach him what the range could not, and would teach it in the only currency that lesson was ever paid in.

The second was that the men did not truly know the weapons, and could not, in the days the war gave him to train them. They knew the manual of arms. He had drilled them on the loading and the aiming and the throwing until the motions lived in their hands, and the motions were not the knowledge. The knowledge was the costs, and the costs were the things a man only came to own by paying them. The gun could jam, and a man who had only ever cleared a jam on a range in his own time would not clear it the same way with a blade coming at him. The grenade carried the dead zone, the emptied mana at the point a crystal dumped its charge, the crystal left inert in that dead space until it could recharge, so that a man who spent his grenade at the wrong moment was a man who had just made a hole in the world where his own magic would not work and stood in it, and Emeric could tell them that, and had told them that, and telling was not the same as a man feeling his crystal go dead in his hand at the moment he reached for it. He could not pour his understanding into them. No inventor could. The men would learn the weapons the way he had learned everything, by paying, and the tuition would be bodies.

The third was the one he said last and quietest, which was that this was the right choice and it would still cost, and that those were not opposed. It was correct to arm them, because unarmed against Aldane's numbers they died in greater number for certain, and armed with half-proven weapons they had a real chance at a smaller and uncertain price, and a commander who refused the smaller uncertain cost to keep his hands clean of the larger certain one was not virtuous, he was a coward dressing his fear as principle. He would arm them. He would field the first rung. And he would not pretend to himself or to Ward that fielding it was a clean thing, because the first fight would show him a jam or a dead crystal or a thrust lost at the wrong moment, some cost he had accounted for in theory and not yet paid in fact, and a man he was responsible for would pay part of it, and that was the shape of the whole war arriving, the shape the census had promised and the guard had promised and now the weapons promised too, that every good thing he had built was about to be tested against a real enemy and would hold, and would hold at a price, and the price would be visible and would be his.

He gave the order. The first small arms and the first grenades went out to the hundred, brief-trained and range-proven and war-innocent, into hands that knew the motions and not the true weight, and the paired line to Ward stayed open the small amount its stones could bear, and the riders stood ready under it in case every stone in the barony went dark at once, and the reserve behind the hundred drilled harder as the muster on the frontier grew. He had wired the army to speak as one mind and sized it to the land and armed it with the first rung of a ladder he would climb only as the war forced him up it, and he had done all of it honestly, to the real size of the threat, with the costs named.

And on the frontier the banner of the honest crafts went up over a hatred older than any craft, and the men he had made small by making things well gathered under it to make him answer, and Emeric looked at the muster growing in the reports and understood that the first fight would come before he was ready, because the first fight always came before a man was ready, and that being right about the war would not spare him the cost of fighting it, and that the cost was going to walk back through his own gate on a stretcher before this was through.
