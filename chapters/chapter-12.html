Chapter 12 - Turned Earth

Ferra brought him the garrison before the fields were half dug, because the garrison was faster to read than dirt.

"Wexler's muster roll," she said, and laid it flat, and beside it a second sheet in her own hand that was shorter and told the truth the first one didn't. "Same disease as the debt pages. He wasn't hiding soldiers. He was hiding wages. There are men on this roll drawing a knight's pay who've never stood a knight's watch, and men drawing next to nothing who do the actual work, and the gap between the two is where his money went."

Emeric leaned over it. He saw what she'd done at once, because it was the thing he'd have done. She'd set skill against pay, duty logged against coin drawn, and let the mismatch sort the room. A soldier's wage tracks a soldier's worth, roughly, the way an honest thing tracks the thing it measures. Break that link, and you get the shape of a lie, one name at a time. Where the pay ran high, and the skill ran low, you found Wexler's friends, the padding, the men bought to be counted rather than to fight.

"This many," Emeric said. Fewer than the roll claimed. More than he'd feared.

"That many I'd stake the books on." She tapped a smaller cluster, set apart. "These I'm less sure of. High pay, but the skill isn't low, it's only unrecorded. Could be Wexler paid them well to keep quiet about the rest. Could be the record's just thin. The number can't tell you which."

He should have stopped there. He knew that afterward, the way he knew most things afterward. But the sort was clean, and clean was the thing his mind reached for the way a hand reaches for a rail, and he took Ferra's flagged names and started building the list of who left. Overpaid and unskilled, out. Overpaid and unclear, out until proven otherwise, because a barony this thin could not afford to keep paying a maybe. It was efficient. It was defensible. Every name on it earned its place by a number he could point to. He brought it to Ward the way he brought most finished things to Ward, expecting it to be received. Ward read it the way he read everything, once, slowly, top to bottom. Then he set a finger on a name near the middle of the cut list and did not lift it.

"Not him."

"He's overpaid for his record. Ferra flagged him herself, the unclear pile. I'm not cutting on a guess, I'm cutting on the fact that I can't afford a guess."

"You're cutting on a number." Ward still hadn't taken his finger off the name. "This is Declan's cousin. Osric drilled beside him for two years before any of us came here. He took a wage he didn't argue because his mother's in the low town and the coin kept her roof on, and he never once asked what Wexler wanted for it, which is the whole reason his record's thin. He kept his head down and did the work and stayed out of the parts that got other men paid. The record's thin because he was clean, my lord. Your number reads clean as bought."

Emeric opened his mouth to argue the method and found the argument had nothing under it. The heuristic was sound. It was sound the way his air-blast had been sound, right up until the ore turned out to be a different ore. He'd built a filter to catch men who took money for nothing, and the same filter caught a man who took money and quietly gave everything and left no paper trail doing it, because a paper trail was the one thing that would have gotten him noticed by the wrong people. Low profile read identically to no profile. His system could not tell loyalty from absence, because loyalty, done right, looked like absence on a page.

"How many others," Emeric said, and heard how quiet it came out.

"On this list? That one I'm sure of. The rest I don't know well enough to swear to, which is its own answer." Ward finally lifted his finger. "I can't hand you a clean sort either. I'm telling you the clean sort is the problem. You want to know which of these men to keep, you're going to have to let me and Osric and Declan stand in a yard with them for a week and watch, and you're going to have to trust three men's eyes over one good page, and I know exactly how much that costs you to do."

It cost more than he wanted to admit. He'd built the whole barony toward the opposite of that, toward numbers a man could check instead of judgments a man had to trust. But he'd also just watched his best tool nearly throw away a loyal man for the crime of being quiet, and he had done it himself, with a clear head, in an afternoon, and he would have signed it if Ward hadn't happened to know a name.

He struck the man off the cut list. Then he struck the whole unclear pile off it and handed it to Ward instead, to be settled in the yard by men who knew faces, and he made himself say the part he'd rather have kept.

"I'd have cut him. On the number. And I'd have been sure I was right the whole time I was wrong."

"I know," Ward said, not unkindly. "That's what I'm for."

The men Ferra's page had caught cleanly, the overpaid and plainly idle, the ones whose loyalty had always run to whoever signed the wage and would run off the moment the wage did, those Emeric let go without ceremony and without delay. Some of them he suspected had been Wexler's eyes as much as his soldiers, and every day they sat in the lower ward was a day they watched. So he moved fast, and he made a point of moving before the barony changed much further, so that what they carried out the gate with them was already going stale in the carrying. They rode off knowing Kaldo as a plague-scarred farm town with a strange third-born prince and a half-mad plan to grow legumes on dead ground. They did not know what the fields would become, or the forge, or the men left standing in the yard behind them, because none of that had happened yet the day they left. Emeric noted, without yet knowing what to do with the thought, that a man carrying an outdated map is more dangerous than a man carrying none, because he thinks he knows the ground.

What was left, when the padding was gone, and the quiet loyal man was back on the rolls where he belonged, was twenty. Twenty men who could actually soldier, most of them underpaid for years, all of them watching to see whether the new lord was one more mouth making promises or something they hadn't met before.

"Twenty," Ward said, looking at the yard the first morning they stood in it. "It's not many."

"It's enough to start with." Emeric was already seeing it the way he saw everything, as a system underbuilt rather than a number too small. Twenty trained men, fed properly, drilled on a schedule that made sense instead of one a bored baron never bothered to set, organized around what they were actually for instead of what flattered the man paying them. "They've been kept like ornaments. Nobody's asked them to be good at this in years. That's not a shortage of men. That's a shortage of anyone bothering."

He set the routine himself, over Ward's shoulder and with Ward's corrections, because Emeric knew how to build a training program the way he knew how to build anything, from the outcome backward, and Ward knew the thousand things about actual soldiering that no outcome-backward plan would ever contain. Between the two of them, the twenty started drilling like men who'd been reminded they were men, and not furniture. It would be a while before it showed. Emeric had learned to be patient with the things that only showed later.

By that same morning the laborers were already moving in the fields, and the barony had a noise to it again, over ground that had been dead or dying the week before. The miners went up toward the hills. The farmers started a fresh survey of the land. The blacksmiths didn't need telling at all; they'd been doing their part long before Kaldo had a reason to hope.

Emeric walked the farms with Farris and listened to what the crews made of what he'd laid out. They wanted to know why he'd split the ground into three sections instead of the single wide field they were used to working. He gave it to them plain. A section resting while the other two carried the load wasn't a section wasted; it was next season's yield being kept back instead of spent. Farris chewed on that a moment, weighing whether a prince's idea was worth the trouble of taking seriously, then nodded like it had settled something in him.

"My father ran this land before the war took the hands to work it right," Farris said. "Always said the ground gets tired same as a man does. Never had numbers to prove it. Just the feel of it, after thirty years."

"He wasn't wrong. There's a reason under the feeling. Legumes put back into the soil what grain pulls out. Rotate them through, and the land mends itself, instead of needing a whole season lying fallow."

That carried further than the layout ever had. Farris turned it back to the rest of the crew in his own words, plainer ones, the kind that would stick with men who'd sooner trust a neighbor's phrasing than a prince's. By midmorning the rotation had stopped being Emeric's idea and started being a thing the farmers understood well enough to argue over among themselves, which was the whole point. A plan only he understood was a plan that died the first day he wasn't standing there to explain it again.

There was one thing about the ground that had made the whole plan possible, and Emeric had been quietly grateful for it since the first survey. The town sat on an island, or near enough to call it one. The river came down out of the hills and split above Kaldo, one arm running wide to the west and one to the east, the two of them wrapping the town between them and closing again below it into a single current that ran on toward the coast. It meant one road in over the upstream ford, one way out down the water, a town you could wall and hold as a single thing. It also meant water on two sides to draw from, and a fall in the land from the split down to the joining that would carry water through a trench on nothing but its own weight if a man graded it right. He'd put the pump-house and the grain works on the western arm, where the flow was steadier, and left the eastern arm and its faster water for the works that would want the current later. He'd walked both banks twice before he let a single shovel bite.

They asked, too, about the sections he wanted left alone entirely, the strips that wouldn't grow anything at all. He told them again that he had plans for those and that it would make sense later. He wanted to say more, to tell them about the array he had in his head, the water network that wouldn't need a body walking the line by hand. He kept it behind his teeth. He was still early with the Control Rune, still finding the edges of what it would do, and Bram didn't yet have the ore for the pipe fittings the full thing would want. Promising it now would cost him more than saying nothing.

He went to Bram's after. The forge was busier than usual, new commissions coming in from every corner of the barony and all of them landing on the one man who had to actually make them. The spare ore was already glowing, going to tools for the mining crews first and plows for the farm second. Emeric caught Bram in the gap between one heat and the next and put in his first real order. Plows. As many as the farm could use before planting slid behind. Bram said he'd have a pair by day's end.

He came back an hour past that, wiping soot off hands that had given up on clean, the rag long past helping. "Got your first two," he said, tipping his head at the pair cooling by the door. "And some numbers you'll want before you plan much past today."

The ore, told straight and unpolished in either direction: enough for what the season asked, plows, basic tools, and nothing past that. The mine itself was steady. The deep sections were their own trouble, and Bram flagged them honestly rather than pretend at an answer he didn't have. The air thinned the further the crews pushed down, thin enough that shifts were shortening on their own without a single order to that effect. Nobody was hauling much ore out of the deep, because nobody could stay down there long enough to try.

Emeric put it where he'd been putting most things lately. A real problem, not an emergency, worth solving properly rather than solving badly today just to feel like he'd done something. "We'll get to that," he said, and meant it as a plan and not a way of setting it down to forget.

Next morning the farmers had their plows and were turning the sectioned fields, the rotation lines going in right where he'd walked them. The dirt broke easier than he'd expected of land that had sat half-dead for years, which told him more about the soil underneath than anyone had thought to check before now.

The irrigation went in as parallel runs, not one main channel. In another life he'd watched a single point of failure drag down everything downstream of it more than once, and the habit had followed him here. One length clogged or collapsed would cost him a section of field, not the farm. The trenches ran off the western arm and fell with the land toward the joining below the town, gravity doing the carrying, exactly as he'd graded them to. Jabari's crew, on loan for the day while the mine had a slow stretch, packed the clay lining, grumbling good-naturedly about a depth he'd called for that ran deeper than plain irrigation asked. None of them pushed on it outright. They'd learned that his over-building usually meant something, even before he said what.

He set the water crystal himself, off to one side of the main trench, catching runoff into a basin he'd had them dig a touch larger than the job wanted. One stone, one plain Release rune, nothing networked, nothing that needed the Hollow Crystal at all, a first small test of the thing before he trusted the whole line to it. It was small enough that nobody asked after it the way they'd asked about the empty lanes, just a prince fussing over water the way he fussed over everything. He liked that it went by unremarked. Not everything needed an audience.

The array lanes went in last, staked out and left bare, long straight gaps running the length of each field, wide enough for a full plow team to come about, walled on one side by a strip nobody was to break ground on at all. To anyone walking it, it read as waste, a farm with holes cut into it for no reason a person could name. One of the younger farmhands finally asked outright what the empty strips were for, half joking that the prince meant to grow air out there.

Emeric let the joke stand. He gave them something nearer the truth without handing over all of it: that the space would end up catching something out of the open sky the same way the fields caught rain, and that it would make more sense once there was something standing in it to look at. Not a lie. Not an explanation either, and he knew the difference even if nobody else in the field did yet.

The name stuck before the day was out. By the time the crews broke for evening, they were calling the bare lanes the sun-collecting array, half in jest, half because nobody had offered anything better. Emeric heard it secondhand from Bram, who passed it along like he found it funnier than he was letting on. He let the name sit. It wasn't accurate, not really, but it wasn't wrong enough to bother correcting, and correcting it would have cost him an explanation he wasn't ready to give.

By dusk the fields carried the shape of an actual plan instead of a list on a desk. Turned earth in clean rotation lines. Trenches cut and lined, waiting on water that wasn't running yet. A basin catching runoff that nobody gave a second thought. Bare lanes holding open for something the barony didn't have the ore or the skill to build, not this season. It didn't look finished. It looked, for the first time since he'd come to Kaldo, like something that was going to be.

Jabari found him at the edge of the last field, watching the light go orange over ground that had been dead a week ago. He'd come up from the mine still wearing the day, dust worked into the creases of his hands, worn down the way a man gets after a full shift underground and a walk past the farm on top of it, when he could have gone straight home.

"Haven't seen it look like this in years," Jabari said, more to the field than to Emeric. "Ran cattle through here when I was young. Before the war took the cattle and the plague took the reason to keep trying."

Emeric had nothing useful for that, so he didn't reach for something that wasn't. He stood there next to a man who'd earned the right to look at a field of dirt and feel something about it.

"Ore's holding," Jabari said, once the quiet had run its length, going back to the practical the way men who'd worked their whole lives tended to, comfort only ever lasting so long before the next real thing needed saying. "Deep sections still worry me. But what we've got covers what you need for now."

"That's all I asked for," Emeric said. "Anything more than that this early would've worried me worse than the shortage does."

Jabari came near a smile at that, the kind of near that meant something coming from him. He didn't say anything else. He didn't need to. He turned back toward the mine to make his report, and Emeric stayed on a while, watching the last of the light go, over turned fields on one side of him and, back up the hill behind him, a yard where twenty men were learning to be soldiers again, before he went in himself.
