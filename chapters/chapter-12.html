Chapter 12 - Turned Earth

By morning the laborers were already moving, and the barony had a noise to it again, over fields that had been dead or dying the day before. The miners went up toward the hills. The farmers started a fresh survey of the land. The blacksmiths didn't need telling at all; they'd been doing their part long before Kaldo had a reason to hope.

Emeric walked the farms with Farris and listened to what the crews made of what he'd laid out the day before. They wanted to know why he'd split the ground into three sections instead of the single wide field they were used to working. He gave it to them plain. A section resting while the other two carried the load wasn't a section wasted; it was next season's yield being kept back instead of spent. Farris chewed on that a moment, weighing whether a prince's idea was worth the trouble of taking seriously, then nodded like it had settled something in him.

"My father ran this land before the war took the hands to work it right," Farris said. "Always said the ground gets tired same as a man does. Never had numbers to prove it. Just the feel of it, after thirty years."

"He wasn't wrong. There's a reason under the feeling. Legumes put back into the soil what grain pulls out. Rotate them through, and the land mends itself, instead of needing a whole season lying fallow."

That carried further than the layout ever had. Farris turned it back to the rest of the crew in his own words, plainer ones, the kind that would stick with men who'd sooner trust a neighbor's phrasing than a prince's. By midmorning the rotation had stopped being Emeric's idea and started being a thing the farmers understood well enough to argue over among themselves, which was the whole point. A plan only he understood was a plan that died the first day he wasn't standing there to explain it again.

They asked, too, about the sections he wanted left alone entirely, the strips that wouldn't grow anything at all. He told them again that he had plans for those and that it would make sense later. He wanted to say more, to tell them about the array he had in his head, the water network that wouldn't need a body walking the line by hand. He kept it behind his teeth. He was still early with the Control Rune, still finding the edges of what it would do, and Bram didn't yet have the ore for the pipe fittings the full thing would want. Promising it now would cost him more than saying nothing.

He went to Bram's after. The forge was busier than usual, new commissions coming in from every corner of the barony and all of them landing on the one man who had to actually make them. The spare ore was already glowing, going to tools for the mining crews first and plows for the farm second. Emeric caught Bram in the gap between one heat and the next and put in his first real order. Plows. As many as the farm could use before planting slid behind. Bram said he'd have a pair by day's end.

He came back an hour past that, wiping soot off hands that had given up on clean, the rag long past helping. "Got your first two," he said, tipping his head at the pair cooling by the door. "And some numbers you'll want before you plan much past today."

The ore, told straight and unpolished in either direction: enough for what the season asked, plows, basic tools, and nothing past that. The mine itself was steady. The deep sections were their own trouble, and Bram flagged them honestly rather than pretend at an answer he didn't have. The air thinned the further the crews pushed down, thin enough that shifts were shortening on their own without a single order to that effect. Nobody was hauling much ore out of the deep, because nobody could stay down there long enough to try.

Emeric put it where he'd been putting most things lately. A real problem, not an emergency, worth solving properly rather than solving badly today just to feel like he'd done something. "We'll get to that," he said, and meant it as a plan and not a way of setting it down to forget.

Next morning the farmers had their plows and were turning the sectioned fields, the rotation lines going in right where he'd walked them two days before. The dirt broke easier than he'd expected of land that had sat half-dead for years, which told him more about the soil underneath than anyone had thought to check before now.

The irrigation went in as parallel runs, not one main channel. In another life he'd watched a single point of failure drag down everything downstream of it more than once, and the habit had followed him here. One length clogged or collapsed would cost him a section of field, not the farm. Jabari's crew, on loan for the day while the mine had a slow stretch, packed the clay lining, grumbling good-naturedly about a depth he'd called for that ran deeper than plain irrigation asked. None of them pushed on it outright. They'd learned that his over-building usually meant something, even before he said what.

He set the water crystal himself, off to one side of the main trench, catching runoff into a basin he'd had them dig a touch larger than the job wanted. One stone, one plain Release rune, nothing networked, nothing that needed the Hollow Crystal at all. It was small enough that nobody asked after it the way they'd asked about the empty lanes, just a prince fussing over water the way he fussed over everything. He liked that it went by unremarked. Not everything needed an audience.

The array lanes went in last, staked out and left bare, long straight gaps running the length of each field, wide enough for a full plow team to come about, walled on one side by a strip nobody was to break ground on at all. To anyone walking it, it read as waste, a farm with holes cut into it for no reason a person could name. One of the younger farmhands finally asked outright what the empty strips were for, half joking that the prince meant to grow air out there.

Emeric let the joke stand. He gave them something nearer the truth without handing over all of it: that the space would end up catching something out of the open sky the same way the fields caught rain, and that it would make more sense once there was something standing in it to look at. Not a lie. Not an explanation either, and he knew the difference even if nobody else in the field did yet.

The name stuck before the day was out. By the time the crews broke for evening, they were calling the bare lanes the sun-collecting array, half in jest, half because nobody had offered anything better. Emeric heard it secondhand from Bram, who passed it along like he found it funnier than he was letting on. He let the name sit. It wasn't accurate, not really, but it wasn't wrong enough to bother correcting, and correcting it would have cost him an explanation he wasn't ready to give.

By dusk the fields carried the shape of an actual plan instead of a list on a desk. Turned earth in clean rotation lines. Trenches cut and lined, waiting on water that wasn't running yet. A basin catching runoff that nobody gave a second thought. Bare lanes holding open for something the barony didn't have the ore or the skill to build, not this season. It didn't look finished. It looked, for the first time since he'd come to Kaldo, like something that was going to be.

Jabari found him at the edge of the last field, watching the light go orange over ground that had been dead a week ago. He'd come up from the mine still wearing the day, dust worked into the creases of his hands, worn down the way a man gets after a full shift underground and a walk past the farm on top of it, when he could have gone straight home.

"Haven't seen it look like this in years," Jabari said, more to the field than to Emeric. "Ran cattle through here when I was young. Before the war took the cattle and the plague took the reason to keep trying."

Emeric had nothing useful for that, so he didn't reach for something that wasn't. He stood there next to a man who'd earned the right to look at a field of dirt and feel something about it.

"Ore's holding," Jabari said, once the quiet had run its length, going back to the practical the way men who'd worked their whole lives tended to, comfort only ever lasting so long before the next real thing needed saying. "Deep sections still worry me. But what we've got covers what you need for now."

"That's all I asked for," Emeric said. "Anything more than that this early would've worried me worse than the shortage does."

Jabari came near a smile at that, the kind of near that meant something coming from him. He didn't say anything else. He didn't need to. He turned back toward the mine to make his report, and Emeric stayed on a while, watching the last of the light go, before he went in himself.
