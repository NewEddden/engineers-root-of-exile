Chapter 72 - And Climbing

The thing that finally made him count was the water, though he did not understand that until Ferra had already made him understand a great deal more than the water.

He had gone down to the town end of the old clay network on a plain errand, to see whether the intakes above the burial ground still ran clean now that the rapids district had grown a second cluster of houses upstream of them, and he had stood at the head of a pipe he had laid himself in the plague years and watched it deliver, at the middle of an ordinary morning, a thinner flow than it had any business delivering, and had felt the old cold drop in the gut that meant a system was telling him something he had not asked it. The pipe was not broken. He checked. The grade held, the joints held, the intake sat clean above the contamination the way he had built it to sit. It was simply carrying less to more, and it was carrying less to more because more had arrived while he was looking at furnaces, and he had never once stopped to ask how much more, because the number in his head was a number he had earned five years ago and had gone on trusting the way a man trusts a floor.

Eighteen hundred and forty-seven. He could still see it hanging in the delving dark where Ferra had pulled it to the front of everything and held it, the true count under Wexler's lie, the figure the whole barony's early planning had been sized to, the plumbing and the farm rotation and the granary and the muster all cut to fit a town of that weight. It had been true. That was the trap of it. It had been so exactly true, and had cost so much to make true, that he had filed it under settled and never opened the file again, and a settled number is the most dangerous kind, because the world does not stop moving to keep a man's arithmetic honest.

He went to Ferra with it the way he went to her with all the things he had gotten wrong by not looking, which was plainly, and without pretending he had come to her with anything but a confession.

"I've been running three territories on one old count of one of them," he said. "I want the real number. All three. Kaldo, Grayrock, Wayford, counted the way you counted the first one, against the parish books and the muster and the tithe rolls and whatever else won't let a man lie to it. I don't want an estimate. I've been living inside an estimate for a year and it's about to cost me a well."

She did not tell him he should have asked sooner, which he noted, because it was the kind of restraint she had learned to spend on him only when she agreed the point was already made. She only closed the ledger she was working and said it would take her the better part of a fortnight to do it right, because Grayrock and Wayford had never been counted at all and a first count was slower than a correction, a man having to build the whole scaffold before he could measure anything against it. He told her to take the fortnight. Then, because neither of them had the patience to wait a fortnight to know the shape of a thing when they could have the shape now and the precision later, they put their hands to the stone that same afternoon and went down together to look at what she already held.

The workspace built the counting room around them the way it always did, her ledgers lifting off the flat and arranging in the ordered ranks her mind kept them in, except that she had been quietly gathering the raw material of this for months without being asked, the way water finds every low place, and so there was more in the dark with them than he had expected. The parish registers of three towns. The tithe remittances. The muster rolls, stripped of the padding Wexler's kind always left in them. The rapids district's own housing tallies, which she had kept from the day the first honest row went up because a woman who tracked everything tracked the people her lord was putting roofs over. It was not a census yet. It was the whole body of a census lying in pieces, waiting for someone fast enough to hold all of it at once and let the number fall out.

She was fast enough. He watched her do the thing she was best at with nothing in the road slowing her down, the columns sorting themselves at her attention, the double-counted names collapsing, the dead lifting out and the newly arrived settling in, and he stayed out of it the way he had learned to stay out of it, because the space did not make her cleverer, it only got out of her way, and getting out of her way was the whole of what she needed from him here.

"Kaldo first," she said, "because Kaldo is the one you think you know."

She pulled a figure to the front and held it, the way she had held the eighteen hundred, and it was not the eighteen hundred, and he had known it would not be and was still not ready for how far it had moved. Four thousand and two hundred, near enough, and she said the near-enough plainly, that it was soft at the edges and would firm as she worked but would not shrink, because the edges were all pushing the same direction and none of them pushed down. He asked her where it had come from, the two thousand and more that had not been there, and she gave it to him the way she gave him everything, in order, cause by cause, refusing to let a single large number stand unexplained.

The return migration first, the people who had fled the plague years and the war years to wherever a body could still eat, drifting back one household at a time the moment a town had work and clean water and a lord who did not skim the tithe to the bone, because a ruined town people leave and a living town people come home to, and Kaldo had become, without his ever framing it that way, a living town. Then the birth rebound, the plain arithmetic of a place that had stopped burying its children faster than it made them, five years of it now, the cohort of the plague survivors' babies grown to a count that showed. Then the rapids district itself, the company town he had built to work the foundry and the glasshouse and the mill, which he had thought of as a works and had never once counted as a population, the housing rows and the families in them and the trades that grew up to feed and clothe and shoe the men at the benches, a whole town risen beside the furnaces while he watched the furnaces. Then the guild refugees, the displaced craftsmen from the absorbed baronies and beyond, the men his own ink and pen had priced out of their old trade drifting toward the one place still hiring hands. Then, last and smallest and most visible to him because he had watched them arrive on the road, the tower students Armand had drawn down, and the faculty forming around them, and the servants and suppliers a faculty pulls behind it.

"Every one of those is a good thing," she said. "You built a place worth coming to. That's the whole reason the number moved. I want you to hold that before I show you the rest of it, because the rest of it is going to feel like a failure and it isn't. It's the bill for succeeding faster than you counted."

Then she showed him the rest of it. Grayrock, never counted, came up at two thousand, the mining boom off the reopened shaft pulling hands into a town that had been scraping cold veins a year ago and now cut into rock the dead air had guarded for a thousand years. Wayford, never counted, came up at two thousand three hundred, the trade lift off the mended road thickening a junction town that had always sat on the routes and had never, under its old lord, been allowed to grow fat on them. She laid the three side by side and let him add them himself, because she had learned he trusted a number more when his own hand had made it, and his own hand made it something past eight thousand five hundred, across three territories, and climbing, and she said the climbing aloud so it would be on the record that the figure was a floor and not a ceiling. Milford she left blank, and named the blank, because Milford was Paxton's and allied and no business of Kaldo's to count, and a man who censused his ally's people was a man telling his ally he had stopped being an ally.

He sat with it in the dark and did the reckoning, and the reckoning was not the pride he might once have taken in a number that large. It was the water. It was every system in three towns cut to fit eighteen hundred souls and now asked to carry more than twice that in Kaldo alone. The clean-supply network he had laid for a town of eighteen hundred was delivering thin to a town of forty-two hundred because forty-two hundred people drew more than eighteen hundred people drew, and he had felt the thinness at the intake and mistaken it for a fault in the pipe when it was a fault in his count. The farm rotation, sized to feed eighteen hundred with margin, fed forty-two hundred with no margin at all and would fail the first bad season the way the granary had failed to fire, quietly, arithmetically, an honest system doing exactly what it was built to do at a scale it was never built for. The granary itself. The standing guard, twenty-four men for a barony of eighteen hundred, now twenty-four men for a holding of eight thousand five hundred spread across three towns and a road, which was not a garrison, it was a rumor of one. The housing. The waste. The burial ground, cut to receive the dead of eighteen hundred and now taking the dead of far more, creeping back toward the water table he had spent the plague years dragging it away from, so that the very first thing he had ever fixed in this barony was, if he did not move on it, about to unfix itself under the weight of the people his success had drawn.

"I did it again," he said. It was not a question and it did not want comfort. "I built the machine right and I built it for the wrong number. I did it with the thresher and I did it with the drying shed and I did it with the wall that ran too long for the men to hold it, and I have just done it with an entire barony, three of them, because I got so good at building that I stopped counting who I was building for."

"You did," Ferra said, because she did not lie to him to make a bad hour shorter, and then, because she also did not let him carry more of a thing than the thing weighed, "and every lord I have ever read about who had this problem had it the other way. They built for the town they wished they had and sat in an empty hall wondering where the tax went. You built for the town you had and it grew out from under you, and that is the better failure by a distance, and it is still a failure, and the water doesn't care which kind it is. Fix the water."

That was the whole turn of it, and he felt it settle into place under everything with the particular finality of a problem correctly named. The census was not a fact to be filed. It was a trigger. Every civic thing in Kaldo was sized to a barony that no longer existed, and the gap between what he had built and what he now had to build was not a wish list, it was a hole the plague could climb back through, and it had to be closed in the order the danger set and not the order desire preferred. Water first, always, because a boom town on plumbing cut for eighteen hundred was a second outbreak wearing a good year's clothes, and he had buried enough men once already to a thing he had thought he had solved. Then the waste, and the burial ground moved again, further this time, ahead of the count instead of behind it. Then the farm, scaled to feed the real number with the margin the fire had taught him never to be caught without. Then roads for the traffic the trade had grown, and housing for the hands the works kept drawing, and past all of it, the thing he had been circling since the tower students came down the road, the thing that turned every irreplaceable principal in the barony from a single point of failure into a role that could be filled twice, because a holding of eight thousand five hundred could not be run on one metallurgist and one shipwright and one doctor any more than it could be watered on one old pipe.

He came up out of the dark with the fortnight's real work still ahead of Ferra and the shape of the whole modernization already standing in his head, ordered, sequenced, sized at last to a number he had made himself look at. And underneath the plan, quieter, running the way it always ran under everything he built, the older arithmetic that never turned off. A barony of eight thousand five hundred was not a thing a man kept quiet. Eighteen hundred souls on a plague coast were beneath a great lord's notice, and the crown had let him alone because a ruin drew no eyes, and the peers had filed him under harmless because harmless was cheaper to believe. Eight thousand five hundred and climbing, across three towns, with a foundry and an ore road and a metal ship and a faculty forming around a former head mage, was not a thing anyone was going to go on filing under harmless. He had spent five years turning a place people fled into a place people came home to, and the coming home was the census, and the census was the number, and the number was the same fact from two distances, the fact that had opened the road and the fact that would bring the world down it. He had built a town worth living in. He was about to find out it was also a town worth counting from a hall he had never entered, by men who would read the same figure he had just read and draw from it the one conclusion he could never argue them out of, which was that the exile had become too large to leave alone.

He told Ferra to firm the number and start the water. Then he went to find Bram, because the pipe he had laid in the plague years was cut for a town that was gone, and there was iron past the barony's debts now, and it was time to build for the people who had actually come.
