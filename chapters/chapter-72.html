Book 2, Chapter 33: Blue

The queen held her first audience at the floor on the Sunday at the second bell, and Zale went down the stages to it behind a pilot like a guest, because Imani had said that if he so much as kicked his legs on the way down she'd know, and he believed her.

He walked. Every bell, every landing, the whole of the long cold way down through the black water with the lamp stones going past on the line one every twenty yards, on his own feet, braced on the steps cut in the stage's wall, with his gills working under his ears and his arms held in to his sides. It took most of an hour. The pilot, a young merman with a long patient face, waited for him at every landing without being asked and never once looked at his side.

He'd stopped being a secret on Friday. He felt it on the way down. Every merfolk they passed on the stages, coming up or going down, slowed, and looked, and then very carefully didn't look; and some of them let a colour come up as he went by that he didn't know, a pale cool flicker at the throat that came and went like a fish turning. He asked the pilot what it was. The pilot said, in careful Kaldo, that it was the colour you showed a thing in the deep that had decided not to eat you.

"Grateful," said Zale.

"Careful," said the pilot.



She was on the seat when he came in. He went the length of the hall again, slowly, because that was how you went, and up the step, and sat down at her right hand on the cold black rock where he'd sat on Friday; and she didn't look at him, and her colour didn't move. He'd expected that. A queen at her first audience didn't look at her husband. A queen at her first audience after her husband had taken a man's head off in front of her and then sat her on his knee didn't look at anybody she didn't have to.

The first lord on the floor had come about the knee.

Zale didn't know his name. He was a big man of a deep house in a coat with a great deal of shell sewn on it, and he stood below the seat as the asked stood and made a formal complaint, in the old form, with Ressar's office standing by to see it done right. That the king had dishonoured the queen's seat. That he'd taken it, in the queen's hall, before the court, and put the queen in his lap on it like a girl on a quay. That this was a thing the stones had a word for, and the word was a bad one, and the court should hear it said.

The watch officer at the end of the step put it into Kaldo for him, low, a sentence behind. Zale knew enough of their tongue by now to catch the shape of it as it came, and the officer's Kaldo filled in the rest.

"He says," said the officer, "there must be consequences."

Zale smiled at him.

He didn't decide to. He'd sat at the tables of three kingdoms' worth of harbour masters in his life and smiled at every one of them who'd told him there'd be consequences, because it was the cheapest thing a man could do and it worked more often than it should. He smiled at the lord with the shell on his coat, pleasantly, and leaned forward a little with his forearms on his knees, as far as the strapping let him, and asked him, in his bad version of their tongue, with real interest, what consequences he had in mind.

The lord looked at him.

It was a real question. Zale wanted to know. He'd killed a man in this hall two days ago and he'd been told by the watch that it was the king's hand under the old killing rules, and he still didn't know what a king of Aumere was allowed to do or not do on a Sunday, and here was a man who seemed to have an opinion. He waited, interested, for the answer.

The lord's colour went a little paler at the throat.

Ilenne spoke.

She didn't raise her voice. She looked down at the lord from the seat with her colour perfectly courteous and studied him, slowly, as Zale had seen her study a chart, from one end to the other; and then she said one sentence, and the officer put it over flatly, a little behind.

"You seem to have mistaken my permission for his authority."

Zale sat back.

He did it at once. He didn't think about it. She'd spoken, and her voice had a door in it that shut, and he took his forearms off his knees and sat back against the foot of the seat with his hands in his lap and his face polite and attentive, as a man sits in a captain's cabin when the captain has started talking. The lord saw him do it. Every bench in the hall saw him do it. That was, he understood a moment later, the point.

"You may go," said the officer, for the queen.

The lord went.



The second was worse, because it was true.

A deep lord from the right side, an old one, thin and very upright, who didn't make a formal complaint, because there was no form for it. He stood on the second tier where he was and said it from there, in the water, so that it was said wet, slowly and without heat. That the court had sat on Thursday and heard a widow name a custom, and found a crime, and given her the seat. That the court had chosen a widow. That on Friday her husband had walked in at the far doors.

He didn't say anything else. He didn't need to. He stood with his colour level and let the hall work it out.

Zale felt it work. The pale cool flicker went along the benches and back.

Ilenne answered him from the seat. She didn't hold her colour. She let it come up plain and grey and steady, so the old lord could see there was nothing behind it.

"Nobody asked," she said.

The officer put it over. Two words. Zale heard them in her tongue first and knew them, and then in the officer's flat Kaldo, and they were the same two words.

The old lord stood a moment longer. Then he sat. His colour didn't move at all, and Zale understood, watching it not move, that it would never move on this. It was a true thing said wet, and her answer was a true thing said wet, and the two of them would sit in that hall side by side for as long as either of them lived, and every lord who wanted to say she'd tricked the court would have the first and every one who wanted to say she hadn't would have the second.

He didn't like it. He thought she didn't either. She'd done it to them so that nobody would have to ask her a cruel question in the water, and they'd never forgive her for having been kind.



The deep houses came about the grove at the third bell.

Three of them together, which Zale learned was unusual, with a spokesman, a smooth middle-aged merman with a good coat and a pleasant even colour who'd clearly been chosen because he had both. They had an offer. The second grove, the one the Rim-born districts had been asking for since before Ilenne was born, that the old king had promised and never begun because there was no purse for it: the three houses would fund it. All of it. The digging and the domes and the beds and the keepers, out of their own holdings, starting this season. Freely.

And in return, the governing of it. Who breathed there, and when, and for how long. The access. The three houses would keep the roll.

Zale watched Ilenne hear it. He thought it was a good offer. He'd thought so for about a breath, until the spokesman said the access; and then he'd thought about the Rim's people he'd seen in the line at the old grove's gate on his second day, standing in the water by the hour with their children for their turn at the thick water, and the house's men who'd kept the gate.

"No," said Ilenne.

She gave her reasons. She gave them courteously and at length, so that the three houses would have nothing to say afterwards about how they'd been refused. A grove the city breathed from couldn't be in the hands of the houses that could afford to fund it, because the houses that could afford to fund it didn't stand in its line. The second grove would be built, and the crown would find the purse for it if it took ten years. And it would have its own body to govern it, not a house and not the crown: the Keepers, who'd keep the access roll and publish it under the crown's seal, every month, every name and every hour, so that anyone in the city could read who breathed and who waited; and a second copy of every roll would be held by the Rim-born districts' own councils, so that if the Keepers' copy and theirs ever disagreed, everyone would know at once.

The spokesman's pleasant colour held, perfectly. Zale thought it was the most expensive thing he'd seen a man hold in that hall.



Bram came after them, and Zale had known he would, and wished he hadn't.

He'd brought the drawings. He'd been working on them since the first week: the domes for the second grove, Kaldo's way, glass on a steel frame instead of the Rim's held glass on stone, with pumps to move the water through them that would run on a dynamo at the surface and push three times what the old grove's vents pushed. He'd shown them to Zale on the Rim on the Thursday, laid out on the hard rooms' table, with the look on his face that Bram only had about a thing he thought was right. They were good drawings. Zale didn't know anything about domes, and he could see they were good.

Bram stood on the floor, braced, in his Mark II, with the drawings in an oilcloth case, and a watch officer put his offer over for him. Kaldo would build it. The domes, the pumps, the frame. Kaldo's crews, Kaldo's steel. Begun this season, before the fleet sailed, and finished by the Rim's own people after, on the drawings, with everything they needed left behind.

Ilenne listened to all of it. Then she said no.

Not to everything. To the building. Kaldo could send the Keepers samples of its glass and its steel, as many as it liked, and do pressure studies with the Keepers' divers on the old grove and the site of the new, and leave the figures. Kaldo could teach anyone who wanted to learn. But nothing installed in her water by Kaldo's hands, and no Kaldo crews working on her floor, until her own engineers asked for them. Not this season. Perhaps not for years.

Bram stood there.

Zale watched him take it. It went into him slowly, the way water goes into a split in a deck, and then it was in, and he went red from the collar up inside his glass, and said through the officer that he understood, in a voice that said he didn't, and bowed, too stiffly, and walked back to where the guests stood with the oilcloth case under his arm, and didn't look at anybody.

So that was the morning. Zale sat at the foot of the seat and counted it. She'd refused an insult, and her own great houses, and an ally. In one audience. In her first week.

And then Dauvre came up the floor.



Zale knew him by the soot. It had been two weeks and the boy still had it in his hairline, as if he'd decided to keep it; the furnace hand off the stair, whose mother had gone over in the skiff under Ward's ship. He came up the floor on his legs, walking, a thin furnace hand of the Rim in a work coat in a hall full of the deep's lords, and stood below the seat, and looked up at the queen as the asked look up, though nobody had asked him anything.

He'd come to ask. Anybody could, at an open audience; that was the one thing a first audience was for, Ilenne had told Zale on the stages' foot that morning. The officer put it over.

"He asks," said the officer, "why the pilots who took the house's coin in the season of the fire are still piloting."

The hall was very quiet.

Zale knew the answer before she gave it. He was a sailor; he'd worked into a dozen harbours with a pilot at his elbow, and he knew what a harbour was without its pilots. He watched her know it too, and watched her decide to say it anyway, all of it, to a boy with soot in his hair, in front of the court.

She told him. That every channel into the Rim and out of it went through the pilots. That there were a great many of them, and some had taken the house's iron in the season of the fire and some hadn't, and the watch didn't yet know which was which for all of them, and wouldn't for months. That if she took every pilot who might have taken the house's coin off the water tomorrow, no boat would come in through the fetch line for a season, and the Rim's fish and its fuel and its iron came in by boat, and the furnaces he worked at would go cold in a fortnight. That the crown had the pilots now, under Taurre's rate book, with a second pilot licensed beside every first, and any pilot who wrote a false route would lose his bond and his office, and the watch would find out which of them had taken the coin, one at a time, and those would go. But not tomorrow. Tomorrow the channels had to be open.

Dauvre stood below the seat and heard it.

He hated it. Zale could see him hate it, the way he'd seen him on the step at Esseth's door not able to choose. His colour went dark at the throat and stayed. He said something short, in his own tongue, and the officer didn't put it over and didn't need to.

Ilenne didn't make him take it back.

"No," she said, in Kaldo, quite quietly, so that it was only for the step and the floor and wasn't wet, and Zale heard it. "Me neither."



She came up to his ship that afternoon in daylight, which she'd never done.

He was on deck under the awning aft, on the folded sail, where he'd been on Thursday night. In the open now. The whole breakwater could see him, and did; there'd been a small crowd at the gate steps since Friday, merfolk on their legs and porters and children, who stood and looked at the land man who'd killed Ushar and then sat on the queen's seat, and pointed him out to each other, and went away, and came back with their friends. Linnet had wanted to rig a screen. He'd said no. If they wanted to look they could look.

Ilenne came up the gangway with the crowd watching and sat down beside him on the sail. A hand's width off.

"You're queen," he said. "You can't sit on a folded sail."

"I can sit on what I like."

"There are people watching."

"Yes," said Ilenne. "Good."

She looked at the gate for a while. Then she told him, in Kaldo, in her careful short pieces, what his staying would cost her.

She didn't dress it. That was the thing he'd always liked best about her and liked least now: she laid it out the way Ferra did, flat, in order. A land man beside the seat, in a new queen's first season. A man of the cursed shore, with the old land king for a father and the new land king for a brother, sitting on the step at her right hand at every audience. Every great house that didn't like her would say it on every stair in the city: that the land ruled through her husband. That she'd named the custom with the land's ledger and won the seat with the land's iron and sat on it with the land's man's arm round her. And on Friday he'd sat her on his knee in front of everyone, and made himself the one man in Aumere no lord would dare stand up to. They'd be afraid to offend him. They'd go round her to him. Or they'd go round him to her and blame him for every no.

"Every no," said Ilenne. "Today, three. The grove, the domes, the fool with the shells. In the street they say, the land king's brother told her."

"I didn't say a word."

"You sat back."

He thought about that. She was right. He'd sat back when she spoke, at once, in front of the whole hall, and every lord on the benches had seen a man obey his wife; and half of them had seen a man let his wife do the talking, and he couldn't do anything about which half saw which.

"I don't send you," said Ilenne. "I want that said. I don't send you anywhere."

"I know."

"Then..."

"I'll go," said Zale.

She turned her head and looked at him.

He'd decided it on the stages coming up, about the fourth landing, with the patient pilot waiting for him. He hadn't known he'd decided it until she said every no, and then he had.

"The fleet needs this ship," he said. "Eight hulls, and two of them on the water, and the other one's the K Tam, which is a bathtub with a mast. She's half the pickup pair. When somebody goes in the sea, it's us. My crew's on her, and they'll sail with her whether I do or not, because she's theirs." He looked at the gate. "And the further I am from that hall, the more your court has to deal with you. Not with me. You. They can't go round you to me if I'm a thousand miles down the coast."

"You choose this."

"I choose it."

She was quiet a long time.

"You're angry," she said.

And there it was.



He was. He hadn't known how much until she said it. It had been sitting in him since the glass floor of her mother's bell a month ago, under everything else, under the shot and the drowning and the change and the dark and the walk up the hall, like a stone in a boot that you stop noticing because there's always something worse; and she said you're angry on his own deck in the daylight, and it came up.

"Yes," he said.

"Say it."

"You knew what the binding made me." He heard his voice go up and didn't bring it down this time. "You knew from a child. Everybody at court knew. You knew at the door, when I said it, when I stood in the water and said it to you wet, what I was saying. That I'd be the thing between you and every house that wanted you. That I'd be the one they'd have to drown. And you let me find it out from a widow at a hospital who couldn't speak my language, through Imani, with signs."

"Yes."

"Signs, Ilenne."

"I know."

"I told you on the floor of that bell I'd have married you anyway. I would. I'd do it again tomorrow." He was standing. He didn't remember standing; his side told him about it a moment later. "That's not the point. It was never the point. The point is you looked at me every day for a month and didn't tell me to watch the water."

Below them, at the waist, Linnet Shaw put down the rope she'd been coiling and said something to the two men at the hatch, and the two men went down the hatch, and Linnet went down after them and pulled it nearly shut behind her, and Zale knew perfectly well that the three of them were standing at the foot of the ladder in the hold with their faces turned up to the crack, and didn't care.

Ilenne stood up too.

"I told you," she said. "In the bell. Why. That I thought there was time. That the house plays the open road first." Her Kaldo was coming apart; she was too angry to keep it in its pieces. "That I was wrong. I said. I don't say it better now. There is no better. I looked."

"I know there isn't."

"Then what do you want?"

"I want you to have told me!"

It went out across the gate. He heard it go, off the water and the breakwater's stone and back, and somewhere on the gate steps a child laughed and was hushed.

"I can't have told you," said Ilenne. "It's done. It's in the water. I didn't." Her colour was going, all of it, the grey and the dark and the slate patches she got in thin water, and she let it go. "You want a thing that can't be. I want you not to be angry. That can't be either."

"No."

"So."

"So," said Zale.

They stood on the deck in the afternoon sun with the crowd at the gate steps watching and the hatch open a crack behind them, and neither of them said anything for some time.

Nobody won it. He knew it while he stood there. She hadn't made a better excuse, because there wasn't one, and he hadn't stopped being angry, because he couldn't; and she wasn't going to grovel, and he wasn't going to pretend, and they both knew all of that, and had known it since the bell.

It ended anyway.

He sat down again on the folded sail, carefully, with his hand on his side. After a while she sat down beside him. Not a hand's width off. Next to him, her shoulder against his, the cool of her through her sleeve.

In the hold, very faintly, somebody said told you, and somebody else said shut up.



They crowned her on the Monday at the floor.

He stood for it. There was no seat on the step for a consort at a crowning; Ressar's office had told him so on the Sunday evening, kindly, with a drawing on a slate of where he'd stand, which was at the foot of the step on the right, level with the floor, a pace back. He stood there in a coat Abloh would have had words about, and breathed the sea, and kept his arms in, and watched.

The hall was fuller than on Friday. Fuller than he'd known it could be. Every bench and every tier, and people standing three deep behind the top tier against the glass, and more in the open water outside the ribs, on their tails, looking in. The old families on the left, not at the back now: someone had moved them forward in the night, and nobody said who. The daughter cities' people on a bench of their own near the seat, in coats Zale had never seen, cut differently, their throat stones a different colour, who'd come in over the weekend from the far islands and the outer banks to see the first queen of Aumere crowned, and whose colours all through the morning were a steady bright gold.

And on the left of the floor, a little apart from the land guests, two others.

He didn't know who they were. Nobody told him till afterwards. Two merfolk, a man and a woman, each with two attendants, in coats of a style he didn't know at all, darker and longer, with their hair bound up in a way nobody on the Rim wore it; and their colours, all morning, held, perfectly, not a flicker. Envoys, the watch officer said later. From two of the other seas. Uninvited and correct.

Ressar crowned her.

He did it without the song. Zale had been told there was a song, the crowning song, cut on a stone older than anything else in the hall, that the Keeper of the Stones sang over every new king; and that Ressar hadn't sung since the change he took forty years ago, when his house's last son died, and couldn't, and wouldn't pretend to. So he spoke it. He stood below the seat with the stone against his chest and said the whole of it, every line, in his flat dry voice that carried to the top of the hall without his raising it; and it went on and on, and Zale didn't understand one word in five of it, and it was the most beautiful thing he'd ever heard.

At the end of it the Keepers put something on her. He couldn't see what. A band of something pale at the throat, where they spoke from. Ilenne sat on the seat with it on and her colour came up, slowly, a deep clear grey with a shell's sheen on it, and held.

The envoys from the other seas bowed. Correctly. He watched them do it, the man and the woman, the bow and then the second smaller movement of the hand that Ilenne had taught the knights in the spring was the courtesy between equals; exactly right, exactly enough, not a hair more.

They left before the feast. Before dark. They went out by the far doors while the court was still on its benches, with their attendants behind them and their colours still held, and swam up through the open water outside the ribs and away into the blue-black, and he never saw them again.

He asked Ilenne that night what it meant.

"They count," she said. "They come. They see. They go." She shrugged, which she'd learned from him. "Now they know there is a queen. Now they know who she married. Now they go home and think."



The feast was at Sael, in the big kiln yard, which the Rim had been rebuilding for two weeks and had got far enough with to stand in.

The racks were new pine, still pale. The furnaces down the yard's side stood with their doors shut, cold, being mended, and the burst rings of fire stone had been raked out of their floors and gone wherever the watch had put them. The ground had been swept and swept again and still smelled of old fire under the smell of the cooking. And the whole Rim came. Up from Below on the stages and over from the islands by boat and down from every street on the spine, on their legs, in good coats and work coats and no coats at all, to the yard where the fire had been; and the furnace hands who'd been on the stair came, Zale saw them, some of them with their burns still dressed, and were given places near the front and brought food first, which he understood was meant.

There was a great deal of food. He ate as much of it as Imani would let him, which wasn't much.

At some point in the evening a lord of the Rim, one of the old families' men with a cup in his hand, came across to where the land guests sat on their benches near the yard's head and asked, in careful Kaldo, as guests are asked, what the land's people did to celebrate. What they played. What they sang. The Rim would like to hear it.

Zale saw Emeric look at Ferra.

Ferra already had the list out.

"This," said Emeric.



The sailors' set went first, because the sailors insisted, and because Finn had been sitting on his concertina all evening and wouldn't be denied.

Zale played the squeezebox for it. He'd brought it in its felt case on the boat that afternoon, against Imani's orders, and got it out on the bench, and Finn came and stood by him with the concertina, and Hesk and Linnet and the rest of his crew got up off the benches and stood round them without being asked, and they did the shanties. The old ones. The hauling songs from Emberfell's quays and the long slow capstan songs and the one about the cook that wasn't fit for the Rim's children and that the Rim's children, who didn't speak a word of Kaldo, joined in on by the second chorus anyway. The merfolk didn't sing. They stood round the yard on their legs and watched, and their colours went soft and warm and moved a little with the songs, like weed in a swell.

He played three. On the fourth, he looked up and saw Imani at the end of the bench, with her arms folded, watching his side.

He sat down.

"Sensible," said Imani.

"I'm always sensible."

"You're never sensible. You sat down because I was looking."

"That's sensible," said Zale.



And then they played the other thing.

Bram and Dagny had brought the gear off the K Discovery in the afternoon on hull four's battery cart, wheeled up from the quay by six men: the valve amp in its wooden case, and the two speaker cabinets taller than a child, and the cask drum and its stands, and the guitars in Abloh's felt; and Bram had sat on the cart all evening watching the battery bank's dials and telling anybody who came within a yard of it to go away. Emeric picked up his guitar. Arthur picked up the other one. Mira slung the bass. Bren sat down behind the cask with his sticks and an expression Zale had last seen on him at a rifle range.

Imani wouldn't. Ferra had asked her, quietly, on the bench, and Imani had said no in a voice that closed the subject, and gone and stood at the front of the crowd with her arms folded.

Ferra stood at the side of the cart with the set list. She looked at it. She looked at the four of them.

"One," she said. "Two. One, two, three, four."

And it came out of the cabinets.

Zale had heard it before, on the K Discovery's deck on still nights in the spring, with the amp turned down to almost nothing so the watch could hear the sea. He'd never heard it like this. It came out of the two cabinets and filled the kiln yard and went up off the rebuilt racks into the open sky over Sael and came back off the cliffs of the caldera wall, a sound like a storm coming down a gorge, if a storm could keep time; and the whole Rim stood in the yard with its mouth open.

Emeric was playing.

Not the way he'd played on deck in the spring. Zale had heard that: four songs, perfectly, every note where it was meant to be, the same every time. He wasn't doing that now. He was playing something Zale didn't know, and he was playing it wrong, a little, here and there, and then right, and then in a way that wasn't wrong or right but his, bent over the guitar with his locs swinging and his foot going and his face doing something Zale had never seen it do in a room full of people. Enjoying it. Not counting anything.

Arthur was terrible. Arthur was very bad and very earnest and had his tongue between his teeth, and every time he got through a run without a wrong note he looked so surprised that Mira laughed at him over the bass. Bren kept them all in time, which was the one thing Bren did better than anyone Zale had ever met, steady as a hammer, with his whole face lit.

And the Rim went through colours.

He saw it start at the front. The merfolk nearest the cabinets, the children first, and then the ones behind them, their colours coming up in the dusk with the sound: not held, not courteous, not anything a court would allow; just coming, and moving with it, gold on the loud parts and a deep pulsing red with the drum and something blue-green and quick that ran along a whole row of them at once when Emeric's guitar went up high. And then further back. And then the whole yard. A crowd of merfolk hearing a thing they'd never heard, every one of them a lamp, and every lamp going with the music, until the kiln yard at Sael under the evening sky was a field of moving light from the front of the cart to the far wall.

Emeric saw it. Zale watched him see it, from the cart, looking up from his hands. His fingers stopped. The sound went thin for half a beat, Arthur's guitar alone, wobbling; and Bren hit the cask twice, hard, without looking up, and Emeric's hands came back down on the strings and he was playing again, and his face was a thing Zale was going to remember for the rest of his life.

Under his feet, Zale felt the bass go down.

He felt it through the soles of his boots, through the yard's stone, through the Rim. Mira's long low notes, and Bren's cask, going down into the rock of the breakwater and the caldera wall and into the water under it, and on down, he knew, because sound went down in water faster than it ever went through air, through the black water to the city on its terraces and the domes and the Breath and the people Below who never came up.

Some of them came up.

He saw them. Halfway through the second song there were heads in the water off the yard's sea wall, dark heads in the dark water, and then more, and then people climbing out onto the wall on their legs, dripping, in no coats, people he'd never seen on the Rim, who stood along the top of the sea wall in a line and stared at the cart and the cabinets and the light of the crowd, with their own colours coming up slowly as they stood there, as if they'd forgotten how.

At the front of the crowd Ferra had come away from the cart and stood beside Imani. Neither of them was watching the colours. They were watching Emeric. Ferra had the set list against her chest and Imani had her arms folded still, and they stood shoulder to shoulder and watched him play as if they'd never seen him before, and Imani said something to Ferra without looking away from him, and Ferra nodded without looking away either.

Zale thought they'd be talking about it till dawn.



Between the second set and the third, while Bram changed a valve with his tongue out and Arthur shook his left hand to get the blood back into it, Zale found his brother sitting on the end of the cart with the set list turned over on his knee, drawing on the back of it with a stub of pencil.

A ring. Seats in a ring, rising, tier on tier, round a round floor in the middle; and in the floor a smaller ring, drawn twice, with an arrow up. And round the outside of the big ring, five long shapes going out from it like the arms of a starfish, or the points of a star.

"What's that?"

Emeric looked down at it, surprised.

"Somewhere for everyone to sit," he said, and folded the list in half and put it in his shirt.



It happened in the middle of the third set, in a slow one.

Zale was on the bench by the cart with his side hurting and his squeezebox in its case at his feet and a cup of something the Rim had given him that Imani had looked at and allowed. Ilenne had come down from the queen's place at the yard's head, where she'd sat all evening as queen with the band at her throat and the daughter cities' people round her, and she'd sat down on the bench beside him without anybody making way for her, because nobody needed to.

She wasn't looking at the cart. He realised that slowly. Everyone else in the yard was looking at the cart, at Emeric bent over the guitar and Mira's long fingers walking down the bass, at the colours. Ilenne was looking at him.

And her colour went.

Blue. It came up out of the collar of her coat, over the pale band the Keepers had put on her, up her throat, deep, dark, the blue of the sea under a hull in clear water when you look straight down; and it didn't stop at her throat. It went into her face, and her hands in her lap, and down her arms under her sleeves, he could see it through the cloth at the wrists. All of her. Blue, in the dusk, in the light of the crowd, on the bench in front of the whole Rim, which had stopped looking at the cart.

He saw them stop. Every face near the bench, and then further, turning, and the colours round them going quiet, one after another, like lamps being shaded, so that the blue of her stood out in the middle of them like the only lit window in a street.

He'd seen it before.

He knew that, looking at it. He'd seen a little of it at her throat at a rail in the spring with his coat round her and put it down to the cold. He'd seen it at supper in April, at home, across a table. He'd seen it come up and go down again a dozen times since, at a door, on a step, in the bell. He'd seen it on Friday in the hall, coming up her throat as he walked toward her, and he'd been looking at her face and walking on a wound and hadn't looked lower. He'd seen it. Everyone else had been looking at it for as long as he'd known her.

He'd never once asked.

"Ilenne," he said. "What is that?"

She didn't hold it. She didn't try. She sat beside him on the bench, blue to the fingertips, and looked at him, and answered him in her own tongue first, a short word he didn't know, and then, because he didn't know it, in Kaldo.

"It's you," said Ilenne. "It's for you. Only. From the beach." She didn't look away. "Everyone sees. Since the beach. Everyone but you."

He sat there.

Somewhere a long way off his brother's guitar was going up into something high and slow, and Bren's cask was keeping time under it, and the whole Rim of Aumere was watching him not say anything.

"I thought it was the cold," said Zale.
