Book 2, Chapter 5: Once Until One Dies

The table on the K Amara was in the chart room behind the bridge deck, and it sat eight if two of them didn't mind the bench, and on the Friday evening at eight it sat seven, with Zale on the bench by himself because he'd asked to be.

Ferra had the end nearest the door, with the book, because she always had it. Emeric was at the other end, with his back to the stern windows and the ring's lights behind him on the dark sea, seven riding lamps in a wheel and the small one at its hub. Ward had the chair at Emeric's right hand and Armand the chair at his left, with a rug over his knees that he'd said he didn't need and hadn't taken off. Imani had come across from the K Endeavor at seven and was beside Ferra, with her card box on her lap. Ilenne sat across from Zale, upright, with her hands flat on the table and her hood down.

Ward took the first item, because he'd said on Wednesday that he would.

"Most," he said. "Not all. That was said at Zale's rail the first night past the wall, to four people, and none of the four were me." He didn't look at Emeric. He looked at the table. "I'd have set the ring that night, not this week. I'd have had the pickup pair both warm. A boy's leg might be a boy's leg."

"Yes," said Emeric.

"That's all?"

"It's what there is. I heard it, I thought it was understood, and I didn't bring it to this table. You should have had it on the Monday." He said it plainly, to Ward and not to the room, and then he stopped, which Ferra thought was the hardest part of it for him. He'd always wanted to go on and give the reasons.

Ward was quiet a while.

"Next time," he said, "you bring me the word, and I'll decide whether it's understood." He turned a page in his own small notebook and drew a line under something. "That's done."

It was. Ferra saw it go into Ward's book and not come out again, which was where Ward kept the things he'd decided not to mention twice. She also saw two of the K Amara's officers, who'd been at the chart table outside the open door pretending to read the log, look at each other, and look away.

"Zale," said Emeric.

Zale put his elbows on the table.

"Right," he said. "Ilenne's going to tell you something. Some of it she told me last night and some of it she told me this afternoon and some of it I don't think she's told anybody. She's going to tell it in her words and I'm going to help where her Kaldo runs out, and I'd ask you all not to stop her till she's done, because it's hard enough in her own tongue." He looked at Ilenne. "Go on."



She told it in pieces, the way Ferra had heard her tell everything that mattered: short, in the present tense, with the small words left out and the large ones put down carefully, like stones in a wall.

There was a man. There was a house. She didn't say which. The court wanted the house close to the throne, and the house wanted to be closer, and her mother had no son and needed a man for her daughter, because by the custom the heir's husband ruled. So it was arranged that in the spring she'd be bound to the man of the house, at the door, before witnesses. And the night before it, she'd gone out of the door herself and swum east into the cursed water, which nobody went into, and kept going.

"And then Zale," she said, and stopped.

"And then me," said Zale. "On the beach. With a bident in her. Go on, you're doing well."

She went on. She told them what a binding was.

"In the water," she said. "Before witnesses. Both say it. Once." She looked round the table. "Until one dies."

"Why the water?" said Ferra.

Ilenne turned to her. "Because in the water a thing said before people is said for always. I teach your knights this. Every lesson. In the water, you do not say I will. Not by mistake, not for a joke. Only the king says a thing for his country, in the water, because he is the only one who can." She put her hand flat on the table. "A binding is the same. But not by mistake. On purpose. It is the one I will you mean."

"Then it's a promise," said Armand, from under his rug. "Made in a particular place, before particular people, which can't be taken back."

"Yes."

"What makes it count?" said Ferra. "If I said 'I'll pay you on Tuesday' in the water, before you and Zale, would I owe you on Tuesday for ever?"

Ilenne thought about that seriously. Her colour moved a little at the throat while she thought, the plain grey-blue it went when she was working something out.

"It must be whole," she said. "All of it. Not a piece. I'll pay you, that is a piece. I'll pay you Tuesday, that is whole." She folded one finger down. "It must be the right one who says it. You say it for you, it binds you. You say it for Kaldo, it binds nothing, you are not Kaldo." Another finger. "It must be seen. People who see, who can say after, yes, she said it." Another. "And it is for after. Only after. It does not make what is before into different."

Ferra took it down as she said it, in four lines, numbered.

Wet speech binds if: 1. whole (a complete saying). 2. said by the one with the right (for self, or the king for his country). 3. seen (witnesses). 4. forward only (binds what comes after, never what came before).

She read it back, and Ilenne listened with her head on one side and nodded at each line, and at the fourth said "Yes, exactly, yes," in the voice she used when Kaldo had for once got a thing right on the first try.

"And a binding's all four," said Ferra.

"A binding is all four. And both say it." Ilenne looked at her hands. "Two people, each one says it. Then it holds until one dies."

Ferra looked at what she'd written. A clerk's whole trade was the edge of a rule: where it stopped saying things. She'd read Kaldo's old charter, and the Board's rules, and Vantic's contracts, and the Isle Compact twice; she knew that the most important thing about any rule was usually what it didn't mention, because that was where somebody would put his foot.

"Ilenne," she said. "Is it written down anywhere?"

"No. Nobody writes it. Everybody knows."

"Then tell me exactly what everybody knows. Does it say who may be bound?"

Ilenne opened her mouth, and closed it again.

"Two people," she said slowly. "In the water. Before witnesses."

"Two people. Does it say what kind of people? Merfolk? Of the court? Of the sea?"

Ilenne's colour had gone very still. She looked at Zale, and Zale looked back at her and didn't help, because he could see where it was going as well as Ferra could, and was letting her get there by herself.

"No," said Ilenne. "It does not say. Nobody says, because nobody needs to say. Who else is there?"

"Does it say where they have to come from?"

"No." Barely.

The chart room was very quiet. Through the open door the officer at the chart table had stopped pretending to read.

"Then here's a thing," said Ferra. She said it to the table, carefully, because she knew how it would sound and she wanted it to sound like what it was. "It's a guess. I want that said first. I don't know their law. I know what she's told me in the last ten minutes. But if the custom says two people, in the water, before witnesses, both say it, once, and doesn't say which two people, then the court's bound by that as much as she is. If she were bound before she got to the door, properly, by all four, to somebody, then she can't be bound to anybody else. Not till one of them dies." She looked at Ilenne. "And the court would have to honour it. Unless they were willing to stand up in front of everyone and say out loud what the custom means by people. Which, if it's never been said, they've never had to decide."

"They'll decide," said Ward.

"Yes," said Ferra. "They'll say it for us. That's the risk. I'm not telling you it'll hold. I'm telling you where the gap is." She bent and wrote it under the four lines, in a hand a size smaller than her own.

Holds if: the water, witnesses, both say it. Doesn't say: who may. They'll say it for us.

"Somebody first," said Imani. "Marry her to somebody first."

"That's what I'm saying."

"It's not what you're saying. It's what you mean." Imani had her hands on the box. "I'm only saying it in fewer words."

Ilenne hadn't moved. Her hands were still flat on the table. And then her colour, which she'd held all evening, all week, at every lesson in front of thirty people, came up under her skin in one long wash from the collarbones to the hairline, gold, and she didn't stop it. She looked across the table.

"Zale," she said.

It wasn't a question. It was a name, said in the voice you'd use for the answer to one, before anyone at the table had so much as drawn breath to suggest anybody else.

Zale looked back at her across the table for what felt to Ferra like a long time. Then he looked round at the rest of them, at his brother, at Ward, at Armand under his rug, and cleared his throat.

"I'll translate," he said. "Since I'm the one who does. I think she's asking if I'll marry her." A pause. "Or she wants my boat."

Imani laughed. It came out of her before she could stop it, short and helpless, and she put her hand over her mouth. Ilenne's colour went a shade deeper. Ward looked at the ceiling.

"Both," said Ilenne, with dignity. "Is a good boat."



Then they told her what could go wrong. Emeric said they would, before anyone could start, and he said it to her, not to Zale.

"You know I'm going to say things before they happen," he said. "That's the rule. I'd say them if it were anyone. I'll say them because it's my brother."

"Say," said Ilenne.

"If a house wants the throne through you, then whoever's married to you is standing between that house and the throne." He said it flatly. "Not in their way. In their road. A man in your road, you go round him or you go through him. If they can't make the binding not count, the next easiest thing is to make him not be there."

"Yes," said Ilenne. "I know this."

"I know you know. I'm saying it so it's said."

Ward was next. He'd been waiting. "We're going into a foreign city," he said, "with eight armed hulls that they've every reason to be frightened of, and you're proposing to walk in at the door with a land man bound to their heir. Whatever that court feels about us before we get there, it'll feel it twice over after. You're handing them a grievance with his face on it. A land man, in their city, among people who can drown him in a room by opening a door." He looked at Zale, not unkindly. "I can't guard you in their water. Nobody can."

"I know," said Zale.

Armand had taken his hands off the cane. "And this," he said. "A binding the court can't undo." He spoke slowly, the way he spoke when he was teaching and wanted to be sure the back row had it. "That's what makes it worth doing. It's also what makes it a thing Zale can't undo. Until one of them dies. If it goes wrong for him in a year, or ten, or if he finds he can't live under the water, or among people who hate him for what he is, there's no door out." He looked at Zale under his white brows. "You'll be the husband of the heir of a country you can't breathe in. Is that understood?"

"It's understood."

"And?"

Zale scratched his jaw.

"I've got a boat," he said.

Ferra put that in the book too. She wasn't sure why. It seemed to her, at the time, the most exact thing anyone had said.

Nobody asked him for his answer. That was Emeric's doing; she saw him decide not to, and saw him look at Ward and Ward not ask either. It was Zale who said it, in the end, standing up from the bench with his cap in his hand.

"I'm not giving you an answer tonight," he said. "Not here. Not at this table with the whole of the K Amara's bridge listening at the door." The officer at the chart table outside became very interested in the log. "I'm going back to my ship and I'm going to sit on my deck with my own lamp and think about it for one night, the way you're meant to think about things you can only do once. And when I've decided, I'll tell her first." He looked at Emeric. "And then you."

"That's the right order," said Emeric.

"I know it's the right order. I'm telling you so you don't come over at dawn and stand on my deck looking hopeful." He put his cap on. "Ilenne. Are you coming in my boat or are you going to sit here being gold at everybody?"

"I don't go in your boat," said Ilenne. "Tonight you think. I go with Imani."

Zale looked at her a second longer, and then nodded, and went.



Emeric didn't go back to the K Discovery that night. Ferra hadn't asked him to and he hadn't asked if he might, and at half past ten he was in her cabin on the K Amara with his boots off and his back against the bulkhead, reading her book over her shoulder, which she didn't let anybody else do.

"Four lines," he said. "You numbered them."

"Things that are numbered get remembered in the right order."

"It's good. It's better than anything I'd have got out of her. I'd have asked her what a promise was and she'd have told me a story about a fish." He was quiet. "You found it in ten minutes."

"I found where it might be in ten minutes. It's not the same." She turned the page back to the four lines and looked at them. "I don't like doing it. Finding the gap in somebody else's law. It's what lawyers do to people. Read the rule for what it doesn't say, and stand in it."

"You're not standing in it. You're telling her where it is."

"And she's standing in it. With your brother." She closed the book. "I'm only saying it's a guess. I said it was a guess."

"You said it three times."

"Then I said it enough."

He put his chin on her shoulder. She'd known he would, about now; it was the hour he got tired of thinking and started looking for something warm. His breath was on her neck. She could feel him through the ring, the whole long hum of him, tired and pleased and worried in about equal parts, and under all of it a small steady thread that was only about her, and that he wasn't hiding, and never did.

"You're doing sums," he said.

"I'm not."

"You're doing the charge. I can feel you doing the charge."

"Seven hulls for an hour and forty minutes on their stones on Tuesday," said Ferra, "and a climb a day early. It doesn't go out of my head because you've put your chin on me." She didn't move away. "Work first."

"It's half past ten."

"Work first," she said, "fun later," and reached up without looking and put her hand flat on the side of his face, and kept it there, and went on thinking about the charge with her eyes shut, which he let her do, because he was learning.



Imani came across at seven the next morning, on the Saturday, in the K Endeavor's boat with Bettany at the oars, and came up the ladder and onto the bridge deck and said to Ferra, before good morning, "I asked her."

Ferra put her cup down.

"Alone," said Imani. "In my cabin, with the door shut. She sat on the bunk and I sat on the chair, and I asked her the way I'd ask anybody who was going to have something done to them that they couldn't have undone. In words. Is this what you want."

"And?"

"She said yes. She said it before I'd got to the end, and then she said it again after, because she said I'd want to hear it after." Imani sat down on the chart bench. She looked as though she'd been up most of the night, which she had; she'd had the boy with the leg as well. "Then I asked whether she understood what it would cost him. Not her. Him. Whether she'd thought about what Ward said, and what Armand said. A land man in her city. A husband in somebody's road. No door out."

"Had she?"

"She'd thought about nothing else since the wall, she said. Since before. Since the beach." Imani rubbed her eyes. "She said she knows exactly what it costs him. She said she's the only one at that table who knows what it costs him, because she's the only one who knows the court. She said she knows it, and she's asking anyway, because she wants him and because it's the only door out she's got, and she won't pretend it's only the one or only the other." She was silent a moment. "And then she said, 'You can tell him I said so. I would sooner he heard it from you, because you don't make it pretty.'"

Ferra looked out of the forward glass.

"Did you ask her the other thing?" she said. "What you wrote on the card."

"What else she's known all week and not thought to say." Imani almost smiled. "I asked. She said: lots. She said she grew up in it, the way we grew up knowing which end of a knife to hold, and she doesn't know which bits we don't know until somebody trips on one. She said she's sorry about the boy. She said, next time, ask her more questions." Imani looked at her hands. "She's right. I've been asking her about her body since the beach and never once about the sea."

They both looked out at the ring.

Zale's ship sat at its hub with the smooth water round her where the old one lay underneath, and the morning on her deck. There were two people at her lee rail. Ferra had been watching them for ten minutes before Imani came up the ladder; she'd seen Zale come up from below at a quarter to seven with his cap in his hand, and seen Ilenne come across from the K Endeavor in Zale's boat with one of Zale's hands rowing, because Zale had sent it for her at dawn. She'd seen them stand at the rail, a yard apart. She'd seen Zale say something, quite short.

And then she'd seen it from five hundred yards off, through the thick bright air, without a glass, the way you'd see a lamp lit in a window across a valley at dusk: Ilenne go gold. All of her. Her hair and her hands and her face, so bright that the people on the K Tam's deck turned round to look, and the knights on the K Amara's starboard apron stopped what they were doing and stood up. It didn't go. It stayed. She'd stopped holding it, and she wasn't going to start again, and Zale was standing at the rail a yard from a woman who looked like the sun had come up a second time on his deck and was very carefully not touching her, and then was.

The set by the chart table clicked.

"Zale," said Zale's voice, a little hoarse. "For the king."

Ferra picked up the handset and held it out across the bridge deck, and Emeric, who had come up the ladder behind Imani in his shirtsleeves and stopped dead at the top of it, looking out at the same thing they were looking at, came and took it.

"I'm here."

"I've told her," said Zale. "She was first. You're second. That's the order." A pause, and the swell in it. "I said yes."

Emeric didn't answer straight away. Ferra watched his hand on the handset.

"Good," he said. That was all, and his voice didn't do anything with it at all, and she felt through the ring how much it was doing.

"I thought you'd say more than that."

"I'll say more at the door."

"You'll say nothing at the door," said Zale. "You're the king. You'll stand there with your mouth shut so you don't marry the whole of Randillia to somebody by accident." Another pause. "She says, the door. Before we go in. In the water at the steps, before two witnesses, and then in."

"Who are the two?"

Ferra heard Ilenne's voice, very faint, near Zale's handset, and Zale putting it into Kaldo.

"Her mother's captain. The captain of the twelve, she calls him, who'll be at the door with the queen. That's one. And the watch. She says there's always a watch at a gate when a land ship comes in, and it's the crown's, not a house's, so it's nobody's. She says that matters. She says they'll say the captain's her mother's man, and they'll be right, so the other one has to be nobody's." A pause. "She says that's two. She says that's enough. She says ask Ferra if it's enough."

Emeric looked at Ferra.

Ferra opened the book to the four lines and read them through once more, and the line under them, and thought about a captain who served the queen and a watch officer who served no house, standing on a step in a city none of them had ever seen, and about a court that had never had to say what it meant by two people.

"Tell her," she said, "it's enough for the custom as she's given it to me. Tell her I don't know if it's enough for them."

Emeric told him. There was a murmur on the line. Then Ilenne's own voice, quite clear, closer to the handset, in Kaldo.

"Ferra. Nobody knows that. Is fine." The faintest pause. "Is a good boat."
