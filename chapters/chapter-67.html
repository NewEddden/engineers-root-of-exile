Chapter 67 - The Same Sin

He did not sleep the night after Ward told him, and not for the reason he expected. He had expected to lie awake on the hatred, plotting against it, fitting the avenger into his defenses the way he fitted every new threat, and he did some of that, in the first hour, the strategist's part of him laying Aldane's true shape alongside the wall and the guard and the walled lawful road and asking what changed now that the enemy could not be traded with. But the strategist ran out of work quickly, because the honest answer was that little changed in the near term, an avenger and a rival both wanted him dead and one was merely harder to talk down than the other, and once he had filed that he expected to sleep, and instead he lay in the dark and found that a different part of him had gotten hold of the story and would not give it back.

The part of him that would not let go was not thinking about the duke's hatred. It was thinking about the hallway. A man who read a woman off her surface and found her beneath him. A man who took her for a lesser thing than she was because she wore no mark of what she was, and who, on the strength of that misreading, decided her refusal counted for less than his own wanting, and moved on her, and laid hands on her to drag her when she made the refusal plain, because in his arithmetic the no of a lesser thing was not a real no, was only a hitch to be pushed through by a man of standing exercising what standing was for. Ward had told it as the origin of an enemy. Emeric lay in the dark and could not keep it in that shape, because the shape kept sliding, in his own memory, onto a different hallway, or not a hallway, a hall, a bright loud hall full of witnesses and wine, and a woman there too, and a man there too, and the man was not Aldane.

He had not thought about the diplomat in a long time, not really thought about her, only used her, the incident with her, as the pivot of his own story, the drunken folly that had bought him his exile and therefore, in the accounting he had made of his life, his salvation, the worst night of the prince's ruin turned into the door through which everything good had come. He had told himself the story that way so many times that the woman in it had gone flat, a plot mechanism, the visiting princess whose rank he had failed to know and whose rebuff he had leaned back against until the leaning became an incident and the incident became a sentence. He had never once, in all the tellings, stood in the woman's place in it. He had stood in his own, the fool who ruined himself and stumbled into grace. Tonight, with Ward's hallway laid over his hall, he stood, for the first time, where she had stood, and did not like the view.

He had not known her rank. Neither had Aldane known his mother's. Each of them had taken a woman for a thing she was not, a lesser thing, on the same logic exactly, the logic of a man who reads worth off marks and finds none and concludes there is none to find. And here, when he looked, there was a beam. He found it at once, the difference that lifted his own case up off the duke's, and it was real, and he could name it plainly. He had never touched her. He had not moved on her the way the duke had moved on his mother, had not laid a hand to drag her, had not carried it where Aldane carried it. His had been a drunk man's persistence in a crowded room, a recluse pushed out among more faces than he had seen in years, wine in him because he had needed the wine to walk through the doors at all, leaning in at a woman who had told him plainly to stop and not hearing the stop as a real thing because the wine and the noise and the long emptiness behind him had turned her no into a hitch in a pursuit rather than an end to it. He had not reached. That was the beam, and it held, and he refused to stand on it. Because the reach was where the two men differed and the root was where they did not, and the root, the contempt that let a woman's refusal weigh less than his own wanting, was the same seed in both of them, and the only reason his had not grown into what the duke's grew into was that he had been too drunk and too hollowed out and too small a man that night to carry it as far. That was not a difference in kind. It was a difference in degree and in appetite and in luck, and a man does not get to feel clean about being a lesser version of the thing he hates.

He turned it and turned it and it did not soften into anything he could stand on. The distance between what he had done and what the duke had done was true, and it was the distance a decent man would beg for, and it did not save him, because he could not point at it without also pointing at the thing that made the distance, which was not any virtue in him but only how far gone he had been, and no man is absolved by having been too drunk to finish what his own contempt had started. He looked, the way he looked for the flaw in any structure, for some load-bearing difference in the two men themselves, and there was none. The only differences he could name lived in the two women. His mother had been a mage and a delver and had put her man down with her own hands, and the diplomat had not been, and had needed the court and her rank and a scandal to answer what a queen had answered in a hallway with a look. That difference was not a difference in the two men at all. It was only a difference in the two women's power to punish, and it shamed him further, that the woman he had wronged had been the one with less of it.

The strategist tried, once, near dawn, to make it useful, to file it as leverage, a thing that let him understand Aldane from the inside and therefore predict him. Emeric let that thought stand for a moment and then found he did not want it, because to make his mother's assault into a strategic aid to fighting the man who committed it, by way of his own matching root, was a colder thing than he was willing to be even alone in the dark, and he had spent two years drawing lines against exactly the coldness that would find that thought useful. So he did not file it as leverage. He let it stay what it was, which was a fact about himself he had managed not to look at for two years and could not, now, unlook.

It changed nothing he could act on and everything about how he could carry the fight. He had wanted, since Ward's telling, to hate Aldane cleanly, the way you are permitted to hate a man who laid hands on your mother, and the clean hatred had felt, for a few hours, like a simple thing he was owed. He could not have it. Every time he reached for the clean hatred his own hall rose up under the duke's hallway and fouled it, because a man cannot cleanly hate another for a contempt he has carried himself and been spared, by drink and by luck and by his own smallness, from carrying all the way. He would fight Aldane. He had no choice about that, an avenger left alive was a death sentence pending. But he would fight him carrying the knowledge that the thing at the bottom of the duke, the reading of a woman as a lesser thing whose no did not count, was a thing that had once been at the bottom of him too, on a smaller stage, with worse wine and better luck, and that the only judge who had ever put that contempt down with her own two hands and no help from anyone was the same woman whose blood was the reason the duke wanted Emeric dead. He did not say any of it aloud. There was no one to say it to, and no words for it that would not make it smaller, and some things a man is only permitted to know in the dark and never to speak, because speaking them is a way of setting them down and this was not a thing he had any right to set down. So he kept it, and slept an hour before the light, and said nothing to anyone, then or after.
