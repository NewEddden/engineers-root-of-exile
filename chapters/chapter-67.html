Book 2, Chapter 28: The First Wet Word

The queen's runner came to the Keepers' bell at first light on the Saturday, and Emeric had been awake for an hour already, sitting on his pallet in the cold with his coat round his shoulders, because something had woken him and he didn't know what.

He'd felt nothing. That was the thing he'd lie awake over later. Somewhere above him in the night, five hundred feet up and a mile across the water, the Rim had burned, and a stone had died, and his brother had been shot and drowned and come back, and his ship had come in with the stone, and he'd slept through all of it on a pallet of woven weed with Armand snoring on the other, and felt nothing at all. The water near the Breath took his sense a few yards out and swallowed it. It had swallowed all of that.

Something had woken him anyway. He didn't know what. He'd sat up in the dark and stayed up.

The runner came up into the bell through the floor on her legs, a young merwoman in the queen's dark coat, and she didn't speak Kaldo and didn't try. She held out a fold of oilcloth. Inside it, dry, a slip of Ferra's paper, folded twice, written in Kaldo script in a hand that he knew had been steady because it always was.

Z shot, side, lives, Imani has him. I. says nobody says he lived. Ward crossed with the stone. Stay down if you can. F.

He read it twice. Then he read it again, slowly, a word at a time, as if it might say something else.

It didn't. Ferra never wrote anything that said something else.

The runner was watching him. She'd waited, he understood later, for first light: the queen had had the night's facts from the Rim and from her own house by midnight, and she'd held Ferra's paper till dawn so that whatever the land king did with it, she'd know what she knew first. He didn't hold it against her. He'd have done the same.

Armand was awake. He was sitting up on his pallet with his blanket round him, watching Emeric's face.

"Zale," said Emeric. "Shot. He's alive. Imani has him." He held the slip out, and Armand took it and read it, and gave it back. "And Ward's come in past the markers. With the stone."

Armand turned the blanket over in his hands.

"Stay down if you can," he said at last. "What does she mean by that?"

Emeric knew what she meant. He'd known as soon as he read it. It was the only line on the slip that was an opinion, and Ferra didn't give opinions in four words unless she'd thought about them for an hour.

"If I go up those stages now," he said, "at dawn, in a hurry, the whole court knows by noon that the land king ran to his brother. A man runs to a brother who's alive." He folded the slip and put it in the suit's chest pocket, behind Imani's card. "Ilenne's said nothing, either way, and asked everyone else to say nothing. Ferra's telling me not to say it for her with my feet."

He wanted to go up the stages. He wanted it so badly that his hands had closed on his knees. He opened them.

"Then you'll stay down," said Armand.

"I'll stay down."



The queen called the court at the floor at nine.

He'd never seen it so full. The benches were packed to the top tier and people stood behind them, against the glass, the way they'd stood behind the old families' benches in the dry hall. Everybody walked. Everybody's colour was held. And through the water, through the glass of his helm, the noise of them was something he could feel on his skin even without his sense: not words, a pressure, a hundred people thinking the same thing at once.

Ilenne wasn't there. He'd known she wouldn't be. A watch officer stood at his shoulder in her place, a middle-aged merman with a slate and careful Kaldo, sent down by Taurre, who put things into his glass from a hand's width off as she had, flatly, without any of her colour.

Taurre's report came first, by a runner, read by the officer from the slate to the court and put into Kaldo for Emeric a sentence behind.

The fire in the big kiln yard, an hour before dusk. The queen's guard drawn up to it. A shot on the breakwater at dusk, from a roof above the yard: the queen's daughter's husband hit, taken down the stages by the queen's daughter. A dead zone over the yard, six minutes after the shot. The seal's stone dead; the shutter down. Fourteen hands on the stair. The hatch barred from above. A man dead at the hatch by a land woman's gun; a man wounded by the other. The bell in the queen's house broken by charges, its shell gone. The husband taken from it by something from the deep, in the dark. The court's watcher at the door saw it go up. The land's ship came in on a sound like thunder, past the markers, to the zone's edge, and took people off the yard, and a skiff went over under it, and a woman was half drowned. The land's ship went out when the watch told it to.

And the watch's times. The officer read them very carefully. The zone was logged at the watch house by the yard's lamps going out, at a time cut on the slate. The land's ship was first heard from the Grey Mark's direction a quarter of an hour after that. A quarter of an hour.

The hall heard it. Emeric could see it hear it.

Then the old families stood up.

Not all of them. Five, six, the ones who'd gone slate in the dry hall. They went slate again now, from the throat down, and stood with it on them, and one of them spoke: the old man with the boy at his shoulder. He spoke slowly and not long, and the watch officer put it into Emeric's glass.

"He says: the stone came in. Past the markers. The thing that sank the First City, in our water, at the ring. He says the land king said it wouldn't. He says it did."

The old man sat down. His colour stayed.

And on the other side of the hall, from the benches where the Rim's house sat, a man stood up whom Emeric didn't know. Not Ushar; Ushar's seat was empty. A younger man, narrow-faced, in the house's coat. He spoke fluently and at some length.

"He says," said the watch officer, "the dead zone came to the Rim on the night the land's stone came to the ring. He says the court can count."

He said nothing about the times. Emeric noticed that he didn't.

The young man sat.

The hall turned to look at the land king standing on the polished floor below the seat; and Emeric understood that they were waiting for him to do what he'd done the first day, and stand there with his mouth shut behind his glass, and let the queen speak for what he'd said on the dry.

He'd thought about this since first light. He'd thought about it on his pallet with Ferra's slip in his hand and the cold coming up through the weed. A dry word was a land word. He'd given it in the dry hall, and it had been broken last night by his own ship, at his own knight's judgment, because he'd told the knight to break it if he had to. Everyone in this room knew it had been broken. What they didn't know was whose it was.

If he stood here with his mouth shut, it was the queen's. Her port order. Her water. Her daughter's husband. Her court. Everything the old families had said in the dry hall ten days ago would come true in front of them: that she'd let it in.

And there was only one place in the world where he could say a thing that nobody in this room could ever call a land word again.

He put his hands up to the clips at his neck.

The watch officer saw what he was doing and put a hand out, and stopped it in the air, and took it back.

Emeric undid the clips. He took the helm off.

The sea came onto his face.

Cold. Salt. Five hundred feet of it, pressing on his eyes and his cheeks and into his ears, filling his ears, and he braced, the way he braced the rest of his body, and held. His mouth was shut. He'd taken a breath off the valve before the last clip, the biggest he had, and it sat in him. The hall was a blur. Light and shapes and colours, the white of the lamp stones going soft and wide through the water on his open eyes. He could see the queen on her seat above him, a dark shape, very still.

He'd never spoken in the water. He'd been told it could be done; Ilenne said every child Below learned to, and a land man could, badly. You opened your mouth and let a little of the breath go and put the word on it.

He opened his mouth. The sea came in, cold, round his tongue. He held the breath behind it.

He said it. In Kaldo, slowly, word by word, every one of them going out of him into the water in a little rush of bubbles that he could feel go up past his face, his voice thick and strange in his own ears, a man talking through a mouthful of the sea:

"If my ship came past your markers last night, it was my word she broke. And not the queen's."

He shut his mouth.

The watch officer, a hand's width off, put it into the water in their own tongue, word for word, as he'd said it.

Emeric put the helm back on. His hands were cold and slow on the clips, and the officer did the second one for him, without asking. He pressed the purge, and the valve hissed and drove the sea in the helm out through the neck seal in a stream of bubbles, and breathed. He breathed. The water ran out of his ears and his eyes stung and the hall came back sharp through the glass.

Nobody had moved.

He'd seen a hall not know what to do before. He'd never seen it like this: a hundred of the deep's lords on their benches in the water with their colours held so hard he could see the effort in their throats, looking at a land king in a dripping helm on the floor below the queen's seat, who'd just taken it off and spoken a wet word into their court. His first. And it wasn't a promise. It was an acceptance. The breach was his. Said wet, it was his for ever, and nothing anybody did would ever move it off him onto her.

On the Keepers' bench, Ressar turned his head, very slightly, to the Keeper beside him, and said something short.

The watch officer heard it. He put it over afterwards, in the bell, when Emeric asked.

"He says it's well formed," he said. "That's all he said. Well formed."

The lean old merman on the second tier stood up. The one who always asked. He asked it courteously, as he asked everything: what did the land king say of his brother.

Emeric didn't take the helm off for that. He took the slate off the suit's belt and wrote on it in Kaldo capitals, and turned it to the watch officer, and the officer read it and said it for him into the water, as a man reading out what he'd been given.

"He says his brother is his brother's business, and the queen's daughter's."

The lean old merman thought about it. Then he inclined his head and sat down.



Taurre's runner came to the Keepers' bell again at two, and the watch officer came up into the bell with her and stood dripping on the rock and told Emeric the rest.

The wounded man at the hatch. Keddrin. The land healer had kept him alive in the zone with her hands, and the station had him now, and at first light, awake, with his hip bound, he'd said who'd paid him. A furnace steward. Maudrec. The watch had gone to Maudrec's yard at eight and found him in it, packing. Held, Maudrec had said by ten whose word he'd taken.

The officer didn't say the name. He didn't need to.

"The watch may hold," he said carefully, "anyone named to a fire in its water. Or to a dead zone. Named by one who was there. Until the court sits on it." He looked at Emeric. "Taurre has done this. At noon. In a closed bell, below the watch house. A great house's heir." He seemed to think the words over. "It's not done often."

"What does he know?" said Emeric.

"Ushar?" The officer considered it. "That he's held. That he's named to the fire. That the court will sit." He paused. "The queen's daughter came to Taurre at one. She asked that he be told nothing else. Nothing of the night. Nothing of who lived or who died. A held man is told what the court allows." He looked at the slate in his hand. "Taurre agreed."

Armand was sitting on his pallet with his notebook on his knees, listening.

"So he thinks it worked," said Armand.

The watch officer looked at him and didn't answer, which Emeric thought was an answer.

Ressar's Keeper came for Armand at three. Armand got up and put on his helm and went with her, out through the floor of the bell, to the hall where the stones were, as he'd gone every day since the first, on time, by Ressar's terms; and came back at six blue with cold and said he'd been shown the difference between a word cut for saying and a word cut for singing, and that it was much worse than he'd thought, and that he'd got it wrong four times.

"You can come up tomorrow," said Emeric. "With me."

"I know I can."

"Armand."

"I'm learning to read," said Armand, wrapping himself in his blanket, "and I said every day. Go and see your brother."



He went up the stages on the Sunday, the seventh, with the queen's pilot, and came up out of the pool at the head at a little after eleven into the cold chamber with its one lamp, and stood on the last step where his set found its voice, and didn't key it.

Ferra and Imani were on the step above.

He didn't know how they'd known the hour. He didn't ask. Imani had her bag on her shoulder and soot still under her nails, and Ferra had her book, and they stood on the step above him in the lamplight and looked at him coming up out of the water in his dripping helm with the pilot behind him; and he felt them. Both of them, along the bond, all at once, after eight days of nothing. Like a door opening in a cold house.

He took the helm off. Imani came down the step and put her hand flat on his chest, on the wet suit, over his heart, and left it there, and looked at him with her doctor's face, and then not with her doctor's face.

"You came back up," she said.

"You told me to."

"I did." She took her hand away. "Never a flask."

"Never a flask."

Ferra didn't come down the step. She stood where she was with her book against her chest and looked at him, steadily, the way she looked at a column of figures she'd added twice and still meant to add a third time.

"Zale," she said. "He's on his ship. Below decks. You should see him first."



Zale was in the after cabin below his own deck, on the bunk, with the scuttle shut and a lamp lit and the whole of his left side strapped, and he was grey, and he was laughing.

"You should see your face," he said.

Emeric sat down on the locker beside the bunk. He'd slept on that locker on the first night past the wall, under Zale's coat.

"Show me," he said.

"Show you what?"

"Linnet said your hands."

Zale held them up. Ordinary hands. A deckhand's hands, big, scarred across the knuckles, with the nails broken. He turned them over.

"That's the thing," he said. "I can't do it here. I've tried." He let his hands fall on the blanket. "In the water it's just there. Like breathing. I got up the wall with Ilenne on it and I wasn't even thinking. And I let it go on the steps because there were people, and it went, easy, all of it, out of me like a breath out. And now I'm in here in the dry and I can't get it back. I've lain here half the night trying. It's like trying to find a word you know." He looked at the lamp. "I don't think I can do it on land. I think it's the water."

There was a bucket beside the bunk. A wooden one, half full, with a cloth over it. Zale reached down and took the cloth off, and the smell of the sea came up out of it.

"Linnet's idea," said Zale. "Watch."

He put two fingers into the bucket. Up to the second knuckle. No further.

Emeric watched.

The skin between Zale's fingers moved. It went thin and tight, and darker, and spread; and the webbing came, between the two fingers in the water, and between the next two, and the thumb, out of the water, and on up. Over the back of his hand. Over his wrist. The skin of his forearm going hard and close-grained, in plates, fine, dark, all the way up his arm to the elbow and past it, under the strapping, and stopping at the shoulder.

Zale took his fingers out of the bucket and held the arm up in the lamplight and looked at it.

"There," he said.

He let it go. Emeric watched it go, from the shoulder down, the plates softening and going back into skin, the webbing thinning and drawing back between the fingers, easy, all of it, as if it had never been. An ordinary hand again.

"Do it again," said Emeric.

Zale did it again. Two fingers. The webbing, the plates, all the way up. And Emeric watched the water this time, not the arm, and saw the thing he'd half seen the first time and not let himself see.

The water had hardly touched him. Two fingers to the second knuckle. A spoonful of sea. And it had come all the way up his arm to the shoulder, as fast as it would have in the open water off the steps.

He didn't say so.

He sat on the locker with his hands between his knees and looked at his brother's arm going back to a man's, and kept it to himself, for later, when he knew what it meant.

"Does it hurt?" he said instead.

"No. It did the first time. In the bell." Zale lay back. "It doesn't now. It's just there." He was quiet a moment. "Ilenne's furious with me."

"For the whistle."

"For the whistle." He shut his eyes. "Imani says I'd have bled out by the time the bubble went. She says I did the arithmetic right. She also says I'm a stupid stupid man. She said that one twice."

"Let me see the side."

"She'll know you looked."

"She'll know anyway."

Zale lifted the shoulder that wasn't strapped, which meant yes. Emeric laid his hand flat on the strapping and looked through it. In under the left arm, below the ribs that had been cracked already, and out at the back. A tumbling round, so both holes were ragged and the flesh between them torn rather than cut. Imani's stitches held the edges together. Under them the body was doing the work, hot and busy and hungry, and somebody had set it going at twice the pace a body mends on its own. He could feel exactly how hard she'd pushed it. As hard as it would go, and not a hair harder.

He fed it some of his own, as he'd once fed his own cracked knuckles, and felt Zale's body take part of it and push the rest back. A knight's body did that. This did it more. Like leaning on a door with somebody heavy leaning on the other side.

"She's already doing that," said Zale, with his eyes shut.

"I know."

"She says twice is all anybody gets. She says she could push it harder and I'd grow silver in my side like a rite man." He opened one eye. "She says I'm heavy, too. To work on. She said it like it was my fault."

"You are heavy." Emeric took his hand away. "Three weeks before you're swimming. Maybe four. And it'll mark."

"Good. I'll show it to people." The eye shut again. "And before you ask, the other thing doesn't help. I tried that in the bucket too. It comes up as far as the strapping and stops, and the hole's just the same."

Emeric watched him. His brother, grey and strapped and laughing, in the cabin he'd been born to want, at the bottom of the sea's nearest edge.

"Zale," he said. "Can I ask you something."

"You're going to."

"Do you like the ocean because you're a sea dragon," said Emeric, "or are you a sea dragon because you like the ocean?"

Zale opened his eyes. He looked at the deckhead.

"I don't know," he said at last. "I've liked it since I could walk. Before I could walk, Mother says. I'd crawl into the sea off the slip at Emberfell and they'd fish me out." He turned his head on the pillow. "I don't know which came first. I don't think I want to."

Ferra was in the doorway. She'd come down the ladder without a sound, as she did. She had her book open on her arm and her pencil, and she'd heard the question and the answer, and she turned to the back of the book, past the columns, to a page Emeric hadn't seen, ruled in two columns, with a heading in her small square hand: Need, or nature?

She wrote Zale's name on the first line. And beside it, under both columns at once, across the rule:

The question is asked.



He walked the yard with Bram that afternoon, in the daylight, with the smell of it still on everything. They went by the station first. The burned furnace hands were in the long back room on their pallets, with the station's healers going between them; Orrane among them, who looked up at the land king in the doorway and inclined her head and went back to the man under her hands.

The racks at the far end were black heaps of ash and charcoal and broken pots. The big furnaces along the yard's side stood cold and dark with their doors open, and in each one, on the stone floor of the firebox, a ring of shattered stone. Fire stones. Burst. The fragments lay round where each bank had sat in a rough circle, grey and dead and sharp.

Bram was on his knees at the third furnace with Dagny beside him, with a glass, and a pencil, and the five words written at the top of a fresh sheet, which Emeric noticed and didn't mention.

"Look at them," said Bram, without looking up. "All of them. Every bank. Look how they went."

Emeric looked. He didn't need the glass. He could feel it, faint, even now, the dead stone giving off nothing and the shape of where it had given off something; and the shape was wrong. A stone that went in a fire went alone, or with its neighbour. These had gone in rings. One into the next, into the next, outward from the middle of each bank, every ring bigger than the one before, and the banks one into the next along the yard.

"That's not a flue fire," said Bram. "A flue fire doesn't do that to a fire stone. Nothing that happens to a furnace by accident does that." He sat back on his heels. "Somebody laid these to go. One into the next. On purpose. So the whole yard went at once, and made a zone big enough to take the yard and the stair and the chamber, and no bigger." He looked at the sheet. "I'm not writing down how. I'm writing down that it was done."

"Seen?" said Emeric.

Bram glared at him.

"Seen," he said. "Every ring. I've been round them all."

They went down the yard stair. The hatch stood open and the bar lay on the cobbles beside it where Ferra had dropped it. Forty-one steps, a turn, and the landing; and the shutter was up again in its slots overhead, with its catch hooked under its lower edge, as Bram had drawn it. The queen's guard captain's men had come round the long way in the night, down the guests' stages and through the chamber from below, and wound it up on the winch on the chamber side. Bram had talked to the chamber's keeper that morning, by hands.

Bram stood under the shutter and looked up at it with his head back.

"It dropped," he said. "Exactly as I drew it. The stone went, the catch went, the bronze came down its slots and sat in its groove, and the sea came up the chamber to it and stopped." He was quiet a moment. "Do I get to change the word?"

"Do you?"

"Imani didn't see it fall either. She heard it, from above the turn, and came round and found it down." Bram put his hand flat on the bronze. "That's heard. That's not one of the five. I'll leave it guessed, and put her line beside it in her words."

On the way back up through the yard they passed the glassmakers' yard's young man, the one who'd answered all Bram's questions in the second week with his hands and a burnt stick on a board, and had come over on the evening boat to steady the drum's foot. He was standing at the arch with two others. He saw Bram, and he saw Emeric; and then he looked past them, at Ferra and Imani coming along the street behind with the revolvers on their hips.

He turned his back. Not quickly. He turned round and faced the wall, and stood there, until they'd gone by.

Bram stopped in the arch.

"He watched," said Ferra, quietly, at Emeric's shoulder. "From the arch. Last night. He saw both."

Bram stood there a while longer looking at the young man's back. Then he went on, without a word all the way to the landing, and at the landing he said, to nobody, "He showed me how they anneal," and that was all.

Ferra and Imani didn't seem to mind it. Emeric thought Bram minded it more than they did.



Ward came in on the watch's boat at four, from the Grey Mark, with the watch's leave and a watch officer, his first time ashore at Aumere; and asked to be taken to the skiff woman's door.

Her name was Esseth. The watch had it. She lived on the third island in the skiff hands' row, a line of low black houses along the water with their boats drawn up on a ramp in front of them, and her door was the fourth, and it was open, and her son was sitting on the step.

The boy off the stair. Dauvre. Emeric knew him by the soot still in his hairline. He looked up when the land men came along the row: the watch officer, and the land king, and a big grey land knight with his hands at his sides.

Ward stopped in front of the step.

Emeric stopped behind him. Two paces behind, a little to the side, where Ward would know he was there and the boy would see that he'd stopped there on purpose.

Esseth came to the door. A lean brown merwoman on her legs with a cough still in her and a shawl round her shoulders. She looked at Ward, and knew him; not his face, his coat. The coat of the ship.

"Tell her," said Ward to the watch officer, "I'm the captain of the ship that put her in the water last night." He didn't soften it. "Tell her I gave the order to come in, and I gave the order to hold where we held. Tell her my ship's wind turned her boat over, and that it was mine. Not my men's. Not the king's. Mine."

The officer put it over. Esseth stood in the doorway and heard it.

"Tell her I'm sorry she went in the water," said Ward. "Tell her I'd do it again, because there were fourteen men on a stair in the smoke and her son was one of them. Both of those. I'm not going to tell her one without the other."

The officer looked at him. Then he put it over, both of them.

Esseth didn't answer. She looked at Ward. Then at her son on the step.

Dauvre had got up. He was standing on the step, between his mother in the door and the land knight in the row, and he looked at Ward, and Emeric watched his face try to do two things at once and not manage either. He said something. Fast, rough, in his own tongue, to Ward, and then turned and said something to his mother, and then turned back.

The watch officer waited a moment before he put it over.

"He says: you got him off the stair," said the officer. "He says: you nearly drowned his mother in front of his street." He paused. "He says he doesn't know which. He says don't ask him which."

"I'm not asking," said Ward.

Nobody asked him.

Ferra paid. She'd come along the row behind them with her book and the chest's iron, and she counted it out on Esseth's doorstep for the skiff and the gear and the days, and said the sum aloud, and Esseth took it without looking at it, as Nimo's mother had. Ferra wrote the names in the book. Esseth. Dauvre.

Emeric stood behind Ward the whole time and owned it. He kept his mouth shut and didn't step forward. It was his order, and Ward's judgment, and Ward's ship's wind, and he stood two paces behind his knight on a stranger's doorstep in the evening light and let every person in the skiff hands' row see him stand there and not take any of it off him.

They walked back along the row afterwards toward the landing, the two of them, with the watch officer ahead.

"I'd do it again," said Ward.

"I know."

"I'd come in again. Same time, same way. I'd ask twice and not wait." He kept his eyes on the officer's back. "And I'll tell you the other thing, since we're saying things. The word was a mistake to give."

Emeric walked.

"Not the order to me," said Ward. "The order was right. If you have to, come, it's mine. That was right, and I'm glad you gave it, and I used it. The word in the hall. The one to the court. It won't come in." He said it quite flatly. "You gave a whole court a promise about a ship you weren't on, in water you'd known a month, with a stone you can't throw away. And then you went where you couldn't hear anything, and left me to break it." He looked at the sea. "I'm not angry. I'm telling you it was wrong."

"It bought her something to stand on."

"For a week," said Ward. "And now she's standing on a broken word, and so are you, and you've had to go in front of them with your helm off and say it was yours. Wet." He was quiet a while. "You did that well. I heard. It doesn't make the first one right."

Emeric didn't answer that. He thought about it all the way to the landing and found that he couldn't, honestly, and that Ward had known he couldn't before he said it.

At the landing Ward stopped.

"I'm going back out," he said. "To the mark. With the stone." He looked at Emeric. "We disagree."

"We disagree."

"Good," said Ward, and went down the steps to the watch's boat.



The hard room, that night, under the black water.

They'd put the lamp out. Bram was asleep beyond the curtain. Emeric lay on his pallet with Ferra on one side of him and Imani on the other, as they'd lain every night until the summons, and he'd thought that he'd sleep, and he didn't.

Ferra told him first. In the dark, quite low, in her accounts voice, the one she used to read a ledger to a committee: the serial on the rifle, and whose it was, and the board, and how their own honest entry was the only proof they had. The old families' yard on Wednesday, and the iron, three of the eight bars, and Maudrec's name. And the Wednesday night, at the table, Arthur saying take him tonight, and her saying no. Holding his line. His words, the ones he'd said in the first week about records and attacks, which she hadn't agreed with when he said them.

"Arthur said he'd run, or burn something," she said. "He didn't run. He set a fire. Two nights later, in his own yard." She stopped. "I held your line and the fire was in Maudrec's yard. I'm telling you so it's said. I'm not asking you anything."

He knew she wasn't. She never did.

Then Imani. Not in the accounts voice. In her own. The hatch, and the smoke, and the man with the spear coming at Ferra, and the two shots, and then the hip: what it had looked like when she knelt by him in the zone with her healing gone and her two hands on it, where the round had gone in, what it had broken, how much blood, how long she'd held it before they carried him out across the edge and she could put something into him. His name. Keddrin. She told it the way she'd tell it to another doctor. She didn't leave anything out.

"He'll walk," she said at the end. "With a stick. For a long time." She was quiet. "I'd shoot him again. I'd kneel by him again. Both."

He put his arms round them.

That was all he had. He didn't have anything to say that was worth saying to either of them, so he didn't; he lay in the dark between them with an arm round each and held on. Imani's head came down on his shoulder. Ferra lay very still with her face against his neck.

Nobody slept much.

Some time after two he felt Ferra's hand move against his chest. Not to him. To the peg by the pallet's head where her holster hung with the revolver in it, and her fingers found the cylinder in the dark and went round it. Slowly. One, two, three. He felt each chamber go under her thumb. Four, five, six. And round again.

He didn't stop her.



Corbin's call came through the line room at eight on the Monday morning, booked, Ambrose's voice first saying the regent's son for the king; and then Corbin.

Not Rowena. Not a message passed on. His own voice, out of the set, a little stiff, the way it went when he'd decided to do a thing and was doing it before he could change his mind.

"I've a question," he said. "If you've a minute."

"I've a minute."

"It's the stewards. The holdings' weekly returns." The relays hissed under him. "They've started coming in good. All of them. Every week, every holding, nothing wrong, everything up a little on the week before." A pause. "Six weeks running. And I've noticed. I know what the north looked like in March. Nothing's that good."

Emeric sat on the edge of his pallet with the set in his hand.

"They're telling you what you want to hear," he said.

"I know they are. I want to know how to stop them without making every one of them think I've caught him lying."

Emeric thought about it. He thought about the long table at home, and Ferra across it.

"Ask every one of them," he said, "for one bad number a week. Just one. The worst thing in his return. Make it a line on the form, at the top, so it's the first thing he writes. And when it comes in, thank him for it. In writing. Every time." He paused. "In a month they'll be fighting over who's got the worst one."

The set was quiet.

Then Corbin laughed. A real laugh, short, surprised out of him.

"I'll try it on Harrold first," he said.

"Harrold doesn't have a bad number."

"That's what I mean," said Corbin, and laughed again, and then said, more quietly, "Thank you," and was gone.

Emeric sat with the set in his hand a while. It was the first time Corbin had ever called him for anything.

Then he got up and put on the Mark II, piece by piece, with Imani doing up the back and neither of them saying much, and the coat over it, and went down to the head of the stages. The court would sit at the floor on Tuesday to hear the furnace night. The queen had sent to say so. He'd be at the floor by evening.

Ferra walked him down. On the last stair she checked his clips, both of them, twice, with her thumb.

"Stay down if you can," she said.

"I will."

He went down the step into the black water.

He didn't sleep that night at the floor. He lay on his pallet in the Keepers' bell, deaf, with Armand breathing on the other side of it and the cold coming up through the weed, and thought about Ward on the doorstep and Esseth's hands, and Ferra's thumb on the cylinder in the dark, and the slip in his pocket that said stay down if you can, and a sea he'd known a month; and about home, a thousand miles off and more, where the relays hadn't moved in five weeks, which was exactly what anybody listening would know.
