The Great Uplift

Randillia first, then the crowns, the Empire and the mechs. Proposal version 1.4, November 18. Not canon yet, and not saved to the notes repo or the Project.

This is the read-aloud version. It gives the plan as it stands now, with the version history taken out. The conflicts and how each one was ruled are at the end.

Part One. What you asked for

Duvrenne is already friendly. You want every other kingdom allied, and you want to share Kaldo's tech so everybody everywhere has what they need. Kaldo could force it with its technology, but that isn't Emeric's way. The uplift has to be accepted, not imposed.

You want Emeric to send them all an invitation. Your worry was that the moment you try to sell something to someone, good or not, you become the lesser party. You also hate how little the other kingdoms, and even the Empire, have mattered in the story so far.

The order you set: Randillia is uplifted first, in big sections, then a time skip, then the invitations. As king, Emeric has all of Randillia's mages, and with clear directions they should build faster than Earth ever did. You want rapid development.

You want the mechs shown off. Some of the roots group ask whether that's smart, and Emeric explains that it forces the others to move quicker, with the Drifters adding to the danger. You want Kaldo to offer the soldier mechs for sale, paid for in monster parts and coin, and to tell buyers it's fine if they try to reverse engineer them, because they can't. You want a demonstration in the museum's central stadium.

And you want binding rules. If a sold mech is used on civilians, Kaldo comes and collects it. Buyers are free to make war on Kaldo, and Kaldo promises they won't win. No sugarcoating: they need to understand that Kaldo could have taken the tyrant's road, and chose not to.

Part Two. The governing idea

Selling doesn't automatically put Kaldo beneath a buyer, and giving something away doesn't automatically humiliate the one who receives it. Bargaining power comes from alternatives, legitimacy and dependence, not from who sends the first letter. The real dangers are more specific.

  • Charity humiliates a crown when nobody asked for it, or when it makes that crown visibly dependent. Requested disaster relief, open textbooks and jointly funded public works don't. What matters is who chooses, who owns, who operates and who gets the credit.
  • A finished future designed by Kaldo makes everyone else's sovereignty look like a formality. Even a generous contract can hide a monopoly on parts, repairs, medicine, trained staff, banknotes or signatures.
  • The uplift must not create captive markets. A foreign crown has to be able to refuse a contract, change a design, hire other builders, and keep its water and power running after Kaldo leaves.

So the invitation is to build together, never to accept Kaldo's answer. Emeric can send the first letter without begging, and the other crowns set the agenda of the meeting after it.

Emeric's mistake is an understandable one. He's an engineer, and he sees water works, clinics, rail and power as solved problems. Other rulers see a lasting shift of power toward Kaldo. Both views are rational, and the arc tests his.

The theme runs in three steps. Early Emeric thinks: I can build the things that will save people. Middle Emeric thinks: I can teach others to build them. Mature Emeric learns: other people can choose, invent and govern without me, even when they disagree with me. The best proof of the uplift is a Kaldo design made obsolete because another people improved it.

The question the arc asks is this: can Emeric make the world safer and richer without becoming the power that decides its future? Every phase answers it through a decision someone other than Emeric makes.

There are three kinds of victory. A population gets working essential services. An independent government gains the ability to maintain and improve them. And independent states restrain Kaldo on something that costs Kaldo. Spectacle proves capability. It doesn't prove consent.

One honest limit. The plan can't promise that every isolated household in the kingdom has electricity, rail and a hospital within three years. "Uplift Randillia fully" means universal minimum access to essentials as the national goal, with coverage and remaining gaps reported honestly. Heavier industry reaches the towns best suited to it first.

Part Three. Phase One: Randillia first

The engine: the Mage Corps

The Mage Corps is a paid crown service of working mages, voted by the Assembly as a budget line on Wednesday 25 October, year 19, in Book 3 Batch 7, chapter 64. That batch is still waiting for your approval, so the date is placed but not yet canon. The Corps is hired, not conscripted. Its first intake is forty-one of the capital's freed lift and utility mages.

The work goes by gift. Earth mages cut rail beds and tunnels and lay foundations. Water mages dig canals, build dams and dredge harbours. Fire mages supply kiln heat while furnaces are built. Air mages ventilate tunnels. Lightning mages charge battery banks. Gravity users handle heavy lifts. The mundane method always runs beside the magic, by the layer rule.

Big civil works go about two to three times faster than steam alone, which is an estimate, because on Earth the slowest steps were moving earth and materials. With clear plans, the building can outrun Earth. But mages speed up physical work, not knowledge. Training doctors, getting chip yields up and proving a bridge will last fifty years still take years. Once earthmoving stops being the bottleneck, skilled staff, material supply, inspections and know-how become the bottlenecks. The two-to-three-times figure applies to selected physical projects, never to health, education, manufacturing and government as a whole.

The pool of casters grows on its own. The rising air and the free Kindling, healing for everyone from spring of year 20, leave many people in Kaldo's states half-lit.

Show the hard parts, then skip

Readers should see the transformation, but they don't need more of Emeric solving every boiler. In Book 3 he solves problems of scale, delegation, consent and money. Four real set pieces come before the skip.

  1. The council of needs. Ferra, Imani, the Works and the regional stewards make Emeric face capacity, cost and jurisdiction when he wants to promise everything at once.
  2. The district that turns down his prestige project. He arrives to approve a power station, and a local doctor shows him the town can't get rid of its sewage safely. The station waits. The king picks the unglamorous fix.
  3. The network at work. A Mage Corps cutting on the Capital Line finished in a single season, with Tess's crew timing it against the steam shovels. Other characters solve the technical details.
  4. The cost of speed. On a fast Mage Corps job, the permanent design is sound, but the temporary staging and the safety sign-off are rushed to meet an opening date that was announced too early. Workers are injured. The inquiry names the skipped approval, the officials responsible and the budget pressure behind it. It doesn't scapegoat a young mage or blame the Church. Work pauses, the injured are compensated and the inspectorate changes the rule. Emeric publicly withdraws a date he promised. Don't invent a death count or overwrite an event that's already seeded.

Where these go: Book 3 Batches 7, 8 and 9 already run from September of year 19 to 8 December of year 20, and none of these four scenes is in them. So they go into Batch 10, which starts on 9 December of year 20, and into the first year-skip batch in year 21. Batches 7 to 9 aren't reopened.

Then comes the skip, through year 22. After it, come back to a clinic run by locals, a machine shop making its own parts, a town arguing over its next rail connection, and a village that's still underserved. A capital skyline isn't a measure of coverage.

Measuring the uplift instead of a montage

At each time jump, the Works publishes a district-by-district service ledger. It records access, reliability, staffing, maintenance money and complaints, not just miles of rail or pipe laid. It's a story device, not a spreadsheet for readers, and foreign observers are free to challenge whether the numbers measure working service.

  • The essential floor: safe water, or a firm path to it; sewage and waste safeguards; urgent medical care and referral; basic education and public information; and a dependable way to move food and emergency supplies. Each district chooses how, and not every village needs a full power grid.
  • The industrial layer: electric service, rail freight, machine shops, phones and larger hospitals go where crews, demand and supply chains can keep them running.
  • Public control: residents can dispute fees and siting, local operators are trained and paid, faults get reported, and outages and repairs are published. Nobody loses land or a living just because the king says the new line is useful.
  • A visible shortfall: one district is still badly served in year 22. Ferra has to defend the allocation at an Assembly hearing, and Emeric can't fix the record by showing up and casting.

By year 22, Kaldo's states and the industrial towns have full utility networks. Market towns have clinics with the Kindling, clean water, freight and phones. Rural areas are uneven, and the book says so. Local crews keep things running with no Kaldo staff on site. Public argument moves from whether to modernize to what comes next, and who decides.

The proof should be human-sized: a village arguing over water fees, a merchant losing a monopoly and fighting the new road by lawful means, miners refusing a rush order from Kaldo and being proved right, a local engineer simplifying a part Kaldo over-designed, and parents fighting over who runs the school.

Outside eyes, and the hollowing

During the skip, the other kingdoms start to matter through their own people. A Velmoor border lord asks for a clinic before his own court will, and he becomes a recurring character. An imperial tax clerk notices Randillia's receipts, migration and freight shifting, so the Empire is shown through a competent observer instead of an exposition dump.

Then there's the hollowing. Randillia's lit towns pull nurses and machinists out of the other crowns. An envoy says it to Emeric's face:

Your border towns are taking our nurses and machinists. You call it freedom of movement. We call it the hollowing of our country.

He's right. Emeric's answer is help with training, local wages and opportunity at home, with the partner crown in control. Some migration continues, and the book doesn't pretend otherwise.

Part Four. Phase Two: the invitations, around year 22

Two invitations, kept apart

The first is the Gathering of Works and Peoples, announced in Emeric's public letter. Every crown, region and recognised people is invited to show its knowledge, crafts, art, history and practical problems. Kaldo provides the space and logistics, and the exhibitors control their own displays. It's held at the Kaldo Grand Museum and Cultural Complex. The Museum of Peoples and Civilizations gives each crown an empty hall to fill, and the Gathering opens around the central stadium.

The second is the Conference of Shared Works, a separate and later table where governments that want to take part send delegates on standards, training, trade, public works, safety and money. Duvrenne hosts it at Kesrath, and Alina convenes it. Whoever is invited to the table is never the one begging.

Going to the Gathering commits nobody to the Conference. Otherwise the hospitality would be a trap.

What the invitation promises

  1. Every delegation chooses what it reveals and keeps ownership of its work.
  2. Nobody has to adopt Kaldo's laws, currency, machines or research priorities.
  3. Attendance isn't tied to trade, religion, marriage or defence.
  4. The agenda is open to additions and criticism.
  5. Delegations can inspect Kaldo's failures and running costs, not just its successes.
  6. No free machines are promised. Every later project needs its own agreement on money, staff and control.

A draft of Emeric's letter:

The works of Kaldo have grown because we have learned, borrowed, tested, failed, and tried again. No kingdom has exhausted what can be known. We invite the peoples and governments of Ostrellin to bring the knowledge they value, the questions they cannot yet answer, and the work they wish others to understand. You will not be asked to become Kaldo. Should you wish to build together, let us decide the terms together.

The Gathering on the page

Delegates aren't fooled by staging. Experienced diplomats know a showroom when they see one, so their experts inspect the real running costs and the failures.

The best scene goes like this. A foreign delegate challenges a Kaldo demonstration because they've spotted a real flaw. Kaldo's engineers admit it. A second delegation's local material or technique fixes it. A third insists the repaired design must be buildable without parts only Kaldo makes. The crowd watches a breakthrough between peoples happen, not a sale.

There's comedy too. The rule is that nobody from Kaldo brings up tech first, and Ferra has to stop Emeric from improvising a lecture on ten things nobody asked about. Emeric is publicly proud of another people's achievement.

The Gathering has to allow a real refusal. One delegation turns down the museum's shared catalogue because Kaldo's curators would have the final say over that people's own history. Its engineers still take part and its artists get full credit, but it won't hand its story to the host. Kaldo gives up control of those labels rather than punishing the refusal. No treaty is offered for signature at this event, and no military buyer is recruited there.

Later, at the separate Conference, a government rejects the first standards draft because Kaldo's inspectors would have more rights than foreign ones. Ferra and Emeric genuinely got the wording wrong. The refusal forces a better agreement at Kesrath months later.

Part Five. Every crown gets its own reason

Duvrenne: from favoured friend to equal

Canon already has Waylon and Alina, the Isle Compact, the shared Isle, and the oil bought at Duvrenne's price. Duvrenne wants access to standards and research, secure trade, its own courts, protection from depending on Kaldo, and leadership that its marriage alone can't give it. It brings treaty craft, a neutral venue at Kesrath, administration, money and a bridge to the sceptical crowns. Its sacrifice is giving up its privileged access so the others aren't second-class partners.

The scene: Duvrenne sides with Velmoor against an overbroad Kaldo inspection clause. Emeric is furious for a moment. Waylon and Alina make it clear that loyalty isn't obedience, and the agreement comes out stronger. The payoff: a Duvrenne-led body settles a dispute between Kaldo and another crown without Emeric deciding it, and Alina gets a diplomatic win that doesn't come from her marriage.

Velmoor: cooperation, with enforceable limits on Kaldo

Velmoor is a free crown bound to the Church. Its lords are the Wardens of the Shore, and it holds the Book 2 trade contract. It wants control of its own territory, protection from foreign watching and interference, practical gains in health and transport, and proof that its own knowledge isn't disposable.

The scene: a Velmoor envoy asks whether Emeric's successors could find and watch a political opponent inside Velmoor. "Trust me" isn't an answer, so Emeric accepts enforceable limits, with complaints, inspection and remedies. Velmoor wins a binding limit on Kaldo, then chooses its first joint civilian project. Its break from the Church is the biggest win of all, and it shouldn't come cheap.

Solreth: fair terms and its own industry

Solreth is "the human cradle that wouldn't kneel," a free crown bound to the Church, with two calendars. It wants market access, its own manufacturing, fair trade terms, and infrastructure that doesn't depend on Kaldo's parts and banknotes.

The scene: a Solreth minister turns down Emeric's very generous turnkey hospital because only Kaldo could maintain it. She demands blueprints, instructors, the right to make parts locally and a repair-school contract. Ferra sees it's the better deal. Later, Solreth develops a cheaper, easier-to-maintain design and sends it back to Kaldo, so the knowledge flows the other way. Don't invent a rich resource or an ancient library for Solreth without your ruling.

The Imperial Core and the emperor

The emperor is a protagonist, not a prop. In canon he's a compromise emperor crowned by the Church. Randillia and Duvrenne are his subject kingdoms, and he has a real legal claim that subject kingdoms can't make treaties with foreign crowns without the Core. The Empire funded both sides of the Isle war, and its banks blocked Kaldo's and Duvrenne's credit.

The Church endgame, confirmed in the repo, gives him his turning point. In the week of the Everywhere Night, late in year 23, a penned Church beast breaks loose under Ostrel, and the emperor's soldiers see a Church machine fight it. The emperor breaks with the Church. Kaldo enters Ostrel at his invitation early in year 24, and the Church's machines go to the emperor, read and silenced by Tess.

Before that, his arc runs like this. At first he opposes Kaldo legitimately. He sees Kaldo shifting taxes, transport, merchant loyalty and the balance between subject crowns, and he's right that a private club of rich crowns could bypass smaller states and the Core's lawful procedure. In the middle, he uses a real power: he voids a compact negotiated without imperial consent. He isn't bluffing, and he wins concessions for the Core and for the smaller subject states. He also starts a modernization program for the Core that Kaldo doesn't control. At his turning point, an imperial district loses its educated workers or suffers a disease that could have been prevented, and a young imperial official exposes graft. He has to choose between the court's comfort and a government that works. His victory is a lawful imperial framework for cross-border civilian works that doesn't need his signature on every pump, protects the smaller states and limits his own arbitrary power.

He also has a different theory of progress. Emeric believes in building as fast as engineering, safety, resources and institutions can responsibly carry, because every year of delay leaves preventable suffering in place. The emperor believes too much change at once destroys trades, local industries, tax bases and the old networks people lean on before anything replaces them, and that a government that loses legitimacy can't build anything. Both men get evidence. A slower imperial project keeps its apprenticeships and its towns behind it, while a fast Randillia project fails an oversight test. But the emperor's slow timetable also leaves a district without help for longer. Neither man is always right, and both pay for the risks they choose. Your direction for rapid uplift stands. The argument is over what speed costs, not whether to build.

After year 24 he holds the Church's silenced machines, and he's the most likely first buyer of an Aegisframe. Whether the Church's machines can ever be converted is a research question his engineers help answer.

Everyone else

Duchies, marches, port authorities, the Isle's Islanders under Mother Sabbe, and the old peoples all get voices of their own. Cultural invitations go out broadly, but treaties are made only with whoever actually holds treaty authority.

Victories that have nothing to do with Kaldo

Solreth improving a Kaldo machine is still a win measured against Kaldo. These three happen with Emeric absent, and Kaldo hears about them afterwards, usually by letter. Each has its own cause. Kaldo doesn't arrange any of them.

  • Solreth saves a Velmoor town. A storm floods a Velmoor port's water works while Kaldo's crews are tied down by a Drifter landing. Solreth's own tradespeople and its first newly trained repair crews, from the program it won by refusing the turnkey hospital, restore the essential section in a fortnight, and the full rebuild continues after. Two Church-bound crowns helping each other without Kaldo starts loosening the Church's hold on both.
  • Duvrenne gets the emperor what he needs. After his break with the Church, the Core's sea trade still runs through Velmoor's ports, which the Wardens of the Shore control for the Church, and Velmoor won't deal with Kaldo on it. Alina negotiates limited imperial shipping rights through those ports, with the consent of whoever actually holds harbour authority. Velmoor gets enforceable protections for its freight and dock workers in return. The emperor owes Kesrath, not Kaldo.
  • The Empire's own breakthrough. The Core already has foundries, since the Church's frames are imperial iron. Imperial engineers develop a heavy rail steel and rolling process of their own, after failed batches and a costly pilot run. It's good enough that the Capital Line's continental extension runs on imperial rail, and Kaldo licenses the process. A young imperial engineer gets the credit, and the emperor makes sure the court hears it.

Part Six. What gets shared, and on what terms

Three tiers

Tier one is open knowledge. Water treatment, sanitation, measurement, food preservation, safer building, accounting, mechanical basics and public health are published and taught free, translated, with paid instructors. Open still means quality control.

Tier two is regulated. Rail, power networks, surgical infrastructure and heavy machinery need standards, inspection and trained operators. The partner chooses whether to license, co-develop, buy or build its own. Kaldo never gets a permanent monopoly on repairs.

Tier three is strategic. Stones, control stones, delving and the crown, the relay sight, the Farstrike, the dragon rifle and the mana bomb are not transferred. The restricted list is published as far as security allows, and disputed cases go to a joint review. There are two exceptions. Canon already sells rifles abroad to governments only, tagged. The second exception, by your direction, is the Aegisframe export.

The existing release ladder already covers goods such as soap, glass, lamps and bulbs, paper and books, cloth, bicycles, gramophones and medicines. Every system that goes out documents its stone needs, parts, maintenance and how it runs in a dead zone, with a mundane fallback.

Clauses in every major agreement

  • Ownership: the receiving side owns the finished works and its local operating records.
  • Training: paid local trainees and a replacement workforce, built in.
  • Repairability: standards, part drawings, tools and local manufacturing agreed from the start.
  • Financing: grants, loans and prices all out in the open, with no debt traps.
  • Labour: safety, pay, injury liability, apprenticeships and disputes.
  • Technology rights: what's public, licensed, jointly developed or restricted.
  • Inspection: independent audits and public reports on civilian works.
  • Consent: local lawful authority is respected, never bypassed for speed.
  • Exit: a partner can leave without losing its essential services.

Never force Kaldo's notes on anyone. Use open clearing, conversion agreements, audited guarantees and the partner's own currency. If Kaldo restrains arbitrary power at home, its partners will demand the same abroad, and foreign scrutiny can expose flaws in Kaldo's own procedures. That ties straight into the Hearth and the currency arc.

A compact that can survive a case against its founder

The Kesrath agreement, in years 22 and 23, starts as a civilian cooperation compact: shared standards, technical exchange, targeted works, emergency help, dispute settlement and narrow limits on surveillance. The imperial treaty rule isn't dodged. A draft agreed by subject crowns has no force where the emperor's authorization is legally needed. The emperor's successful objection produces a ratification procedure and an approved category of routine works, so his victory is constitutional, not ceremonial.

It has five small institutions:

  1. A secretariat at Kesrath, with a published budget and a clerk chosen by the member governments, not by Emeric. Hosting it doesn't make Duvrenne the judge.
  2. A works and standards commission, which publishes test and inspection results and has to allow local alternatives to Kaldo parts. Its reach is civilian only, unless a later treaty widens it.
  3. An independent dispute bench, drawn from an agreed pool of qualified judges, excluding anyone tied to the parties. If Duvrenne is in the dispute, Alina and Duvrenne don't judge it. The bench can order restitution, enforce a contract right or publish findings, but only within powers the states actually ratify.
  4. A safety review mandate, defined before any Kaldo project is paused: the measurable concern, the jurisdiction, the standard of evidence, how long the order lasts, the review date and remedies if the pause was wrong.
  5. A surveillance protocol, with specific grounds, prior authorization where practical, audited emergency exceptions, minimal record-keeping, and a way for other states to challenge misuse without seeing every secret of Kaldo's sight.

The first real test: a foreign contractor wins a ruling against a Kaldo agency or a favoured supplier. Kaldo pays, changes the contract and publishes the decision. It costs the Works money and delays an admired project. A treaty has to inconvenience Emeric sometimes to be believable.

It's not a federation in disguise. The compact allows no common taxes, no unlimited military inspection, no political interference and no automatic defence pact. A crown can come to the Gathering and never join the treaty. A crown can join the civilian standards without ever buying a weapon. The Aegisframe agreement is ratified separately, with its own enforcement rules.

Money and capacity

For every major project, show who pays to build it, who pays to run it, what imported materials cost, how long the training takes, and who carries the risk if it fails. Grants aren't humiliating when they're requested and run locally, and loans aren't automatically better. Publish spare parts, inspection intervals and staff qualifications, and give every civilian installation the right to be maintained by someone other than Kaldo.

Trade makes losers as well as winners. A caravan contractor may lose work to the railway, an old port may lose traffic, and a foreign crown may lose nurses to Randillia. They can seek retraining, compensation, local investment or lawful opposition, and they aren't villains for needing time. The migration problem can't be solved by building a school overnight, or by forbidding people to leave.

Part Seven. The flex and the mech sale

Which mechs exist, and when

Wren's Bulwark, 30 metres tall and piloted by a crowned delver, walked in October of year 18. It's top secret, kept as a sealed works, and was shown to Rowena under oath in October of year 19. Ward's Bastion, the first Axions, Arthur's Valoris and Emeric's Galefoil come in the middle home years, as their cores and the optimization arrive. Zale's Undertow comes late in year 23. All of those need a crown.

The Aegisframe is different. It's 30 metres tall, about 1,280 tonnes, and piloted through the cap by an ordinary person with no stone, no delving and no crown. The cap was begun in autumn of year 19, the bench arm comes in year 20, the ride room in year 21, the frame in years 22 and 23, and the Stage 3 cores late in year 23 from the Everywhere Night. Its first walk is in year 24, with Hattie Roan aboard and Torvild beside her in a dwarven-built frame.

So at a Gathering in year 22, Kaldo has three to five crowned machines, and the class it can sell doesn't walk until year 24.

The flex, and the roots group's objection

Some of the Standing Order ask whether showing the mechs is smart, and Emeric explains that it forces the others to move quicker, with the Drifters adding to the danger. The objection should be sharp, not polite.

Saban, who runs intelligence, points out that every one of these machines runs on a Drifter core, while the Church is telling the courts that the beasts carry the royal line's gold eyes and come from Randillia's island. Parading beast-hearted giants looks like proof. Ferra adds the cost: once they walk in public, the Church and the money houses know Kaldo holds working cores, and the sealed works is sealed no longer. Ward backs the flex, because deterrence only works if it's seen.

Emeric's answer is that there's no parade. Military observers from each crown get their own invitation, separate from the Gathering, to watch an authorized defensive hunt, but only if one is actually needed and safe to watch, from the Skyliner or a safe ridge. If none happens, they get verified records and an inspection of the equipment. Kaldo never lures, keeps, breeds or provokes a Drifter for publicity. He pairs it with an inspection offer: the beast's parts, the reading of the beast, and a seat for observers.

The hunt doesn't claim the mechs are the only answer. In canon, guns and knights are what kill Drifters, because the beasts cancel spells but can't read steel, powder or a knight's inward strength. What the mechs add against the biggest and worst beasts is survivability, reach and holding power, so fewer hunters die. The observers leave more afraid of the beasts than of Kaldo, having seen their own riflemen's work made safer.

The Aegisframe export

Canon already says the crown is never sold, and that the cap is the first piloting kit Kaldo can share without sharing delving. That makes the Aegisframe the only machine a foreign army could run.

"They can't reverse engineer it, and we're fine if they try" is mostly true. Canon says a captured cap teaches nothing without a signed core. Cores are rewritten and signed by delving, and nobody abroad can delve. There's one crack: a hull still teaches joints, armour plate and the sprung brakes, and the Church writes its own cores for its own frames by late year 23. Emeric can keep his line, and Tess privately knows which parts would teach them something.

The timing works on canon's clocks. The Gathering in year 22 stays civilian. The Aegisframe offer comes after its first walk in year 24, once the crowns have seen the Church's frames walk out of Ostrel. That gives them a real reason to buy.

A first walk isn't export readiness. Hattie's walk proves the machine works. It doesn't prove production reliability, safe use in battle, repairs, pilot training or upkeep in another kingdom. So year 24 brings the stadium demonstration and orders only. Before any combat-ready delivery there has to be certification of the production frame, trials with Kaldo's own Aegisframes on real hunts, foreign pilot training on the cap at the Standing Hall, a service depot in the buyer's country, and acceptance tests run by the buyer's own officers. The first deliveries come late in year 25 at the earliest, at Book 3's end or in Book 4. If anything isn't ready, deliveries move later rather than breaking the timeline.

Paying in monster parts

The buyer's own hunters bring a core, a Stage 3 for a full Aegisframe. Kaldo reads it, silences it, rewrites it, signs it and builds the frame around it. The buyer pays in cores, other beast parts and coin. Kaldo never ships its own cores, and the buyer's people do the hunting. Imported cores pass Randillia's Inspector of Sealing before Tess's office touches them.

A market for cores invites reckless hunts, hidden live beasts, forged paperwork and governments that treat their hunters as expendable. The Church's pen under Ostrel is the warning. So:

  1. Cores are credit, not tickets. A verified core is credited at the beast line's formula price against the machine's price. A core on its own never buys a machine or a place in the queue.
  2. Registered hunts, and honest emergencies. Planned hunts are registered in advance, crewed by certified sealing teams and open to inspection. A village that kills a Drifter during a surprise attack isn't refused credit for not registering. It gets provisional recognition on the evidence, followed by a custody review.
  3. Documented custody, using the canon tideshell beach procedure: a numbered seal, witnesses, duplicate records, a named custodian, named readers and a way to challenge it. A disputed specimen is held for examination, not quietly paid for or thrown away.
  4. No breeding, no deliberate live capture, no live sales. Keeping beasts in a pen for export is a serious breach. Temporary emergency containment, purely to evacuate people or kill a threat safely, is allowed under reporting rules. Kaldo's own legal research pen has to be addressed openly, so nobody pretends only foreigners face limits.
  5. The order book is capped by certified production and training capacity, not by how many cores a crown brings in. A buyer can pay in coin if it has no qualifying hunt, so getting a machine never depends on producing more beasts.
  6. Hunters and their families are protected, with insurance, death benefits and safety inspections funded under the buyer's law and audited jointly. A crown can't write off a hunting party it threw away as an ordinary purchasing cost.

The likeliest first buyers are the emperor, facing Church frames and beasts after his break, and Duvrenne, which shares the Isle with the beasts' cradle.

The Church's machines are a research question, not a service. Tess can read and silence them, but that doesn't mean she can rewrite their native code to take the cap, or that their crude imperial-iron frames can carry an Aegisframe's loads. If conversion works at all, it's a joint Imperial and Kaldo project in which the emperor's engineers solve something Tess couldn't. If it doesn't, the emperor still holds valuable machines nobody can drive yet.

The export agreement: legal ownership and mechanical dependence

Here's the contradiction at the heart of this arc. Emeric wants other crowns to stand on their own, yet he asks them to buy a machine whose core only Kaldo can re-sign. The treaty has to admit that dependence. Calling it a service lock instead of a kill switch doesn't make it go away. The emperor and Velmoor are right to challenge it, and Emeric isn't automatically wrong to demand accountability for weapons this destructive. That argument belongs on the page.

Emeric's honest answer, said once: "Yes. I hold half a key. And my own machines answer to the same panel."

How the halt actually works: the service lock

There's no remote kill switch. A remote command needs a receiver, and a buyer can rip a receiver out. Worse, a remote command sent by vibration is exactly the Quiet's "listen" back door, which Kaldo stripped from its own cores, and building a new one would hand every enemy a target. Exported machines are piloted from aboard, so Kaldo has no live channel into them.

The mechanism, ruled yes by Claude and waiting for your look, is a count written into Kaldo's signature on each exported core. It passes canon because delving already writes conditions into cores (the Quiet's recall and halt, and Kaldo's own firewalls), and stones already cost mana for every unit of work. A signature that keeps a tally and acts at a threshold is the same kind of instruction, not new magic.

  • What it counts: the core's own work driving the joints, measured as mana drawn through the gait core. It counts work, not calendar time.
  • How much: the allowance matches the machine's routine service interval for joints, plate and tendons, which it needs anyway. Roughly a season of ordinary duty, which is a guess to be set by testing.
  • The warning: a needle gauge in the head sphere shows the allowance left, and a bulb lights at the last tenth.
  • Running out safely: at zero, the core doesn't stop mid-stride. It finishes a kneel-and-brace sequence, then hands the joints to the sprung brakes. This is tested on the proving pad.
  • Tampering: the tally lives inside Kaldo's signature. A bad write or a tampering attempt trips the canon firewall, and the machine braces.
  • Dead zones: the core stops in a dead zone, and the tally stops with it.
  • Re-signing: a certified Kaldo delver does it at the buyer's own depot, with the joint office's clerk present. Every re-sign goes into a custody book that both sides keep.
  • Proof: Tess's office runs test cards in the same form as the Bulwark's, covering overload, dead zones, damaged linkages, bypass attempts and a refused re-sign.

So the halt is Kaldo declining to re-sign. The machine runs until its count ends, then kneels and locks wherever it is. Nothing is sent, so nothing can be jammed, and range doesn't matter.

There are three separate events. First, the legal order, a ruling that allows the re-sign to be refused. Second, the technical halt, which happens only when the allowance runs out. Third, physical recovery, carried out under the treaty with the host's officers present. Nobody should talk as if the first one automatically causes the second.

If you say no to this mechanism, exports with a service lock don't happen. You could choose unlocked exports or no exports at all, but the plan doesn't quietly invent a different mechanism.

The export protocol and its bench

The Aegisframe Export Protocol is separate from the civilian compact. Each buyer and Kaldo's Assembly have to ratify it, with the emperor's consent where the law needs it. It publishes who can buy, approved roles, inspection thresholds, appeal rights, export limits and remedies.

  • A technical office inspects production frames, safety incidents, records and core custody. It can't order political surveillance of a foreign state.
  • A neutral bench is drawn from an agreed pool of independent members. No government's appointee judges that government's own breach.
  • The normal procedure is evidence and notice, the buyer's right to answer, a written ruling with reasons, and an independent appeal. Refusing a future re-sign takes two keys: the panel's finding and Kaldo as the signing state. The panel has to name the machines, the length of time and the remedy. A government doesn't lose its whole fleet because one pilot is accused.
  • In an emergency, a verified atrocity happening right now justifies urgent physical protection of the victims. But the signature can't stop a machine on the spot. If force is considered, it still needs a lawful basis and a clear command. A commercial contract is not a blank cheque for Kaldo to invade. The buyer's own troops, the victims' own lawful authorities and properly authorized help from several states come first, and a court reviews the necessity and any mistakes afterwards.
  • Theft or capture: a stolen machine is reported, and refusing to re-sign it is allowed on a proper emergency finding. Theft doesn't make the count run out faster. The lawful owner gets a quick way to recover it or prove control, and Kaldo can't label an enemy's lawful military use as theft.

What the machines may be used for

Buyers may use them against Drifters, for disaster protection, and in war between states under the buyer's own command, including war with Kaldo. Permission in a contract doesn't decide whether a particular war is lawful. Internal political force is ruled out. No exported Aegisframe may break up protests, enforce political decrees on citizens, or fight an internal rebellion, armed or not. Disaster rescue inside a divided country is still allowed. Velmoor can refuse the contract over this clause, and it does.

Protection isn't a quota of acceptable dead civilians. Deliberate attacks on people not fighting, the wounded or the surrendering, attacks on civilian infrastructure as such, collective punishment and indiscriminate force are all forbidden. When attacking a lawful military target, civilian risk has to meet the tests of distinction, proportionality and reasonable precautions, and a single disproportionate attack can break the agreement. A government can't get around this by relabelling protesters as armed enemies.

For evidence, the ruling is a mundane event recorder, with no video. A clockwork-timed stylus scratches joint loads, weapon fire and walking time onto a strip of metal foil inside a sealed, armoured case, much as Earth's early flight data recorders did. It's pre-chip, keeps working in a dead zone because it's clockwork, and its seal shows tampering. It records what the machine did and when, not what it saw. Witnesses, the panel's observer and the after-action findings supply the rest. A chip recorder can replace it later, at the compute pace rule. The relay sight can only be used within the separately ratified limits on watching, never on a fishing expedition. Only case-relevant records are examined, under an order, with both sides present, and the buyer can challenge the conclusions.

Penalties can include suspending specific machines, restitution to victims, payment, published judgments, repair obligations, losing export privileges and, in the end, lawful recovery. Recovery with the host's officers present is normal. Recovery against a hostile host is neither easy nor guaranteed.

Symmetry, and its honest limit

Kaldo's own Aegisframes, and its crowned machines when they operate outside Randillia, are held to the same civilian rules and the same review. But the panel can't necessarily make a crowned machine lock, and Kaldo mustn't pretend the mechanisms are equal. The real symmetry is in jurisdiction, evidence duties, restitution, penalties and public accountability. If the other crowns demand powers Kaldo won't grant, that disagreement stays real instead of being covered over with a slogan.

Don't call it independence after ten years while only Kaldo can delve. After ten clean years, a buyer can earn the transfer of the legal veto over re-signing to a neutral panel, with Kaldo bound to keep signing as a contracted provider under enforceable remedies. That still leaves a technical dependence. A permanent unlocked signature, or a way for someone outside Kaldo to sign, would need a separate ruling from you and technical proof. Local depots, owned spare parts and independent inspection are real progress, but they don't give anyone the ability to delve.

War with Kaldo, faced openly

A buyer owns what it bought. Using its machines against Kaldo in a war between states isn't theft and doesn't break the agreement, and the civilian clause binds both sides. During such a war, Kaldo's duty to supply staff, parts and re-signing to an enemy can be suspended by a clause both sides agree to. Because the signatures run out with use, that's a real future loss of capability, not a neutral footnote. A competent emperor will call it what it is: a strategic dependence that makes a Kaldo-built mech a risky centrepiece for any nation's defence. On Earth, Iran kept its American-built F-14s flying for decades after 1979 without American parts, so buyers know it's survivable, but expensive.

Buyers keep real alternatives. They can keep their own forces and artillery, avoid building their whole defence around the imported class, pay for parts and certified local maintenance, decline to buy, or demand a different bargain. Nobody has to call the deal equal.

Emeric gives his warning privately, ruler to ruler, once, and never as a boast written into the treaty, because a published treaty would humiliate every partner in front of their own people. A draft:

You are free to make war against us. I won't insult you by pretending otherwise. You may even use the machines we sell you. But understand this: what you are purchasing is not everything we possess. If you choose that road, you will not win.

He can add, in his own words at drafting, that Kaldo could have taken the tyrant's road and didn't. If what Kaldo actually has at that date can't back up his confidence, change the wording rather than giving him omniscience. Ferra or Saban argues for saying it privately, and Emeric agrees.

The gate in the Assembly

The Assembly has to pass a named exception to the release ladder, the second military export exception after the existing tagged rifle sales to governments. The beast line has to be renewed past year 25 in the same session, because the export's core custody depends on the Inspector of Sealing still existing. So the export vote and the beast-line renewal get argued together. Book 3's question is institutions acting without Emeric, so the Assembly can amend the terms against him. The emperor, Duvrenne, Velmoor and Solreth can all respond differently and keep their dignity.

The best negotiation scene

The emperor doesn't ask whether the machine is impressive. He asks whether his troops will still have it on the day Kaldo and the Core disagree over a border. Emeric says the frame is his once bought, and war itself isn't forbidden. The emperor points to the clause suspending re-signing in wartime and says the machine's true owner may be the man who signs its core. Emeric admits the dependence and doesn't insult him by pretending neutral words change it. The emperor then asks for years of stocked spares, local repair rights, an independent tribunal, a price that reflects the vulnerability, and time to test the cap with imperial pilots. Emeric doesn't win the room by boasting. The emperor may sign only a trial arrangement and hold back the full purchase. Another ruler may decline altogether, and the scene still succeeds if the relationship survives.

The stadium proving ground

The stadium demonstration in year 24 is its own event, after the first walk, never part of the Gathering. No live Drifter comes to the capital. The danger to the gathered delegations is too great, and a staged beast fight would make the diplomacy look like coercion.

The floor has to carry the machine. The Aegisframe weighs about 12.6 meganewtons. With both feet down, each about 25 square metres, that's about 251 kilopascals. When one foot carries the full weight, it's about 502 kilopascals, before any extra force from braking, stumbling or turning. An ordinary stage or untested ground can't take that. The site needs a ground survey, load calculations, piles or a raft as needed, joints that keep the stands isolated, safe fall zones and proof testing. Gravity stones can lighten the load, but basic safety can't depend on them.

That's a Tess and Bram scene. Tess wants a confident walking demonstration. Bram refuses to let the crowd sit beside foundations nobody has tested under one-foot loading. They settle on a short certified strip with a stopping mark, speed limits and exclusion distances, and Hattie Roan has to hit the mark. The real success is that an ordinary pilot without a crown can control the machine safely, not that every trick gets performed in one afternoon.

The exhibits are inspected plates, vanes, a safely sealed core, workshop cutaways, costs and maintenance needs. There's no whole carcass on casual display, because in canon a core bleeds out and crumbles with its carcass unless it's sealed.

So there are three separate events: the civilian and cultural Gathering in year 22; the military observers' hunt in years 22 to 23, only if a safe one happens, otherwise documentary evidence; and the proving-ground demonstration in year 24, which allows orders subject to certification.

Part Eight. The Church

The Church's strongest arguments are partly true. Fast, unfamiliar magic, high-output research, surveillance and the sheer speed of social change are real risks.

A properly constituted joint technical panel finds a real, specifically evidenced Kaldo hazard, orders a limited pause under a mandate the states actually ratified, and Emeric obeys even though it sets back a project he cares about. The Well's condensing output is only a candidate for that hazard, and its failure mode has to be shown against canon before a chapter names it. That proves the crowns can restrain Kaldo better than any speech could.

Keep ordinary clergy, real safety concerns and guilty leaders distinct. Some local clerics support sanitation and schools while distrusting Kaldo's politics. The Church's political monopoly breaks because a working alternative exists, and that fits the ruled endgame: the pen, the machines and the emperor's break, from late year 23 into early year 24. Nothing here changes the endgame's mechanisms or dates.

Part Ten. The causal chain and the set pieces

These aren't twenty extra chapters. Some are full chapters, some are scenes inside existing chapters, and some arrive as letters, minutes or testimony. The ones that deserve full weight are: the four Randillia scenes before the skip, the Gathering's cultural refusal, the Conference's failed draft, the emperor's legal win, one independent rescue across a border, a treaty decision that costs Kaldo, and the year 24 mech demonstration.

The chain runs in nine links.

  1. Promise and constraint: Emeric proposes national service goals. Ferra forces a public ledger, real operating budgets and a district's right to object. A regional doctor defeats his power-station timetable with sewage figures.
  2. Competence and error: the Mage Corps pulls off a huge physical success, while a separate rushed job injures workers because oversight didn't keep up. The inquiry gives the triumph teeth instead of cancelling it.
  3. The visible gap: a clinic runs without Kaldo staff, another place still lacks service, and an imperial observer records skilled workers leaving. Kaldo's success has a cost abroad.
  4. Invitation and refusal: the Gathering works as a place of ideas, but a delegation refuses Kaldo's control of its own story. At the separate Conference, another refuses unequal inspection terms. An expert corrects a Kaldo device. Neither refusal is punished.
  5. Real opposition: the emperor wins his jurisdiction objection, Alina reshapes the Kesrath talks, Velmoor forces surveillance limits and Solreth forces repair rights. The compact ends up smaller, but real.
  6. Independent proof: Solreth helps Velmoor, Alina bargains for an imperial route, and an imperial engineer produces tested rail steel. Kaldo arranged none of it.
  7. The stress test: the Church endgame and the beast crisis test what the states built for civilian reasons. The independent panel rules against Kaldo, and Emeric complies.
  8. A separate hard choice: the export debate makes Emeric face the dependence his service lock creates. A proud crown declines, a willing crown places conditional orders, and Hattie proves the prototype without anyone mistaking a first walk for a certified army.
  9. The landing image: Emeric attends a foreign-run hospital or works opening where nobody needs his orders. The local people are already arguing about their next improvement. He's a guest, and the story belongs to them.

The scene to aim for: a regional hospital opens with no Kaldo engineer present. Part Eleven. The characters across the arc

  • Emeric goes from inventor-king to architect of capacity he doesn't control. He fails by helping too efficiently, with turnkey solutions and choosing other people's priorities. His defining choice is an enforceable constraint and local ownership over the faster path he'd prefer.
  • Ferra knows the institutions are the real invention. She asks for budgets, repair terms, treaty authority and consent, and holds the purse on every promise.
  • Imani stops health systems from becoming monuments, insisting on local clinicians, referral, consent and sanitation. The Kindling and the menders keep their canon limits.
  • Saban reads the buyers' names, raises the objection to the flex, and draws the line between real danger and surveillance on a pretext.
  • Ward backs the flex and wants more intelligence access, which pushes against the treaty's limits on the sight.
  • Waylon and Alina show that family ties and state interest don't always line up. Alina gets a win of her own.
  • The emperor is a rival in the art of governing, not in science, with his own theory of progress that's sometimes right.
  • At least two recurring outsiders, a sceptical official in the Velmoor border lord and a brilliant local practitioner in a Solreth engineer, each make a decision that changes policy or Kaldo's own technology.

Part Twelve. What not to do

  • Don't force everyone to agree.
  • Don't make the museum a sales funnel.
  • Don't give every invention away free. Free knowledge isn't the same as the running cost of rail, clinics and power.
  • Don't create permanent dependencies.
  • Don't hand anyone strategic weapons beyond the Aegisframe terms.
  • Don't treat dissent as ingratitude.
  • Don't make the emperor a ceremonial guest.
  • Don't make the Drifters the only reason anyone acts sensibly.
  • Don't skip all the building, and don't write another 391 chapters of boilers.
  • Don't confuse a dazzling capital with a modern nation.
  • Don't make the Church wrong about every hazard.
  • Don't pull the Quiet's arrival forward.
  • Don't say a signed promise can stop a mech instantly, when the lock may take a season to run out.
  • Don't claim technical independence after ten years while only Kaldo can delve.
  • Don't treat a pilot's view through Drifter eyes as a recording.
  • Don't have the Gathering guarantee a hunt, build a live Drifter attraction, or treat mechs as the only way to kill beasts.
  • Don't hide the buyer's wartime dependence behind the phrase "no kill switch."
