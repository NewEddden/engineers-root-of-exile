Chapter 11 - Head Count

Emeric was back in the barony before he'd shaken the road off, and he went straight at the farm. The harvests had come in thin for years, one after another, and for the first time since Kaldo he felt something he'd have called eagerness if he'd stopped to name it. But the thing about a harvest is that it does not wait for a man to finish reorganizing the world around it. The standing crop, what little the old fields had managed on their own while he'd been away at the capital, was ready to come in now, this week, before he laid a single line of the bigger plan he was carrying folded in his head. So the first work he did in Kaldo as its lord was not the clever work. It was getting a thin, ordinary crop off the ground before the weather turned it to nothing, and it was there, doing the oldest work there was, that he learned the first of the season's lessons the expensive way.

They threshed on a beaten dirt floor behind the mill, the way the barony had threshed since before the barony had a name, men and women swinging flails in a rhythm older than the crown, beating the grain loose from the straw one slow armful at a time. It worked. It had always worked. It was also brutally slow, it wanted more hands than a barony down to a couple thousand souls or a barony this shrunk could spare in the one week it all had to happen, and Emeric watched it for the length of a morning and did what he always did, which was see a machine standing where a crowd of tired people stood.

The idea was not complicated, and he did not need magic for the bones of it. A drum set with wooden beater bars, turned by a crank and a belt off the mill's own wheel, the sheaves fed in at one end so the beaters knocked the grain loose against a screen without a human arm having to do the knocking. Bram made him the drum in a day and a half of grumbling, iron pins in an oak body, and Emeric set a plain water-stone at the axle with a single channel cut to hold the drum at a steady speed against the wheel's dips, so the beat came even whether the current surged or slacked. He fed the first sheaves in himself, because he wanted to see it, and it worked, and he let himself be pleased for most of an hour before the pile coming off the screen told him he had built the wrong thing well.

Half the grain was cracked. Not threshed, cracked, the kernels split and crushed where the beaters had come down harder than the grain could take, and a cracked kernel was a ruined one, it would not store without spoiling and it would not sow, so a cracked kernel was neither seed nor bread but only a faster kind of loss. And running through the cracked half, worse in its own way, was a scatter of heads that had gone through whole and unbeaten, the grain still locked in the straw, because those sheaves had come in a little greener, a little damper, tougher in the stalk, and the same beat that shattered the dry ones had barely troubled the wet ones at all.

He stood there with a handful of the mess and understood he had made an error he made in more than one shape. He had set the drum to a speed. One speed, fixed, chosen because it seemed a reasonable middle and because a fixed number was a thing his mind reached for the way a hand reaches for a rail. But the grain was not one thing. It came in dry and it came in damp, brittle and tough, sheaf by sheaf, and a beat that was right for one was wrong for the next, and he had built a machine that treated every armful as if it were the same armful, and it had faithfully, evenly, mechanically ruined the ones that weren't.

It was Farris who put the last of it into words, and he did not do it to teach, only to say what was plainly true while he pulled a cracked ear apart with a thumbnail. He had threshed on that floor since before the war, more harvests than he'd bothered counting. "Never hit it all the same," he said. "You listen to it. Dry sheaf sounds hollow, you go light. Green one fights you, you lean in. My arm knew it before I did." He said it the way a man says water is wet. It was the whole problem, and the whole answer, spoken by someone who had never once thought of it as either, because to him it had only ever been the work.

Her arm knew it. That was the thing his machine did not have and the flailing crowd did, a hand that felt the grain it was hitting and answered what it felt. The manual way had never trusted a fixed number, because a person could not help but read the real one, sheaf by sheaf, every stroke. He had taken the one advantage the old way had and engineered it out, and called the result progress because it turned faster.

So he rebuilt it, and the rebuild was the part he kept. He left the drum and the beaters and the mill-wheel drive exactly as they were, because the bones were sound. What he changed was the stone. He cut a reader into it, a second channel that did nothing to the grain and only listened to it, sensing the load coming through the drum by the drag it put on the axle, the way a damp tough sheaf hauled at the beaters and a dry brittle one gave almost no resistance at all. And he tied the reader to the driver, so the stone no longer held one speed but rode the load, easing the beat where the grain came dry and pressing it where the grain came green, moment to moment, faster than Farris's arm and never tiring the way Farris's arm did by the end of a long day. It did with a stone what he did with a lifetime of instinct, which was refuse to trust a number when it could read the thing itself.

He kept the manual baseline under it, because he was learning to keep the manual baseline under everything. Pull the stone out and the drum still turned off the wheel at a plain fixed speed, and a person had to stand there and feed it careful and pull the wet sheaves aside by hand, slower and with more cracked grain than the reader allowed, but the crop still came in. The stone made it good. It did not make it possible. That distinction was becoming the whole of how he built.

The corrected drum ran through the rest of the threshing in a fraction of the hands the floor had wanted, clean grain off one end and straw off the other, and Farris walked past it once, watched it ease off a hollow-sounding dry sheaf, and gave it a small grudging nod that Emeric valued more than most things men had said to his face. He filed the lesson under the ones he meant to keep. Never trust a fixed number when you can read the real one. He would build a furnace on it before the year was out, and he did not yet know that, and it would not have surprised him to be told.

The phrase was not entirely his own. His master had owned it first, years and a whole life ago, in the small back room where a boy had learned to read stones before he learned to hide that he could read them at all. Master once taught me that a number on a page is only a guess a man made about a thing he wasn't touching at the time, Emeric thought, and the real one is whatever the thing is telling you now, if you've the wit to feel for it. A stone did not care what the tables rated it to hold. It held what it held, that day, in that hand, and a smith who trusted the figure over the crystal in front of him was a smith who would one day pour more into it than it could pay back out. And I'm reminded by the journal, he added, turning the old maxim over in the master's own cramped hand as clearly as if the page were open on the desk in front of him, that every smith who ever blew the skin off his palms did it trusting the page over the stone. The threshing floor had only taught him the same thing in grain. The real number was not the one you set once and walked away from, sure it would keep. It was the one the thing itself gave back, moment to moment, sheaf by sheaf and stone by stone, to a builder who had thought to make something willing to listen for it. A fixed number was a guess wearing a certainty's clothes. The real number never had to guess, because it was reading the thing as it was.

Threshing gave him clean grain and a second problem in the same motion, because a good part of what came off the drum had come in damp, and damp grain put into store was grain that heated and molded and rotted the sound grain packed around it. The barony's answer had always been the sun and the weather and luck, spread it on cloths and pray for a dry week, and in a bad year the luck ran out and a share of the harvest turned in the bin before winter was half through. Emeric was not willing to store a season's food against the weather's mood, so he built a way to dry it that did not ask the sky's permission.

It was a low shed with a slatted floor, the grain spread thin across the slats and a fire stone bedded in a brick channel beneath, throwing a steady gentle heat up through the grain to carry the damp off. He set the fire stone and lit the first batch and went to see to the drum, and he did not think about the drying shed again until Farris came at a run to tell him it was smoking wrong.

He'd made the drying stone the way he'd first made the threshing one. He'd set it to a heat and left it there. It held that heat faithfully, exactly as told, and kept holding it long after the grain was dry, because he had told it how hot to burn and had not told it when to stop. The batch nearest the channel had gone past dry to scorched, brittle and cracked and useless as the threshing rejects, kernels split from the heat, and the shed had begun to smell of the sharp edge of grain a breath away from catching. A shed full of bone-dry grain over an unattended fire that did not know its work was finished was not a dryer. It was a granary fire that hadn't happened yet.

The threshing lesson had been about reading the load. This one was about knowing when to quit, and it was a different lesson wearing a similar coat. A fixed heat with nothing to stop it was a thing that could only run one direction, toward too much, until a person happened to be standing there to end it. He had built a machine that could start and could not finish.

The fix was a target and a cutoff. He cut the drying stone a reader, as he'd done for the drum, but this one read the grain and not the load, the moisture coming off it as it dried, and he set the stone a mark to aim for, the dryness that stored well and no dryer than that. When the grain reached the mark the reader cut the heat and held the stone cold, and the drying stopped itself, and a batch could sit finished on the slats overnight with no one watching and no fire waiting to find the dry patch. Below the stone, the mundane bones again: the channel drew a plain draft whether the stone lived or died, so a man could dry a batch on a low wood fire and his own judgment if it came to that, slower, with more spoiled to the weather, but not lost.

He stood in the doorway of the fixed shed with the warm smell of drying grain coming off the slats and thought that he had now been taught the same thing twice in a week by two different machines, that a thing which does not read and does not stop is a thing that will faithfully carry an error all the way to ruin, and that the whole trick of building was to give the machine the one human thing the human hands had always brought to it without noticing.

Clean grain, dried to a mark. The last of the chain was keeping it, a full year, against damp and vermin and time, and here Emeric had a rune he had not used before and had been turning over since he'd first read it in the pouch of his mother's stones, a preservation working, a stasis that held whatever sat within its field in the state it found them, unchanging, unspoiling, time simply declining to touch the grain inside. His first thought, because it was always his first thought, was to do it big. One great sealed vault under one strong stasis field, the whole harvest inside it, nothing rotting, nothing aging, the barony's food held perfect for as long as the stone held charge. It was elegant. He drew it that evening and liked it, and then he made himself do the thing he was slowly training himself to do, which was ask how it broke.

It broke two ways, and both of them were ways he had sworn off in other parts of the barony. The first was the stone itself. One vault, one stasis field, one stone holding it, and if that stone failed, all at once and with no warning, the whole year's food began to spoil in the same hour, a single point of failure with everything the barony would eat behind it. He had cut his irrigation into parallel runs so a collapsed bank starved one line and not the farm. He had built his furnace to fall back to charcoal when the crystal died. And here he had nearly hung the entire winter on one stone in one wall because the drawing was clean.

The second way was subtler and he only saw it because the drum and the shed had put him in the habit of looking. Stasis held things in the state it found them. It did not clean them. Seal a bin with a damp pocket in the heart of it, or a few grain weevils riding in on the straw, and the stasis would preserve the damp pocket and the weevils as faithfully as the good grain, hold them perfect and waiting, and when the field came down months later the rot would be exactly where it started and the weevils exactly as hungry, released whole into the food at the moment it was most needed. Stasis did not defeat a problem sealed inside it. It saved the problem for later, in perfect condition. A machine that preserved whatever state you gave it demanded, more than any machine he'd built, that you give it a clean one.

So he built the store the way the rest of Kaldo was teaching him to build. The mundane baseline did the real work and did most of it: grain genuinely dried to the mark, held in bins sealed against damp and lined and limed against vermin, kept the better part of a year on its own strength, the way good granaries had kept grain for a thousand years before anyone in Kaldo had a stasis stone to spare. That was the floor, and the floor alone would carry the barony through a winter. The stasis was the supplement laid over it, stretching the keeping of the bins meant to hold longest, buying margin, not standing as the thing the food depended on.

And he did not build one vault. He built many small silos, bins a man could walk around, each one sealed and stored on its own, so a failure took a bin and not the crop, the same logic as the parallel ditches carried up out of the water and into the grain. Each got its own modest stone where a stone was spared at all, and the ones without simply relied on the honest dry baseline. He made it a standing rule, written down and given to Farris to enforce whether or not the lord was standing over it, that no bin was sealed until it had been walked, checked dry to the core, cleaned of any damp or any vermin a hand could find. You never put a problem into stasis. You inspected first, because the field would keep faith with whatever you handed it, and it could not tell good from bad, only now from later.

Three machines in a week, and the same lesson wearing three coats, and Emeric came out the far side of the thin harvest with clean grain dried to a mark and stored in a way that did not lean on any single stone to keep the barony fed. It was a small crop. It fed the town and left a little over, no more. But he had brought it in with a handful of the hands the old way wanted, and along the way he had learned, on grain that cost little to spoil, the way to build the things that would soon carry a harvest the whole barony's survival was going to hang on. He preferred to learn expensive lessons on cheap grain. He was not always going to get the choice.

With the old crop in, he turned to the larger thing, which had never been the standing harvest at all but the redesign of the whole farm around the season to come. And planning didn't start at a desk with him guessing at what a crew needed. It started with the right people in a room. So he went to Bram's first and asked him to gather everyone who'd have a hand in the work, as soon as it could be done.

Then the castle. He'd half forgotten, in the noise of everything since the capital, that the office was his now. Not on loan. Not held for somebody else's benefit. His. The land, the people, every problem inside these borders landed on his desk whether he'd asked for them or not. The weight of it settled somewhere behind his sternum and stayed there, and his sense of duty was enough to carry it without slowing his feet.

He sat and made a list. Not a wish list. A working one, the kind he used to build before a review, back when a review had meant something different than it did now. He sorted it into categories, because that was the only way his head had ever held anything worth holding.

Labor. Head count against whatever the ledger claimed the barony had. Farmers, miners, smiths, split by trade and checked against who could actually stand in a field tomorrow, not who happened to be written down.

Land. Fields cut for rotation, nine divisions at the least, the fallow cycle built in from the start instead of bolted on after the soil quit. Layout fixed now, before a shovel touched dirt. Moving a ditch after you've dug it is a different job than digging it right the first time.

Water. Irrigation lines, clay-lined and hand-dug, no metal spent on something that didn't need it yet. Grading mattered more than material this early, but grading only ever carried water downhill, and some of the ground he meant to work sat above the river's easy reach. That wanted a lift. A wheel in the current, a pump, a tank set high enough to hold a head of water against the dry weeks, all of it built out of wood and the river's own weight so that none of it leaned on a crystal to run. The stones would come after, to make it quicker and cleverer. They would not be the thing it stood on.

Materials. Ore, honest numbers only, enough for what the farm needed this season and nothing dressed up to sound like more. Clay, wood, stone for everything that didn't strictly demand iron.

Storage and processing. This one, at least, he could write with something under it now instead of a promise. The thresher ran, and read its own load, and the drying shed knew when to stop, and the grain went into small sealed bins no single failure could empty at once. It was built for a thin crop and a small town, and the season coming would ask it to handle many times what it had, which meant more drums, more sheds, more bins, the same design multiplied rather than a new one invented. He preferred a problem that only got bigger. The ones that got stranger were the ones that cost him.

Reserved. Space in the layout he wasn't ready to account for. Grading a little deeper than it needed to be. Rows set a little wider than rotation called for. Nobody would ask why today. Somebody would ask eventually, and he meant to have a true answer ready instead of an excuse.

Six items. He looked at the page longer than six items warranted, the way he always had, trusting a plan a little more once he'd seen it laid down in ink. Then he added a seventh, in a different hand, because it belonged to a different part of the barony and he wanted it to look different on the page.

Garrison. Wexler's men, now his. A count against the payroll, same as the townsfolk, and the same question underneath it: who was real and who had only ever been written down. He didn't know the first thing about most of them beyond the fact that a dead traitor had paid their wages, and that was exactly the problem. He wrote a note to run the whole roster through Ferra once the farm figures firmed, because whatever Wexler had done to the barony's books he had almost certainly done to its muster rolls too, and payroll was the one place a lie like that couldn't fully hide.

He added a note to run the farm wages through Ferra once the figures firmed as well. She'd earned that with a ledger already, and he wasn't about to keep payroll in his own head when somebody better at it sat one building over. The meeting filled a room built for fewer people than it held. Bram's forge crew took one wall, arms crossed, soot still under their nails from a job they'd left half-done to be there. Jabari stood off from his own men the way of someone used to being blamed first and asked last, watching Emeric with the flat patience of a man who'd sat through more noble promises than he cared to count. The farmers hung loosest of the three, more of them than Emeric had expected and, somehow, fewer at the same time. He filed the wrongness of that without yet knowing why it snagged.

Bram kept the introductions short. Nobody in Kaldo had patience for a speech, and Bram knew it better than anyone in the room. "This is the prince." He said it like it closed a subject rather than opening a hundred. "He's got work. Paying work. Figured you'd rather hear it from him than from me."

Emeric had the numbers set before he opened his mouth. Nine fields for rotation, a rough count of hands per section, the ratio of farmers to labor-hours he needed to clear before the season turned. He gave it plain. No court language, nothing dressed up. Fair wage, paid on time, work that would look strange before it looked useful. He watched the room while he talked, the way he did now, cataloguing faces because he'd long since stopped trusting himself to read them. The doubt sat plain on most of them. Arms crossed, weight back on the heels. A prince who talked crop rotation like a man who'd turned soil himself didn't reconcile easily for people who'd spent a decade watching the old baron's promises dry up the moment the ink did. It helped, more than he'd expected, that half of them had spent the week watching a strange drum thresh their own thin crop in a fraction of the hands it should have wanted, and had seen the lord pull it apart and rebuild it in the open when it came up wrong rather than pretend it hadn't. Bram caught the doubt and stepped in without being asked. "I've worked with him," he said. "Pays what he says he'll pay. Won't waste your time telling you something's fine when it isn't." Short. No polish on it. The kind of word that lands harder for being brief.

It was enough to tip the room. Not to eagerness, not yet, but to a wary willingness to be shown, which was the only opening Emeric had ever actually needed. He worked through the farmers' questions about field division and Jabari's flatter ones about ore. How much, for what, when he'd see it come back as tools instead of talk. Emeric answered straight: enough for what the farm needed now, not enough to pretend Kaldo was suddenly rich, and no intention of pretending. Jabari's face didn't soften, exactly, but something in his shoulders let go a fraction. There's a particular relief in being handed a true small number instead of a false large one, and Jabari had clearly been handed the second kind before.

It was somewhere in the middle of walking the farmers through the rotation that the head count finally nagged hard enough to make him stop and do it. He ran the room against the figure he'd pulled from the barony's official ledger that morning, the same figure Bram would have used to guess how many chairs to drag in. It didn't match. Not by a little. He said nothing about it there. He set it beside the other thing that hadn't added up since he'd arrived and made a note to put it to Ferra: old rot in the records, or something newer and worth worrying over.

By the time the room emptied, he had commitments from all three groups, wages agreed, a rough date to break ground, and a promise to himself to walk the fields on his own two feet before he settled where anything got dug, built, or left conspicuously, deliberately empty for later. He went back to the office to sort the next day. He'd barely sat before the other thought caught him up again, the one still sitting unresolved from earlier. The Hollow Crystal. Not a name he'd chosen so much as one everyone else used and he'd never bothered to replace.

He'd nearly forgotten the rest of what his father had pressed on him at the banquet, a pouch of uncut stones he'd dropped into a trunk and not looked at since. He fetched it and tipped it out across the desk. Mostly flat stones, plain, no affinity, the sort a tutor used to teach a child the basics before letting them near anything that did real work. A few water-tuned pieces among them. One or two that read as fire the second he lifted them, a warmth under the skin with nothing to do with the room, the same pieces he'd bedded under the drying shed that week. He couldn't have said why he expected the Hollow Crystal to matter here. A feeling, only, the kind he'd learned not to wave off even when he couldn't defend it yet. He set one of each stone beside it and reached for the delving space.

It came easier than it had any right to, the shallow way, the waking way he'd been drilling since the back room at Bram's. Not instant, but the gap between deciding to go under and being there had shrunk to almost nothing. The space still wore the shape of his office, same desk, same walls. The only tell that he wasn't fully asleep was the text hanging at the edge of his sight like a readout, and the stones on the desk registering the way nearby devices used to surface on a phone screen back home. Present. Listed. Waiting to be asked. He had the rest of the day. He decided to test the thing rather than assume it.

The water stone answered first and fastest. He didn't carve anything onto it, didn't touch it at all. The Hollow Crystal simply addressed it, the way a name gets an answer without a hand needing to move the thing it names. He set a release timing from the crystal's side, and the water stone obeyed at once, no separate instruction cut into its own surface. That settled what he'd half-guessed in the office that afternoon. He wouldn't need ten tuned crystals strung along a ditch. He'd need one Hollow Crystal doing the addressing and a row of dumb, uncarved water stones doing exactly what they were told.

And the dumb ones, he realized, were the part that made the whole thing affordable. He didn't need tuned pieces or good ones. He needed plain water-stones by the basketful, and there was a place a man could get plain water-stones by the basketful. The river below the town ran its bed over gravel that was half common stone and half the low, dull, barely-charged kind the trade didn't bother to cut, too weak to be worth a carver's time and exactly strong enough to hold a single instruction told to it from somewhere smarter. A boy with a sieve could pull a season's worth out of the shallows in an afternoon. The clever stone was his mother's, and there was only the one he's tested so far. The obedient ones cost nothing and lay in the riverbed by the thousand. He filed that harder than he'd filed almost anything all week.

The flat stones answered too, though there was less to ask of them. No affinity meant nothing to direct past a simple release of charge. Useful anyway, and proof the same principle held for the cheapest substrate he owned, not only the specialty pieces. The fire stone answered slower, and the line to it felt thinner, less sure. He couldn't yet say whether that was the stone, the distance between two signatures that didn't match, or only him asking a fire question in a vocabulary that was mostly water. He filed it. Not urgent tonight. He left the Gravity Crystals where they were, out in the carriage. Four stones, and no more where those had come from. Whatever the Hollow Crystal could or couldn't do to them wasn't a thing to learn by accident.

By the time he surfaced, the morning's plan had changed shape, and not in any way that stung to admit. One stone addressing a network, instead of ten each carrying its own copy of the same order. The ditches wouldn't need a man walking the line to check each length. They'd need the Hollow Crystal at the head of it, and everything downstream simply doing as it was told.

He set that beside the seventh item on his list, the one in the different hand, and thought that a barony was really only ever two problems wearing different clothes. A great many pieces that had to be told what to do, and the hard, narrow question of which of them could be trusted to do it. The water stones were easy. They had no loyalty to be wrong about. The men in his lower ward were the other kind, and Ferra was going to have to read them the same way she read a scraped ledger page, by what the numbers did when nobody was arranging them to lie.
