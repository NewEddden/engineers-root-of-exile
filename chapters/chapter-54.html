Chapter 54 - The Clean Melt

The glasshouse was the first building in the district Emeric could not think his way through, and he found out the size of that fact standing in front of a furnace that had, on the page, worked perfectly. The page had given him the temperature and the batch and the crucible and the draw, and every number on it was right, and he had walked in that morning expecting to watch numbers become glass, and what he watched instead was a season of his knowledge run head-first into a wall it did not know was there, which was that glass is not made by a furnace. It is made by a man standing at a furnace, and the difference between those two sentences was going to cost him months he had not budgeted, because he had budgeted for a machine and what he had built was a trade.

He had lit the furnace himself, or rather had stood beside the men who lit it and understood, watching, that his hand was the least useful in the room. The melt was the easy part and even the easy part was not easy. Sand and ash and the fluxing salts went in cold and came out, after the long climb to a heat that made the iron foundry's fires look temperate, as a thick glowing honey that was not yet anything, that would become window or bottle or lens or nothing at all depending entirely on what happened to it in the next handful of minutes by hands he did not have. The furnace was mundane on purpose. He had been strict about that, stricter than usual, because glass was going to be the wealth that carried the barony and he would not hang the barony's wealth on a crystal that only his own people could feed. The heat came from fuel and draft and a well-built stack, the way it came in every glasshouse that had ever stood in the empire, and if every crystal in Kaldo went dark tomorrow the fire would not so much as flicker. That had been the whole of his cleverness here, and he had thought it would be enough, and it turned out to be merely correct, which he was learning was a different and lesser thing.

Because the glass came out of the pot and then it had to be worked, and the working was where his knowledge stopped and a stranger's craft began. He knew, in the way he knew everything, exactly what a gather was and exactly what a gaffer did with it, the turning and the blowing and the reheating and the quick sure shaping of a thing while it was still soft enough to move and before it was cool enough to crack. He could have written the sequence out for a student. He could not do a single step of it, and neither, at first, could any man in Kaldo, because the sequence written on a page is a description of a skill and not the skill itself, and the skill lived only in the arms of people who had spent ten years growing it, and there were no such arms on this coast. This was the thing his whole method had never once had to face. Every problem before this had yielded to knowing the answer. Here he knew the answer completely and the knowing bought him nothing, because the answer was not a fact. It was a calibration in a human body, built by failure, over years, and there was no page in the world that could pour it into a man who had not stood at the furnace and ruined a thousand pieces learning it.

So he did the thing that galled him, which was to be slow. He had brought two glassworkers out of the capital at real expense, a father and a son who had left a guild that was strangling them and come to a barony that promised a furnace of their own, and Emeric had them not as employees so much as seeds. He did not manage them. He watched them fail and did not correct it, because he had nothing to correct them with, and he made himself sit with how strange that was, the man who fixed everything reduced to a patron who could only pay and wait. The first weeks produced ruin. Bubbles seized in the glass, cords ran through it, pieces cooled wrong and shattered on the bench with a sound like a small betrayal, and the father cursed in a dialect Emeric did not fully have and threw more work away than he kept. Emeric priced the breakage, and it was brutal, and he paid it without a word, and understood that he was not buying bottles. He was buying the years he could not skip, at the only exchange rate the world offered, which was waste.

What he could do, he did, and it mattered, and it was not the thing. He could give them a furnace that held its heat truer than any guild furnace they had ever cursed at, because heat he understood and could build for. He could give them sand cleaner than they had ever been handed, washed and sorted by a process he had worked out on the same principle he worked out everything, and a cleaner batch meant fewer flaws before a single hand touched it. He could give them, above all, an annealing oven built right, a long low tunnel of falling heat that let a finished piece cool over hours instead of minutes, so the glass did not carry its own death inside it in the form of strains that split it days later on a shelf. The lehr was the part of the shop his knowledge served best, because it was a machine and not a craft, a thing of temperature and time and geometry, and he built it and it worked and it saved more pieces than any advice he could have given. He raised the ceiling. He kept finding, in shop after shop, that this was the shape of his gift and its limit both. He could lift the height a good hand could reach. He could not make the hand.

And then, past the ruin, there was a morning. He was not even there for the first good piece, which he thought later was fitting. The son came to find him in the counting room holding a thing in a cloth the way a man holds something he is afraid to trust, and unwrapped a small clear bowl, plain, unlovely, faintly green at the rim where the batch had not fully cleared, and set it on the table in the light. It was, by any measure Emeric's Earth had known, a poor bowl. It was also the first piece of glass made in Kaldo that had come off the bench whole and stayed whole through the lehr and would still be whole a month from now, and the son looked at it and then at Emeric with an expression Emeric had seen exactly once before, on Bram, the first time the foundry ran clean iron. It was the look of a man who had done a thing with his own hands that he had begun to fear he could not do. Emeric held the bad little bowl up to the window and turned it, and the light came through it not perfectly but through it, and he felt the specific joy of a thing that was going to work, which he had learned to distrust and could not, this time, make himself distrust at all.

The bowl told him the craft could be grown here. It did not tell him what to grow it into, and that was a separate question, and one he had already answered months before the furnace was lit, because it was a question of arithmetic and arithmetic he could do. There were a hundred things a glasshouse could make and he had ranked every one of them cold, on margin and on demand and on how many he could sell before the novelty wore, and the ranking had surprised the people he explained it to, because the obvious wealth in glass was windows, and windows sat near the bottom of his list. Windows were heavy and fragile and hard to carry far and a lord bought them once and then not again for a generation. The money in windows was real and it was slow and it was last. He would get to it. He would not start there.

He started, to the visible confusion of everyone he told, with spectacles. It was the least imposing product the shop could make and it was, he was certain, the engine that would pay for all the rest, and the reasoning was so plain to him that its strangeness to others was itself a kind of edge. Every other glass good was a thing a man bought because he wanted it. Spectacles were a thing a man bought because without them he could not do his work, and the men who could not do their work without them were precisely the men with money, the scribes and the clerks and the aging merchants and the lords past fifty whose eyes had begun, one quiet year at a time, to fail them at exactly the reading and the accounting that ran their lives. A merchant going blind at his own ledgers was not a customer who bought once and admired the purchase on his wall. He was a customer who would pay nearly anything, and pay it with relief, and come back when the glass scratched or his eyes moved further, and send every friend at his table who had started holding letters at arm's length. The demand did not wear out because eyes did not stop aging. It was the rarest thing in trade, a luxury that was also a need, sold to the only people who could afford it, and it renewed itself for free as fast as men grew old.

There was a second reason, quieter, that he did not put in the ledger, which was that spectacles were lenses, and a lens was a lens. The grinding that put a reading glass in front of a clerk's eye was the same grinding, in kind if not in degree, that could put a curve on glass sharp enough to make a small thing look large, and a thing that made small things large was a thing he had wanted in his hands since the first week he stood over a well full of death in a village that would not believe him. He did not say microscope aloud to anyone. He filed the capacity where he filed the things he meant to hold and not yet use, because he had learned the hard way in that village that a tool which showed a man a truth he had no frame to receive did not persuade him, it frightened him, and he had a use for that particular tool that was worth more than any bowl or pane and depended entirely on showing it to the right man at the right time. There would come a day he needed to convince someone educated, a physician or a scholar, of something he could not prove with a story, and on that day a lens ground fine enough would do the arguing. Until that day it was better that no one knew the shop could grind one. He could make the tool now. He chose, deliberately, not to, and set the choosing beside the other choices he was keeping, and told the shop to grind reading glasses.

The grinding was where the one machine he permitted himself earned its place, and he was careful about it in a way he was careful about very little, because it touched the exact seam between what a machine could do and what only a hand could. The blowing stayed human. He had made his peace with that and more than peace, because the hot work was the part that could not be copied, the reading of the glowing gather by feel, the timing that changed with every pot and every day and every man, and no device he could imagine would ever stand at that furnace and do it, and he had stopped wishing one would. But the grinding of a lens was not that. A lens was a shape, a fixed geometry, the same curve repeated true a thousand times, and a curve repeated true a thousand times was the one kind of labor his world already had a machine for. The pantograph took a single master lens, ground once and ground perfectly by the best hand in the shop, and let a dozen lesser hands trace it into a dozen copies that came off as true as the original, so that the shop's whole output was no better than its one master lens and no worse either, and the skill that used to live in every grinder's fingers now lived once, in the master, and was copied down the bench by the machine. It broke the bottleneck exactly where the bottleneck was breakable and nowhere else. It could not make the master. It could only copy him. Emeric still needed the one great hand to grind the one true lens, and that hand was human and scarce and had to be found and grown like every other, and once he had it the machine made it into a hundred hands. That was the trade he had wanted and the trade he got, and he looked at the row of men bent to the tracing benches with the pantograph running its shaped current down the line of them, and saw only clean lenses, and counted the output, and found it good.

He did not look longer than that, because there was nothing on the page that asked him to. The men at the grinding benches came in the morning and left in the evening and were paid well and housed well in the honest row he had built first and built to last, and the lenses they turned out were the best on the coast, and every ledger the shop kept said the same untroubled thing, which was that the glasshouse worked. What the ledgers did not say, because no one had thought to open a column for it, was what happened to a man who spent his days a hand's breadth from a running current of shaped mana, tracing the same curve from dawn to dusk, season upon season, breathing the same faint charged air. It was not written anywhere. It was not, yet, anything a man could point to. It was only a slow thing beginning in bodies at a bench, under a roof Emeric had built to shelter them, running on a machine he had filed under solved, and it would be a long time surfacing, and when it surfaced it would not surface as a number in any book he knew how to read. For now the glass came clean off the lehr and the lenses came true off the machine and the first cases of spectacles went out to the capital packed in straw, and Emeric, who had built a wealth out of aging eyes and grown a craft he could not perform and held back a tool he could have sold, walked the shop floor in the good green light and was, for once, entirely satisfied with what he could see, which was the whole of the problem and had always been.
