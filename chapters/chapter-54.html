Book 2, Chapter 15: Clicks

14 May. The ring.

The first click came at twenty to six in the evening on the Thursday, eleven hours after Bram bedded the drum on the stable wall, and Emeric was standing beside it with a cup of the hot green stuff when it went.

He didn't hear it. Nobody heard it. The pen heard it. It had been drawing its slow line round the turning drum all day, the line with the fine fur on it that was the sea against the breakwater and the kilns across the ring and the caldera breathing, and at twenty to six it jumped: a single sharp stroke up and down, the height of a fingernail, there and gone, and the fur again.

Bram had his nose almost on the paper.

"That's not the kilns," said Bram.

"No."

"That's not the sea, either. The sea doesn't do that. The sea's all..." He made a slow rolling movement with his hand. "That's a thing breaking. Something small. Not near."

They watched the drum turn. Two of the watch's people at the next fixed stone had stopped pretending not to look at it. Ilenne, who'd come out with them that afternoon, sat on the breakwater's crown with her knees up and her colour held and watched the land men watching a pen.

At twenty to seven there was another.

By midnight there'd been five, and Bram had sent the drum's sheet across by watch boat to the hard rooms and put a fresh one on and sat down beside the frame on a folded sail with a lamp and a blanket, and said he wasn't going anywhere, and Emeric hadn't argued.



By the Friday evening there'd been forty.

Emeric had the sheets spread on the table in the guest rooms under the cold lamps, end to end, five of them, with Bram's times pencilled over every spike; and Ferra had made a column of the gaps between them in her book; and the gaps were getting shorter. Not steadily. Not so you'd draw a line through them with a ruler. But they were. An hour and more at first. Then forty minutes. Then twenty, and a pair together, and twenty again. And the spikes were a little taller. Not much.

He knew what it looked like. He knew it in the way he knew a great many things, all at once, out of a place that had come with him and that he no longer thought of as anywhere in particular: a page in a book, a drawing of exactly this, a pen on a drum somewhere on the side of a mountain on the other side of the world, and the spikes coming closer and closer together for a week, and the town below the mountain told to go, and some of it going. Rock breaking. Rock breaking under heat and pressure, a little at a time, near something hot that was pushing up. A swarm, the book had called it. You read the gaps. When the gaps closed up, you went.

There were vents in the caldera. Everybody knew where. The open channel on the far side of the ring from the gate ran close past the biggest of them, where the water came up warm and the grove's glow was brightest, and there were homes along it, in the mid-water domes, and Below under it, and a channel that boats used every day.

"It's the vent," he said. "The big one, on the north side. Rock's breaking near it. That's what this is." He put his finger on the sheet where the gaps closed. "If it goes on like this, it'll do something in a few days. I don't know what. I'd want that channel closed and the people along it moved. Now. Before it's sure."

Ferra looked at her column.

"How sure are you?"

"Not very. I'm reading it the way you'd read rock where I learned it. That's the only way I know how to read it." He was quiet a moment. "I don't know their rock. I'd still want the people moved."

"Then tell Taurre," said Ferra.



The watch house was a long stone building on the island north of the gate, built out over the water on piers, with its back to the Rim and its front open to the ring through tall arched windows with no glass in them; and on the Saturday morning at eight it was full.

Emeric had asked for the meeting. Taurre had given it. He'd given it, Ilenne said on the way, the way the watch gave anything: on the watch's terms, in the watch's house, with the watch there. So it was there. A dozen of them on their legs at the long table that ran down the middle of the room, in their dark coats with their slates, and more along the walls, and in the water under the open windows a few heads, listening. A runner at the door. Taurre at the table's far end, with his own slate and his colour level.

And on the table in front of him, laid out end to end, the drum's sheets. Bram's. The watch had read them too. Taurre had said that it would, on the stable wall, when Bram set it up, and it had.

Emeric said what he'd said to Ferra. He said it plainly and Ilenne put it over. He put his finger on the gaps. He named the vent. He said: close the north channel, move the people along it, before it's sure, because by the time it's sure it's late.

Then he said the other thing, because it was true and because it needed saying first, not after.

"I'm reading this as I'd read it where I learned to read it," he said. "In rock. I know what rock does when it breaks near heat. I don't know your rock. And I don't know your glass. If it's something else, you'll know it and I won't."

Ilenne put it over. Taurre listened with his eyes on the sheets.

He'd been listening to them already. Emeric could see that. He'd been listening since before they came in. He had one hand flat on the last sheet, Saturday's, the one Bram had sent over at six, and his fingers were on the spikes, one after another, as if he were reading them with his hand the way Ressar read the stones; and his colour was perfectly still, and there was something in his face that hadn't been there at the gate or at the door or at the dry hall. Not alarm. Recognition.

He said something short. He said it not to Emeric but to the table, to his own people, and three or four of them looked up quickly.

"He says it's not the vent," said Ilenne.

Taurre spoke again, longer, and Ilenne gave it a sentence behind, carefully.

"He says he knows the sound. Not from the drum. From the water. He says he heard it twice. Once when he was young, at a footing on the outer wall, and once at a dome in the shallows. Small work, both. He says it's glass." She listened. "Held glass. When it's hot too long. When the holding goes out of it. It doesn't crack, he says. It lets go. Little by little, here, here." She tapped the table twice with one finger, apart. "Then all at once."

"Where?"

Taurre answered that without being asked twice. He didn't point at the north. He turned in his chair and pointed through the arched window behind him, down and to the left, at the Rim's own side of the ring, where the inner wall went down under the islands.

"He says there's a seal," said Ilenne. "On a vent. Under there. On the Rim's side, not the north. Six years old. Built to stop the gas. He says behind it there's a structure. Held glass. Hot." She swallowed. "He says nobody looks behind a seal. That's what a seal is for."

Emeric looked at the sheets.

He looked at them, and at Taurre's hand on them, and he understood two things at once and didn't like either of them.

The first was that the man was right. He couldn't have said how he knew. He hadn't heard the sound in the water. He'd never heard held glass let go. But he could see Taurre's face, and he could see the faces down the table, and four or five of the older ones had gone still in exactly the way Taurre had gone still, the way men go still when somebody names a thing they've seen once and hoped not to see again; and the drum had no direction in it, none, only the size of each click and when it came. Bram's drum could tell you something was breaking. It couldn't tell you where.

The second was that if they'd listened to him they'd have cleared the wrong side of the ring.

They'd have shut the north channel and moved the people along it, and sent the boats round the other way, past the Rim's side, past the seal. Toward it.

He knew that before anybody at the table said it. Nobody said it. Taurre didn't. Taurre looked at him across the length of the table with his colour level and his hand flat on the last sheet and waited to see what the land king would do.

"Then it's your water," said Emeric. "Your call."

Ilenne put it over.

"And I was wrong," he said. "I'd have cleared the north. Tell him that. Tell them all."

Ilenne looked at him. Then she told them.

It went down the table. He felt it go. Nobody's colour did very much; they were the watch, and they held it. But there was a small turning of heads, and a man near the far end with grey in his hair looked at Emeric for a moment longer than he looked at anything else, and then wrote on his slate; and Emeric knew, as surely as if he'd read it, that it had been written down. On the record. A land king had read the water wrong in the watch's house in front of the watch, and said so.

It cost something. He could feel what. Three days ago he'd stood in the dry hall and let the whole court see him not answer a charge he couldn't answer. Now the watch had seen him answer a reading wrong. The next time he said anything to anyone in Aumere about the water, there'd be a man at a table somewhere with a slate who'd say: he was wrong about the clicks.

He'd have paid it again. That didn't make it cheap.

Taurre moved fast. Faster than Emeric had seen anyone in Aumere move about anything.

Before eleven he'd closed every channel within half a mile of the seal, on both sides of the inner wall: closed by the watch, with marker floats and boats and a man at each end, so that nothing swam in or out. He'd moved the people out of the two hard rooms on the Rim's side nearest it, a trade hall and a store, and had both rooms flooded, slowly, over the day, by their own vents, because a full room couldn't break. He'd sent word Below to the homes under it. And he'd sent a crew.

A repair crew: six divers, merfolk, with their tools and their props and a sled of shoring timbers, to go down behind the seal by the passage the builders had left, and look at the structure, and shore it where it was weakest. Taurre had read the clicks too. He'd read the gaps, as Emeric had, on the sheets, with his hand. And he'd read them against what he'd heard twice in his life on smaller work, and he'd judged it.

"He says there's time," said Ilenne. "Days. He says glass like this goes slow until it goes. He says he has days to shore it."

"How many?"

Ilenne asked. Taurre held up three fingers, and then, after a moment, two.

Emeric looked at the last sheet on the table, at the gaps closing up toward its right-hand edge. He didn't know their glass. He'd just said so in front of everybody, and been right to, and he wasn't going to stand in the watch's house an hour later and tell the watch it was wrong about its own water because a pen on a drum made him uneasy.

He said nothing. He went out.



The Challenger went down at two.

It had been arranged for a week. The Keepers' etchers had asked to see her glass under load, the stacked nose, three domes one inside the next, down on the inner wall at working depth where the sea pressed on it, because they'd seen it at the gate and wanted to know what land glass did when it was asked to do their glass's work. The queen had given leave. Ressar was going down in her. Ned had asked, and Emeric had said yes, and the watch had given her a route: the stable inner wall on the gate's side of the ring, well away from the Rim's side, inside the closed water's markers by half a mile.

Taurre had looked at the route again that morning, after the meeting, and let it stand.

So at two the Challenger went down off her cradle under the K Endeavor's lee, outside the ring, and came in through the gate on the surface behind a pilot, and went under inside, slowly, on her stones, with her floods lit, and Ressar sitting bolt upright in the observer's seat behind Ned with a stone on his knees; and Emeric stood at the window of the watch house and watched the place where she'd gone until the water closed over it.

She was loud. She was always loud. He couldn't hear her, but he knew she was: the pumps and the trim and the floods' hum and the whole of her going through the water like a cart going through a wood. Fletcher had said so in March, and before him Ilenne, laughing, the first time she ever heard her: Loud.

At twenty past three Bram came on the set from the stable wall.

"They're running together," said Bram.

His voice was quite flat. Emeric had heard it go flat like that perhaps twice in all the years he'd known him.

"How close?"

"There's no gaps. There's no gaps at all. It's one long..." A pause. "The pen won't come down. It's just going. It's going right off the..."

Emeric turned from the window.

Taurre was already on his feet. He'd heard it without Bram, because in the corner of the watch house, on a stool, there was a man of the watch sitting with his eyes shut and both hands flat on a grey stone set into the floor, a stone with lines running from it out through the wall and down into the sea; and the man's eyes had come open and he'd said a single word, loud, and every head in the room had turned.

"Listening," said Ilenne, beside Emeric, very fast. "The nodes. In the water. He hears what they hear. He says..."

She didn't finish.

The water under the Rim's side of the ring went white.

Emeric saw it through the arched window. He saw it before he understood it: a patch of the ring's flat green water a quarter of a mile off, under the inner wall below the Rim's islands, going pale. Then it bulged. It came up in a low dome of white water the size of a field, boiling, heaving, and broke, and went on breaking, a great slow churning boil that didn't stop, and over it, rising off it into the still afternoon, a haze.

He smelled it a second later. Rotten eggs, struck matches, a smell that went into the back of his head like a nail.

His sense was useless. He'd known it would be. In this water, this thick, it came back to him from a boat's length and no further; he'd been deaf at the rail since the wall water. He stood at the window with every part of him open and felt nothing at all past the piers, and the boil went on.

Taurre was shouting. Not loud. He didn't shout loud. But the whole watch house was moving, people going out through the doors and down to the boats under the piers, and the runner gone, and slates everywhere; and the man at the listening stone had both hands flat on it again and his eyes shut, and his lips moving, saying something over and over that Ilenne put into Kaldo without being asked.

"A passage," said Ilenne. "Gone. Behind the seal. The passage the crew went in by. Gone. He hears the glass." She stopped. "He hears a dome."

"Whose dome?"

"Theirs. The hard room by the seal, the one they work from. It's..." She listened. "It's holding. It's failing. Slow. Not all at once. Slow."

"And the crew?"

Ilenne didn't answer. The man at the stone was counting. Emeric could hear the shape of it, the same short sound over and over, one, one, one, as you'd count heads off a boat. He counted five. Then he stopped, and listened, and his face changed, and he didn't say six.

And then, from the wrong side of the ring, from the gate's side, from the stable inner wall where the Challenger was, the sea went up.

It wasn't a boil. It was a roar.

Emeric heard it this time. Everyone did. It came up through the water and the rock and the piers under the watch house, a deep tearing bellow like a waterfall turned on its side, and it went on, and the sea half a mile off along the inner wall toward the gate tore open in a long straight white wound, a furrow, a hundred yards of white water racing across the ring's green as if something under it were dragging a plough through the sea at the speed of a train. It ran straight. Dead straight. Away from the Rim's side, away from the boil and the haze, along the stable wall toward the gate and the clean water at the ring's centre.

The rush. Ned had thrown the rush.

Emeric had sat in the shed on the Cradle with Ned and Noll and Fletcher and the drawings and talked about it for an evening and said: once. One scene. Straight, loud, hungry. You'll use it to get out of something you can't get out of any other way, and then you'll never use it again on that voyage, because it drinks your air. Ned had said, what's it for, then, if you only get the once. Emeric had said, for the once.

The furrow ran on for most of a minute. Then it stopped, all at once, as if someone had turned it off; and in the ring's centre, in clean water, far from the haze, the Challenger came up.

She came up fast. She broached, her nose out of the water to the first ring of her glass, and fell back with a slap they heard from the watch house, and lay there rolling on the flat green water with the white of her wake still settling behind her, and her floods still lit.

The set on Emeric's collar clicked.

"Challenger," said Ned. He was breathing hard. "We're up. We're all right. All up. Ressar's all right." A pause. "He's not very happy. But he's all right."

"What happened?"

"The water went. Off to port, by the Rim's side. It went white and then it went bad. You could see it coming, a sort of... the floods went brown in it. Noll had the gas on the bottles before it reached us and the lime went straight cloudy and I wasn't waiting to find out what next." Ned stopped for breath. "So I threw it. Straight down the clean channel. I'm sorry about the noise."

Another voice came on behind his, closer to the set. Noll's.

"A fifth," said Noll. "We're a fifth down on the bank. In under a minute. That's the rush." He paused. "That's the rush for this voyage. We don't do it again. Not on this one. I'm saying it now so it's said."

"It's said," said Emeric.

He looked out of the window, at the Challenger rolling in the clean water in the middle of the ring with every soul on her alive, and the long white scar of her wake still across the green, and the haze over the Rim's side, and the boil.

And every person on Sael, he thought, every person in every yard and kiln and rope-walk and house on that whole ring of islands, had just heard her do it.



It came out piece by piece over the afternoon, and every piece was worse.

The crew had been six. Five had come out. They'd been working on the structure's face, shoring it, with their props and timbers, when the clicks ran together, and they'd heard what the man at the listening stone had heard, and they'd known it, the way the watch knew it, and gone: back out down the passage they'd come in by, which went, behind them, as the last of the five came through. The sixth hadn't come. He'd been at the far side of the dome they worked from, the little hard room by the seal, at its far door, with the bell beyond it; and when the passage went and the water round the dome went foul he'd gone through that door into the bell, because that was what you did, and shut it.

So he was alive. The man at the listening stone had heard him. Tapping. On the bell's glass, slowly, in a rhythm the watch knew, which meant alive and air and not hurt.

His name was Haurin.

He was in a bell at the far side of a hard dome that was failing, slowly, on the Rim's side of the ring, sixty feet down or so, in water that a man couldn't breathe and a merfolk couldn't either, behind a passage that wasn't there any more.

And the listening nodes had heard everything.

That was the last piece, and it came at dusk, and it didn't come from the watch. It came from the Rim.

The nodes were strung all down the inner wall at intervals, on lines, listening, and they'd carried it all up to the stone in the watch house corner, and the man there had written it down as it came: the clicks running together; the passage going; the dome; the boil. And before any of it, minutes before, a thing on the stable inner wall, half a mile from the seal, going through the water like a cart through a wood. The land ship. Loud. The nodes had her there, on the wall, at working depth, close enough to the Rim's side to be heard all the way along it, right up to the minute the water went.

And then they had her rush, after. Which every house on the Rim had heard for itself.

By dusk, Ilenne said, coming back from the gate with her colour held so hard it shone, the whole of Sael was saying it. The land ship had been down on the wall. The land ship had shaken the glass loose. And then it had run, roaring, and left a man behind a dome.

"It isn't true," said Ferra.

"I know it isn't true." Ilenne sat down. "The nodes have her there. That's true. The nodes have her loud. That's true. Then it went. They have that." She looked at the table. "Taurre's people have it on the slate. In order."

"And the clicks? Bram's drum? Two days before she went down?"

Ilenne didn't answer that at once.

"Taurre says the watch won't read the drum," she said at last. "Not now. Not for this. He says it's the land's drum. The land ship is the one... the one they say. He says the watch doesn't take readings from the one who's accused." She said it as flatly as she could. "He says it to everyone. He says it so it's said."

Bram, at the end of the table, put his face in his hands.

"It's his water," said Emeric.

"It's his water," Ferra agreed, quite quietly. "And it was our drum that told him. And he's right not to read it. If somebody accused us of something at home with a gauge they'd built themselves, I wouldn't read it either."

Ressar had come in at five, still damp, and said his piece at the watch house: that he'd been in the land ship from the moment she went down until she came up, that she'd been where the watch's route said she would be and nowhere else, half a mile from the seal on the stable wall, and that she'd run because the water went bad round her and not before. Ilenne had heard him say it. A Keeper's word. Some of the watch had believed him and some hadn't.

"The Keepers are split," said Ilenne. "Some say the land is the cure. Some say the land is the ones who took the groves." She looked at Emeric. "He said it. He was in her. Half of them believe him because he's Ressar. Half of them say he would, wouldn't he. He went down in her because he wanted to."



He went back to the watch house at seven. He didn't go to argue.

Taurre was at the long table with the haze hanging over the ring behind him through the arched windows, going red in the last light, and his people round him, and three boats under the piers being loaded with things Emeric didn't recognise. Taurre looked up when he came in. His colour did nothing.

Emeric stopped at the table's near end, where he'd stood that morning, and didn't sit.

"What do you need measured?" he said.

Ilenne put it over. Taurre took his time.

Then he told him. The water round the dome: what was in it, how much, how far it went. The dome itself: how fast it was failing. The bell's air. Not guessed. Measured, before anybody went near, and measured again, and written. He said nobody went into that water until he knew what was in it, and nobody went near that dome until it couldn't break, and that the order was his and wouldn't change for anybody, not the queen and not the land.

"Good," said Emeric.

Taurre's colour moved, very slightly.

"We've bottles on the Challenger," said Emeric. "For the water. They tell you if it's foul and what with. We can draw them on a line from the passage mouth without anybody going in. We've a man who can read them." He paused. "And there's one thing I'll say now, before anybody asks, so it's said."

The watch waited.

"Our gill packs," said Emeric. "The ones my divers wear. They take their air out of the water, the same as your gills do. Out of what's in it. That water out there hasn't got what they need in it, and it's got things in it that'll hurt. They'll fail in it. The same as gills fail." He looked down the table. "So if anybody's thinking a land diver with a pack on his back can swim through that where one of yours can't, he can't. Nobody goes in on a pack."

Ilenne put it over.

There was a silence down the table. Then the grey-haired man who'd written on his slate that morning wrote on it again.

Taurre said something.

"He says thank you," said Ilenne. "He says he would have asked. He says he's glad not to have to." She hesitated. "He says the bottles. Tomorrow. At first light."



The Keepers' hall was quiet when he came through it at nine on his way back to the guest rooms.

It was always quiet, with its shelves of stones; but tonight there was somebody in it. A merfolk woman on her legs, young, in a plain coat with the hem wet, sitting on the stone bench under the shelves by the far door with her hands in her lap and her colour grey, not held, just grey, all the way down. She was looking at the door to the tunnel. Not at him. At the door he'd come through, and past it, at the dark.

One of the younger Keepers was sitting with her. Not talking. Sitting.

Ilenne, behind Emeric, stopped.

"Oussa," she said, very low. "His. Haurin's. They're..." She found the word. "Partners." She swallowed. "She's waiting. Here. Because here is where the watch's runners come through to the Keepers. If there's news, it comes through here first."

Emeric looked at the woman on the bench.

She didn't look at him. She went on watching the door. Her hands were folded in her lap, quite still, and her colour was the grey of the shallows, and she was waiting, as you'd wait at a harbour wall, for a boat that was late.

He thought of a dozen things he could say to her. All of them were lies, or promises he couldn't keep in somebody else's water, or both. The bottles were at first light. The dome was failing. A man was in a bell behind it with the air going bad round him, and the only thing that would get him out was measuring, and planning, and doing it right, slowly, in order, the watch's way.

So he didn't say anything to her. He went past her through the hall, and through the far door into the tunnel, and into the guest rooms, where Bram, and Noll in from the Challenger, had her gas bottles out on the table under the lamps, and the lime water, and the lead papers in their packet; and he sat down with them, and pulled the drawings of the passage toward him, and went to work.

It was the right thing. He knew it was the right thing.

It felt like nothing at all.
