Book 2, Chapter 4: Thick Water

The thing came up under the bow at a little after eleven on the Friday, and lay there.

Zale had the wheel. He'd taken it at ten because Hesk wanted his dinner and because he liked her at this pace, low and slow behind the slick with her veins at a murmur and the sea going by fifty feet under the keel, near enough to smell. He saw it come up the way you'd see a cloud's shadow come over a field, only from underneath. A darkness in the water, flat and wide. Wider. It rose until it was a yard under the surface and then it simply stopped rising, and lay along the sea ahead of them with the swell going over it, and it was as wide as his mainsail and longer, and the edges of it moved.

"Hesk," he said, not loudly.

Hesk came up the companion with his bowl. He looked over the bow. He put the bowl down on the hatch.

The two hands forward had seen it too. One of them had gone back from the rail so fast he'd sat down on a coil. The other was pointing, which was no use to anybody, and saying "It's got wings, it's got wings, Captain, it's..." in a voice that went up at the end.

"It's a fish," said Zale.

"It's a blanket," said the hand. "It's a blanket with a face."

It did have a face, or it had something at the front end that turned, slowly, and two dark round places that might have been eyes looking straight up at the bottom of his ship. On the set behind him, somebody on escort one was asking escort three if they could see what Zale was seeing, and escort three was saying yes, and could they, and nobody was saying anything useful at all. He took the handset off its hook.

"Zale. It's lying still. Keep your guns cold. I'll ask."

He didn't need to ask. Ilenne had come up out of the cabin with her hair still wet from the basin and her hood down, and she stood at the bow rail and looked at the thing in the water for about the time it takes to read a line, and then she laughed. It was the laugh she had when somebody fell off something.

"Oh," she said. "Him."

"You know him."

"I know what. Not him exactly." She leaned on the rail. "It sleeps. It comes up in the sun, it lies, it is warm, it sleeps. It eats small fish. Very small." She showed him a size with her fingers. "Children ride them. You hold the front, it does not notice, you go all round the outside of the city and back before it wakes up."

"Children ride them," said Zale, into the handset.

A silence on the set, and then Ward's voice from the K Amara, flat: "Noted."

The slick had stopped, a mile ahead. It lay on the sea a while, and then it shortened, and came back toward them, fast, faster than he'd seen it move all week, and the thing under his bow felt it coming before any of them saw it. The wide back rippled at the edges all round at once, every inch of it, and it tipped and slid off sideways and down into the dark with a long slow beat of the whole of itself, and was gone, and the place where it had been was only blue.

The slick turned and went back to its mile.

"He tells it, go," said Ilenne. "It is in the road. The road is ours today." She sounded sorry about it. "It was only sleeping."

"Well, it's awake now," said Hesk, and picked up his bowl again, and found it had gone cold, and said a word about that.

The hand who'd sat on the coil was still sitting on it. Zale left him there. It would do him no harm to sit a while with the thought that the sea had things in it as big as a sail that children rode for fun, and that this was the sea they were going to live on for the rest of the year.

That afternoon they passed the marker. It stood on a reef that showed brown under the water for a quarter of a mile, a post of the same grey reef-stone as the pillar, waist high above the sea, with a band of the same coloured glass round the top of it. Somebody had cut a mark into the stone below the glass: a curved line with an arrow at the end of it, pointing south-west, the way the water was setting. Ilenne said it showed the current. She said it as if a post that told you which way the sea was going was the most ordinary thing in the world to find in the middle of it, which, he supposed, to her it was.

He looked back at it a long while after it had gone past, as long as he could see it. The glass was clean.



They climbed on the Saturday night, at ten, and Ilenne came up on deck after him at twenty past with her teeth going.

He'd known she would. He'd been up there with her in March, and in the Band over the wall water a week ago, and on the Wednesday for the ride, and she'd been all right in the March run because it was short, and all right since because she'd stayed below in her blankets with the door shut. Tonight she'd come up anyway. She stood at the lee rail in her own cloak with her arms wrapped round herself, looking at the cloud a mile under the keel with the moon on it, and the stars, which were many and close up here, and she was shaking so hard he could hear the cloak.

"Go down," he said.

"I look first."

"You'll look like a fish in an icebox."

"I don't know icebox."

"It's a box. It's cold. You put fish in it." He took his coat off. It was the long blue one, the good one, which had been his mother's choice and which he'd worn at the yard on the morning they left because she'd have known if he hadn't. He put it round Ilenne's shoulders over the cloak, and she let him, and stood inside it, and it came down to her ankles.

"It's cold up here," he said.

"Yes."

She didn't give it back. He'd thought she would, after a minute, when she'd stopped shaking, and she didn't, and he didn't ask for it. They stood at the rail while the racks pulled all down the line and the cold came off the brass, and after a while he went and fetched another coat from the locker, an old pea jacket of Hesk's that smelled of tar and pipe, and put it on, and came back.

The set clicked behind him, the night set, which was his until Monday. He'd learned enough of the key by now to know it was the K Amara asking for the hour's figures and not the end of the world. He went and sent them, slowly, one letter at a time, with his tongue between his teeth, and heard Ferra's operator acknowledge with a speed that was nearly rude.

When he came back to the rail Ilenne was still there in his coat, with the collar turned up round her face, and there was a little blue at her throat, above the collar, which he put down to the cold.

He looked at the stars instead, and kept his hands on the rail.



Ferra moved the mark on the Sunday because Imani said so, and wrote the new figure on all eight sheets before she'd finished her tea.

It was the second notch since the wall. Imani raised it every few nights by the cards, a notch at a time, hull by hull, and Ferra moved it on paper and the boatswains moved it on the cabin doors: the scrubber's setting, a brass pin in a slot, and each hole it went up let more of the thick air into a shut cabin overnight. On the K Amara by Sunday the pin stood two holes higher than at the wall. Most of the knights had stopped using their cabins at all. They slept on the aprons in their bags, in rows, with a watch walking between them, because Ward had said they might once the cards said so and the cards had said so on the Friday, and a knight who'd bled off heat all day slept better in the cold.

The unchanged still went below at dusk. They would for weeks. Ferra had a column for it.

She had a column for most things, and by the Sunday the columns had a shape. Charge, which the racks' gauges gave her twice a day and which had come down more slowly since the wall than her sheets had said it would, so that the margin's pencil figure, the free charge Noll's dials had found, was slowly becoming a figure she'd have to write in ink if it went on. She didn't write it in ink. Food, which was what the sheet said, because nobody had touched the hold yet but the galleys. Noses: Imani's count, sent at noon and dusk, which had been four on the Wednesday and none since. Distance, which Sorrel gave her by the log and the sun, and which was smaller every day than the day before, because Imani's stages kept the day's run short and the old one went at the pace it went. Ilenne said it was tired of waiting for them. Ferra wrote that in the margin too, because it was the only reason anybody had offered.

At three Sorrel came on the set to say there was something on the water to the south. Ferra went out to the bridge deck's forward glass with Ward. It was a long way off, a smudge on the horizon in the hazy south light, low and dark, with points standing up from it, and something pale above it that might have been smoke or might have been birds.

"Islands?" said Ward.

"It's moving," said Sorrel's voice. "Slowly. Ilenne says, and I'm to say it as she says it: it is a camp of the outer water, for fishing. Not hers. She won't go near it."

"Not hers meaning what?"

A pause. "She says, not her mother's people. Other people. She says they don't like the queen's house and the queen's house doesn't like them, and they're none of our business, and they'd very much rather we weren't theirs."

Ahead of them the slick turned. It went off south-west by west, away from the smudge, and the K Discovery turned after it, and the convoy turned after her in its order, and Ferra watched the thing on the horizon go round from their port bow to their port quarter over the next two hours, without ever getting nearer. Ward watched it with her the whole time with his hands behind his back.

"People," he said at last. "Out here. Living on a raft."

"Or on something."

"Not hers to command." He said it as though he were filing it under a heading he didn't like. "That's twice."

She knew what the first time was. The pillar. The post on the reef with the arrow cut in it. Somebody had put both there and somebody kept them clean, and it wasn't the people they were sailing to meet, or if it was, it was in a way that had nothing to do with them. The sea was somebody's. It had been somebody's all along. They'd sailed out onto it as if it were empty.

Ilenne came across to the K Amara at five in Zale's boat, with Zale at the oars, for the courtesy lesson.

It had been Ward's idea, on the second night past the wall: an hour every second evening on the K Amara's aft deck for anybody who'd be at the door or in the halls, on the courtesies, the colours, the signs, with Ilenne to teach and Zale to put it into Kaldo where her Kaldo ran out. Ferra came to all of them. She had a page in the back of the book with the signs drawn on it. Tonight there were thirty on the aft deck, knights and officers and Armand in a chair with a rug, and Maeve and Mira at the rail at the back, and Emeric on a hatch cover at the side, and Ilenne stood in front of them with her hood back and taught for an hour, and Ferra watched her.

She was good. She'd been bad at it in the autumn, impatient, cross when they got it wrong; she was patient now, and exact, and she held still while she taught, so still that even in the thick air her colour stayed where she'd put it, a pale even sheen at the throat and nothing else. Thirty people watched her for an hour and saw nothing she didn't mean them to see. Ferra had watched her do it every second evening for a week. She was doing it with Ward, and with Armand, and with the knights, and with Emeric, who asked two questions, both good, and got two careful answers.

And then Zale, who'd been sitting on the boat's thwart at the foot of the ladder with his oars shipped, called up something about the time, and she turned her head to answer him, and it went. All of it. It came up her throat, the deep-water blue, dark as the sea under a hull, in less time than it took her to say "Yes, I know", and then she'd turned back to the deck and it was gone again, held, put away. It had taken perhaps two seconds.

Ferra looked round the deck. Nobody had seen it. They'd been looking at their own hands, working out the sign for "after you". She looked down at the boat. Zale was looking at his oars.

She didn't write that down. She did make a small mark in the margin of the lesson's page, which meant nothing to anyone but her.

At the back of the deck, by the rail, Mira was showing Maeve the sign for "after you" with her hands, as if Maeve couldn't see it from the front, and standing considerably closer to do it than the sign required. Maeve, who had been taught to sit a horse and hold a court and say no to her mother, was letting her.

Emeric saw it at the same moment Ferra did. She saw his head turn. She saw him take it in, the two of them at the rail, Maeve's face, and his face did a thing she knew well, the thing it did when a figure didn't add; and then his eyes went past them both, a yard further along the rail, to where Bren was leaning on his elbows grinning at the sea, and stopped there, and narrowed, and stayed.

Bren felt it. You couldn't not. He looked round, and saw the king looking at him, and stopped grinning, and straightened up, and stood correctly at the rail looking at nothing for the rest of the lesson with the air of a man who knew he'd done something and couldn't think what.

Ferra bent over her book. Beside her Ward said, without moving his lips, "Are you going to tell him?"

"No," said Ferra.



On the Tuesday night it came up under them.

The convoy was down on the sea at a quarter to midnight in its string, sea anchors out, lamps at the mark, as it had been every night for a week; and the old one had gone under at dusk as it went under every dusk, to feed, Ilenne said, somewhere deep where nobody could follow. Zale was asleep. He was asleep in his own bunk for the first night in a week, because Linnet had come back on the night key on the Monday, as the card said she might, and had said that if he so much as looked at the set she'd send him to the K Endeavor with a note.

The set woke him anyway.

"All hulls." Emeric's voice. Not loud. Very clear. "Something under us. Big. Coming up slow from deep. I can't read it. All hulls, up off the water now. Now."

He was on deck before he knew he was out of the bunk. Hesk was already at the vein housing with the cover off and his hands in it. All down the line, in the dark, the lamps were going up off the sea: the K Amara's great row of them lifting, slow and then faster, the water sheeting off her keel and pattering on the sea like rain; escort one, escort three, the K Endeavor with the Challenger slung under her, hull four, the K Tam beside them, lifting, every one, twenty feet, fifty, a hundred, until the sea round Zale's ship was empty and dark and there was nothing on it but Zale's ship.

"Hesk."

"They're banked." Hesk didn't look up. "We banked them at dusk. Our night to sit. She's the pickup. She sits." His hands were moving. "Five minutes to warm. Four if she's kind."

Zale had made that rule himself. The pickup pair took the night in turns: one sat on the water with her veins banked and cold to save the charge, because somebody had to be on the water if somebody went in it, and the other kept hers warm. Tonight the K Tam had the warm watch, and Nell Haddow had heard the king say now. Tonight it was Zale's turn to sit. He'd thought it a good rule at dusk.

"Linnet. Tell them we're warming. Tell them five minutes."

"Telling."

The sea was quiet. The swell came up the side and went down. He stood at the rail and looked over it into the black water and saw nothing at all, and the deckhands were coming up from below in their shirts, all four, and the youngest of them, who'd sat on the coil on Friday, said, "What is it?" and Zale said "Stay off the waist," and the boy went to the waist, because that was where the ladder was, and leaned over it.

The ship jerked.

It came through the deck into his feet, a single hard tug from forward, the whole hull checking on the swell as if she'd been made fast to the sea bed. Then the sea anchor's line went. He heard it go, a crack like a gun, and the hull came free and swung, and something in the water ahead of her made a sound he'd never heard water make, a long sucking pull that went on and on.

"It's got the anchor," said Hesk. "It's eating the anchor. Three minutes."

Then the rudder.

The wheel spun in his hands so hard it took the skin off his palms, and he let go of it, and it went round and round on its own with the spokes a blur, and under the stern there was a grinding, splintering noise that he felt in his teeth. The ship heeled. She went over to port, a long way, and everything on deck that wasn't lashed went over with her, and the youngest deckhand at the waist went over the rail.

He didn't shout. Zale heard him go in. That was all. A splash, and then nothing.

Ilenne was already past him.

She'd come up from below at the set's first word, as he had, and she'd been at the companion head, and now she went down the deck in her shift in three strides and over the rail after the boy without stopping, without a word, and her legs were gone before she hit the water.

"Lamps," said Zale. "Every lamp in the ship. Over the side. Now."

They got them up. The two big deck lamps on their hooks, the binnacle lamp, the cabin lamps, the two from the galley, Linnet's from the radio cabin, and they hung them over the port rail on boathooks and lines and anything else, so that the sea under her side lit up yellow for twenty yards, and it was no good at all. The light went into the water a few yards and stopped. He could see the swell, lit gold on its face. He could see the white of the boy's shirt, there, held up, held, and Ilenne's head and shoulders beside it, shining, and her arm round him. And under them, a little way under, the water moving round and round in a long slow circle that was much bigger than the lit place, and had no edges, and was coming round again.

She was talking to it. He could see her colour going under the water, fast, the arguing colours, the ones she'd used on the old one at the ladder. It didn't answer. It came round again under her, a little nearer, and he saw her stop, and he saw her understand something, and then she wasn't talking any more, she was only holding the boy up and turning in the water to keep her face to the circle, and the boy was crying.

"It's not listening," she said. She said it up to Zale, clearly, in Kaldo, in a voice he'd never heard from her. "It is hungry. It does not hear. It is hungry."

The set was going behind him. Ward, on the K Amara, a hundred feet over the sea, half a mile down the line: knights at the apron rail with the night glass, and they could see the swirl, they could see it plainly, the water going round, and nothing under it, nothing at all. A round into that water would go a few feet and stop. They had nothing to give him. Ward said so, in four words, and Zale was grateful to him for not saying anything else.

Then the sea lit up white from above.

Emeric came down out of the dark over the K Discovery's quarter with his hands full of fire, both of them, and he was holding it out from him the way a man holds out a torch in a cellar, and the sea under him blazed. It didn't help either. It only made the gold water white. He came down until he was ten feet over the swell beside Ilenne and the boy, and Zale saw him look into the water at the circle, and saw his face, which had nothing in it but the thing in front of him. He was going to go in.

"No!" Ilenne had her free arm up, at him, flat, the sign for stop that Zale had watched her teach thirty people on Sunday. "No! The old one comes. I feel it. You go in, it does not know you from food. It does not know him from you. Stay up." She was shaking her head, hard, water flying off her hair. "Stay up!"

He stayed up. Zale saw what it cost. He hung there in the air with the fire in his hands and his whole body leaning at the water, and stayed.

Then the sea came up.

It came up under the circle, from straight down, the whole lit patch of water lifting at once in a long smooth hump that went up past the height of the rail, and Zale saw grey in it, pitted grey, sheeting, and then the hump broke and something else broke with it. He never saw what. He saw a thing come out of the water beside the grey for one second that was darker, and had a mouth, and was turning; and then both of them were under, and the sea where they'd been heaved and boiled and went white, and the ship went up on the wave and over and down, and two of his lamps went in, and the boy and Ilenne went up on the wave too, and down, and she held him the whole way.

It went on under them for perhaps a minute. He felt it through the hull. Blows, far down, slow and huge, like doors slamming in a house a long way off. Then they stopped.

The sea flattened. The swell came back over the place a yard at a time.

"It goes down," said Ilenne, in the water. She was so bright the boy's face beside her was lit by it. "It goes down. It does not come back." She looked up at Emeric, still hanging over her with the fire in his hands. "Now. Now you help."

He had the boy out of the water before she'd finished saying it.

They got her up the ladder after. She came up it on her own, on her legs again, in her soaked shift, and stood on the deck and shook, and Zale went and put his arms round her, which he had never done, and she let him, and put her wet face against his shoulder and stayed there.

"He went in," she said into his shirt. "He went at the waist. I tell you, all the week, they lean on the waist."

"I know."

"It did not listen. Nothing does not listen. Everything in the sea listens, a little, even the stupid ones. That one..." She stopped. "That is the hungry. Your Hesk's saying. That is the one it means."

"I know," said Zale again.

Over his shoulder he could see Emeric on the deck on one knee by the boy, with his hand flat on the boy's leg above the knee, where the shin had gone wrong; and he could see the hulls coming down out of the dark one by one, slowly, onto the sea round them. Linnet, behind him in the radio cabin door, was saying into the set that the boy was out, and breathing, and that his leg was broken, and that the captain's ship had no rudder and no sea anchor and was going round in a slow circle on the swell, which was all quite true.



The ledger had two new lines in it by the Wednesday noon, and Ferra had written both of them herself.

The first was the charge. Seven hulls had lifted off the sea at a quarter to midnight and stayed up for an hour and forty minutes, on their stones, with nothing in the Band to fill them; and the stones had paid for it out of what the racks held. She'd had the gauges read at one, at two and at dawn. It wasn't ruin. It was the best part of a day's slow fill from the thick air, gone in a night, and the next climb, which she'd had down for the Thursday night by the racks, was now the Wednesday night, a day early, and would come sooner still if it happened again. She wrote the figure. She wrote under it, in pencil, if twice, the climbs come every other night, and under that, smaller, and every climb is hours in the Band. She didn't know why that second line bothered her. She left it there.

The second was Zale's ship. One sea anchor, with its line, lost. One rudder, the blade broken off below the stock, the stock sprung. Bram had been across at dawn in the K Tam's boat with Dagny and had looked at it from the water and said it was a dockyard job and he'd do it at sea anyway, at the first two days they sat still, and not before; and until then she'd be steered by her veins, which Zale could do, and which he said was like steering a horse with your knees.

She didn't write down the boy. Imani had him; Ilenne had gone across to the K Endeavor with him in Zale's boat. Imani had sent across at seven: I.: Tibia, clean break, set. Two weeks on my deck, then a crutch. He asks after the woman who held him up. I said she's in the sea tank and he can't see her, and he said he knew that, he wanted to know if she was all right.

Ferra wrote back She is, which was more than she knew, and then went to find out.

The ring went together that afternoon. It was Ward's, and it was simple: from tonight the hulls came down in a circle and not a string, bows out, a hundred and fifty yards apart, with Zale's ship in the middle of them, where nothing could come up under her that didn't come up under the ring first. She watched it form from the bridge deck. Seven ships on the sea in a wheel and the small one at its hub, without a rudder, tied by a long line to the K Tam's stern so as not to drift; and at dusk, when the slick a mile ahead shortened and went down for the night, it didn't go down for the night.

It came back. It came back slowly along the sea toward them, and through the gap between escort one and hull four, and stopped. And then it was under Zale's ship. Ferra saw the water round her go smooth for a hundred yards, all round, and stay smooth.

"It's lying under her," said Sorrel's voice, on the set. "Ilenne says she asked it. She says it said yes before she'd finished asking." A pause. "She says it was offended. She says it's offended that something came at its queen's guests while it was at dinner, and it'll be offended all the way to the door, and it'll sleep under Zale's ship until we get there."

Ward, beside her, let out a breath through his nose.

"Most things," he said.

"You weren't told about most things," said Ferra. "Were you?"

"No." He looked at the smooth water. "I was told last night, by the king, after. That there'd been a word at Zale's rail the first night. Most. Not all." He was quiet. "Four people knew it a week before it came."

"He told you."

"After." Ward put his hands behind his back. "I'll take it at the table. Not here."

At the lesson Ward called that evening, Ilenne, over from the K Endeavor with Imani, didn't teach the courtesies. She stood on the aft deck with a basket of fish from the K Endeavor's lines at her feet, the catch of the day, and took them out one at a time and held them up, and said for each one: eat this; eat this; this one, no, never, it is like eating a stone; this one, a little, for the knights only. The heavy ones, the ones she said no to, were dark along the back and pale underneath and dense in the hand, and when Ferra took one to hold, out of curiosity, she felt it hum faintly against her palm, the way the thick water hummed, the way the stones did.

Imani had come across for it. She wrote the heavy ones on the back of the dose card as Ilenne named them, with a drawing beside each, and her face didn't change at all; but Ferra, standing beside her, saw what she wrote under the last one. Ask her what else she's known all week and not thought to say.



On the Thursday night Ilenne came back across in his boat after the lesson, and on his own deck, with the old one lying under the keel, she told him.

It was late. The ring was lit round them, seven ships' worth of riding lights on a sea that didn't move, and the knights on the K Amara's aprons had gone quiet in their bags, and Hesk had gone below. Zale had the ship's one remaining deck lamp turned low on the hatch cover and was mending a glove. Ilenne sat on the deck with her back against the deckhouse and his blue coat round her, which she hadn't given back since Saturday and he hadn't asked for, and she'd been quiet a long time.

"I tell you a thing," she said.

He put the glove down.

"Not all. A piece." She looked at the lamp, not at him. "You know I run."

"I know you ran."

"From a binding. Yes." Her colour moved under the coat's collar and she let it. "A binding is... I tell you how it works. So you know. You will see one, at the door, maybe. Somebody's. You should know what it is when you see." She held up one hand and folded the fingers down as she counted. "In the water. Before witnesses. Both say it. Once." She folded the last finger. "Until one of you dies."

"Once," said Zale.

"Once." Her hand opened again. "There is a man. There is a house. My mother's court wants it. The house wants it very much. So they say: at the door, she binds. And I go out of the door the night before, into the cursed water, where nobody goes, and I don't come back." She looked at him at last. "And then you."

He thought about it for a while. The lamp hissed.

"Did anybody ask you?" he said.

She stared at him as though he'd said it in a language she'd never heard.

"Not did you say yes," said Zale. "I can see you didn't say yes. You're here. I mean before. Did anybody ask you? Your mother, or the court, or him. Did anybody come and sit down in front of you and ask?"

She was silent so long he thought she wasn't going to answer.

"No," she said.

"No."

"It is not... we do not..." She stopped. "No. Nobody asks."

"My mother's got a duchy," said Zale. He picked the glove up again, because it gave his hands something to do. "Emberfell. You've heard her on the set. She had it from her father, and it's a good one, a port and a sulfur works and hills, and when my father married her he married it. I'm her eldest. It's mine when she's done with it. Everybody's always said so." He pulled the needle through. "I said no. I went to sea when I was twenty. She wanted me to have it, and she never once made me, and I still went, because if I'd stayed I'd have been the door."

"The door."

"A family wants a thing. A seat, a port, a bit of ground. You're the way in. Everybody's very kind to you. They're kind to you the way you're kind to a door." He tied off the thread and bit it. "I'd sooner be a man with a boat."

She didn't say anything for a while. Her colour had gone soft and steady, under the collar, and she was looking at him as if she'd never quite looked at him before, or not like this.

"You understand," she said. "You don't... I don't have to make it into Kaldo first, in my head. You understand it before I say it."

"I've been a door."

"Yes." She drew his coat closer. "Yes."

"What's his name?" said Zale.

She went still.

It was the ordinary question. He'd asked it lightly, and he saw at once that there was no light way for her to hear it. Her colour didn't go. It held, and it was holding hard, and the effort of holding it showed at the corners of her mouth. When she spoke it was quietly.

"If I say it here," she said. "On the sea. Then it is here too."

He waited.

"Out here it is not. Out here is only the water, and you, and the old one, and ships." She looked out at the ring of lights. "At home it is. At the door it is. In a week, Sorrel says, it is. But not here. Not yet." She put her face down into the coat's collar. "Let me have here."

He could have asked again. He knew, sitting there, that he should, that a man who meant to stand on those steps beside her in a week had a right to know whose house he'd be standing in front of, and that she'd tell him if he pushed; and he didn't push, because she'd asked him not to in a voice he couldn't refuse. He thought she knew all that too.

"All right," he said.

She didn't look up. Under the keel, a long way down, the old one shifted in its sleep, and the whole ship rose an inch on the swell and settled.

"A week," said Zale.

"A week," said Ilenne, into his coat.
