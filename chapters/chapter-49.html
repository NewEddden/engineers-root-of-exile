Book 2, Chapter 10: Held Messages

Imani came in through the gate at six in the evening in the stern of a red and white boat, with her bag on her knees and a merman she didn't know rowing her, and the first thing she saw when they came under the arch was the king's coat hung over Zale's boom to drip.

It hung there like a man who'd been shot. The skirts were still black with sea from the knee down, and the gold dragon between the shoulders had gone the colour of old brass, and somebody had put a bucket under the hem. She watched it all the way across the water inside the gate and thought about what a wet wool coat that size weighed on a man with two cracked ribs, and decided to say so later, when it would be more use.

The pilot put her alongside Zale's ship as gently as if she'd been eggs. She went up the side by the ladder with the bag on her shoulder. Hesk took it off her at the top without asking and set it on the hatch cover, and she let him.

"She's in the cabin," he said. "She's been in it since four. She came up the stair with the big one, the captain. He's in the waist. He won't sit."

The captain of the twelve was standing in the waist of Zale's ship on his legs, in his long dark coat that shone, with the pale stone gorget at his throat and his bident left in the pilot's boat by somebody's sense, his own or Taurre's. He stood with his feet apart and his weight forward, the way the deckhands stood in a seaway, though the water inside the gate was as flat as a pond, and every man of Zale's crew walked round him the long way. He looked at Imani as she came over the rail, once, from her boots to her bag, and inclined his head the depth you'd give a healer, which Ilenne had taught her on the aft deck was a particular depth. She gave it him back.

Then she went down to the cabin, because she had a patient going somewhere she couldn't follow.



Ilenne was sitting on Zale's sea chest under the stern window with her hands in her lap, in the pearl-and-shell dress, with her hair still damp at the ends. The cabin smelled of tar and Zale. The good coat he hadn't put on that morning was still laid out on the bunk.

"You don't need me," said Imani, before she'd got the door shut.

"No," Ilenne agreed. "You come anyway."

"I'm only saying. Turn to the light."

She turned to the window. Imani put her bag on the bunk beside the coat and took out the card and the pencil and didn't look at the card. She looked at Ilenne.

A year on South Sentinel she'd watched this woman come up grey-white out of the tank in the mornings, a little less grey each month, a little less each week once the dose was right; she'd watched her climb to pearl for a day and slide back for three. She had a whole page of the card for it, in a hand that got smaller as the months went on. And in one day of her own water Ilenne had gone past every line on it. The sheen on her shoulders and her arms was the sheen of the inside of a shell held up to a lamp, and it moved when she breathed. Imani put two fingers under her jaw and turned her head, gently, and looked at the gills: the four slits under each ear, lying shut in the air as they should, and their edges full and soft and dark, not the dry grey lips she'd had in the winter. The eyes were clear. The hands were warm, which they'd never once been in Kaldo.

"Breathe in," she said. "All of it. Hold it. Let it go."

Ilenne did, and smiled at the end of it.

"Now stop holding it," said Imani.

The smile went. "Holding what?"

"Your colour. You've been holding it since I came in. You're as still as a plate. I can't read a plate." She sat back on the bunk. "I've a card here with your colour on it every day for a year, and I'm not putting a guess on it the first night you're home."

Ilenne gave her nothing at all, a plate's face. Then she let a breath go, a different breath from the one Imani had asked for, and the stillness went out of her, and her colour came up into her skin all at once as if a lamp behind it had been turned up: pearl, and bright, and a warmer pearl than Imani had ever seen on her, and under it, coming and going at the throat, a little of that dark blue.

Imani didn't remark on the blue. She knew about the blue. It wasn't hers to write. She wrote the pearl. Then she turned the card to the bottom, where in March, before the wall, she'd ruled one line more than she'd ever needed and written a single word at the start of it and nothing after; and she wrote the date after the word, and the hour, and drew the mark.

Home.

"There," she said. "Now you can hold it."

Ilenne held it. It went back down into her like water into sand, and the plate was back, and only the eyes stayed bright.

"It's hard," she said. "Here. In my own water. Everyone sees. I am gone so long, and I am back, and I am bound, and everyone looks. So I hold. All day I hold." She looked at her hands. "Not hard with you."

"Good. I'm the doctor." Imani put the pencil away. "What does it mean for him? The thing at the door. The hand."

Ilenne's colour didn't move, because she didn't let it. But her hands closed in her lap.

"Water," she said. "A man whose word is water. You know."

"I know what it means. I'm asking what it costs."

"Before witnesses, it is said. So before witnesses, he answers. The same witnesses, the same place. In the season." She lifted one hand and turned it over and back, a short motion, both sides of a coin. "If he answers, good. If he doesn't answer..." She stopped. "Then it is his name. Not a thing a man said once at a door. His name. For ever. Every house says it. Every pilot. His sons."

"Does he know?"

"He knows it is bad. He doesn't know the season is short." Ilenne looked up. "I tell him tonight. Down there. Not here, with the ship listening."

Imani nodded. The ship was listening. She could hear it through the deck over their heads: feet going round the captain the long way, and Hesk saying something to somebody about the lamp, and under it all the faint dry ticking of the set coming on in the deckhouse for the evening post.



The evening post was Linnet Shaw's, and it had been since the run west.

It had started on the second night past the line, when she'd been off the night key with her nose and had nothing to do in the evenings but sit in the deckhouse with the day's traffic in front of her, decoded, in her small fierce hand, and some of the crew had come and stood in the door to ask what Sorrel had said about the weather. By the third night she'd been reading it out. By the end of the first week it was a thing the ship did: at the evening set, whoever was aboard came aft, and Linnet sat on the deckhouse step with the sheets on her knee and read them the day. Hull to hull, mostly. The K Discovery's turns. Ferra's counts. A line from home if there was one, which there wasn't every day. Zale said it was the only time anybody on his ship listened to anything, and Linnet said that was because she was the only one on his ship who could read.

So they were all there. Zale on the hatch cover with his shirt dried stiff on him and his good coat still not on. Ilenne came up behind Imani and sat beside him, and didn't touch him, and the plate held. Hesk at the wheel he didn't need, because nobody was going anywhere. The crew who weren't minding the veins. Maeve, who'd come over from the landing at five and stayed for her supper, sitting on a coil of rope with her knees up. Ferra, who'd come in from the K Amara at four in the watch's boat with a pilot in front and Ward's opinion of it behind, standing at the rail with her book. And Emeric, against the mainmast, in a dry shirt and Zale's second-best coat, which was short in the arms on him.

And the captain of the twelve, in the waist, on his legs, with the long way round him.

Linnet read. She read the K Discovery's noon position and the K Endeavor's sick list, which Imani had written and knew, and hull four's tally of what the refit had used, which made Ferra's mouth go thin. She read a line from Ward to all hulls about the K Amara's watches that was really to Emeric. She read the relay check, ten drums, all talking. Then she turned the sheet, and turned it back, and Imani saw her see what was on the next one and decide.

"Held message from home," said Linnet. "Came in on the four o'clock relay. Saban. Addressed..." She checked it again. "Addressed: To all hands, Zale's ship (no name yet). Not to the captain. To the ship."

The crew liked that. Somebody at the mast laughed.

"Go on," said Zale. "What's he want? He'll want the bill for something."

Linnet read it out. There wasn't much of it. She read it in her flat clear evening-post voice, the one she used for Sorrel's turns and the sick list, and gave it exactly the same weight.

"I was a yard off. Her neck went blue."

For about the length of a breath, nothing happened.

Then the crew went. All of them at once, the way men go who've been carrying a thing for the best part of two years without saying it, because half of them had been on South Sentinel that first summer, in the wash and on the beach and round the tank, and every one of them had seen it or been told it by somebody who had. The man at the mast sat down on the deck. Hesk put his forehead on the wheel. Maeve, who hadn't been there and didn't know what was funny, laughed anyway because everyone else was, and then stopped and looked at Ilenne, and then at Zale, and then started again for a different reason.

And Ilenne, sitting on the hatch cover with her colour held still as a plate, turned her head to the captain in the waist, who was the only one on the deck not laughing and plainly didn't know why anyone was, and told him.

She didn't think about it. Imani saw that she didn't. For a year she'd put everything said near her into the other tongue for whoever didn't have it, Zale into hers and hers into Zale's and both of them into Imani's and back, at the mess table and the tank and the bedside, a hundred times a day; it was what her mouth did when somebody near her hadn't understood. So she said it to him, short, in her own speech, quite kindly. Imani heard the shape of it. A name, Saban's, and a word that was surely yard, and a word for neck, and one more.

Then she heard herself.

It was too late by then. The captain's colour had already moved.

It went over him from the throat down, fast, light and dark and light in a ripple like wind across shallow water, there and gone in less than a second. A flicker. Imani had never seen one before, on anybody; Ilenne had described it on the aft deck, once, as the thing your skin did before you knew what you felt about something, and had said that a highborn man of the twelve would be ashamed to be seen doing it. Then it was gone, and he was still again, still as he'd been all evening, with his colour held level and his eyes on Ilenne.

Then not on Ilenne. On Zale.

He looked at Zale for a long breath, as if he hadn't properly looked at him before. Not hostile. Imani couldn't have said it was hostile. It was the look a man gives a horse he'd taken for a hack when somebody tells him what it won at.

Zale was laughing. He'd gone back against the hatch coaming with his arms folded and his head back, laughing up at the sky, and when he caught the captain looking at him he raised a hand to him, cheerful, the way you would to a man who'd heard a joke at your expense and was too polite to laugh.

"He means me," Zale told the deck at large. "He means I was the last man on the island to notice. Two summers. Every one of you knew and nobody told me." He wiped his eyes. "Saban, you sour old..."

Ilenne didn't laugh. She sat very straight on the hatch cover with her hands in her lap and her colour held still, perfectly still, so still that it cost her something to do it, and Imani could see what. She'd just told a man of the twelve, in her own speech, on the day of her binding, in front of a land crew, that a land witness had seen her neck go blue for this man the first summer. Not this year. Not for a sham, set up in a hurry at a door because a house was coming. Then.

Emeric was looking at the captain. Ferra was looking at Ilenne. Imani looked at Linnet, who was the only other person on the deck who'd seen what Imani had seen, and Linnet looked back at her over the sheet with her mouth shut tight and her face going slowly red from the collar up.

"Next," said Linnet, too loud. "Weather. Sorrel. Wind backing south-west, glass steady."

Ferra kept it until the post was done and the crew had gone forward to their supper and the captain had gone to stand at the ship's side, looking down into the water.

Then she came across to the hatch cover with her book open on her arm and sat down by Ilenne, on the other side from Zale, and turned the book round on her knee.

Imani knew the page. Everyone who'd been in the word room in July knew it. Ilenne's own page, ruled for her on the slab, with the words she'd given them down one side and Ferra's Kaldo down the other, and among them three lines with nothing after them. The first two had been there since July. The third Ferra had ruled on the K Tam's deck in March, in the wall water, the morning the queen's answer came: a date and a place and her mother's, and then nothing. A name in the warning colour, not given. Ferra hadn't once, since March, tried to fill any of them.

She'd filled the third. Imani could see the fresh pencil from where she stood.

She hadn't asked. She'd sat on the K Amara's bridge deck that morning with a glass, four miles off, and watched a merman come up from below at the queen's own door, at the Rim's own gate, where the Rim's pilot who'd been looking back at Emeric all the way in had looked first, before anyone, at the place in the water where he was going to come up. She'd had his name from Emeric on the set at noon. She'd thought about who in a city on a ring of furnaces would want a daughter's binding never to have happened, and whose men brought every ship in and took its iron at the gate, and whose own breakwater it was. She'd written three words after the date and stopped.

The Rim's house.

She held the book where Ilenne could see it, and waited.

Ilenne read the page. Her colour didn't move. Then she nodded, once, small, and looked away across the water at the arch and the red lamp in it.

Ferra closed the book.

"That's all," she said. "I'm not asking anything else."

"I know," said Ilenne. "You never do." And then, after a moment, more quietly: "Thank you."

Zale, on her other side, had stopped laughing some time before. He looked at the closed book on Ferra's knee and then at the side of his wife's face, and he didn't ask either.



They went in at dusk.

The guest rooms were under the Rim, Ilenne said, close under, in the rock of the wall's inner face, and you went down to them from the landing at the top of the steps. The queen's people had been pumping them dry since the day before. Not fast. You never emptied a hard dome fast. Bram and Armand had gone in at four with their bags and a pilot to see it done, and Bram had sent word up by the pilot that the pumps were the best he'd seen and he'd like to take one apart, which Ferra read as a sign that he was happy.

So they went over to the steps in Zale's boat, the six of them and the captain, and up onto the dry landing where Taurre had stood that morning; and from the back of the landing a stair went down into the rock.

It was cut, not built. Black rock, worn smooth in the middle like the steps outside, with lamps set in the wall at a man's head height: glass ones, cold, holding a steady light that didn't flicker, that Imani guessed were stones and Emeric would know. The air was wet and smelled of salt and something mineral, like the inside of a well. They went down forty steps, and the sea's noise outside went away behind them, and the stair turned, and there was a door.

It was a doorway of black stone with a sheet of water standing in it.

Imani had heard Ilenne describe it. She'd thought she'd understood. She stood in front of it now and didn't understand at all. The water stood in the doorway from the lintel to the sill, upright and still, as clear as glass and moving faintly at the surface the way a pond moves under no wind. Lamplight came through it from the other side, wobbling. A stone the size of her two fists sat in the lintel above it, grey, with a fine glyph cut into its face.

A Keeper stood on the near side of it. She knew he was a Keeper because Ilenne said so: a merman of middle age on his legs, in a plain grey coat without any shine to it and a slate hung at his belt on a cord, with a pilot beside him, a younger man, holding a lamp.

The Keeper looked at each of them in turn, and at the captain, and said something to Ilenne. Then he looked at Imani's coat, and at Ferra's.

"He says you carry," said Ilenne. "Under. Both. The iron for shooting." She glanced at Imani apologetically. "He sees how you walk."

Ferra opened her coat without a word. Imani did the same. They each had the revolver on the belt at the left hip where Ward had made them wear it, small and black and plain, and they stood and let the Keeper look. He looked. He didn't touch. He took the slate off his belt and made two marks on it with a stylus, slowly, and said something else.

"He writes it," said Ilenne. "On the body is the terms, he says. Yours. Fine. Nothing else comes in. He asks..." She listened to the rest. "He asks what they are for."

Imani thought about it. There were a number of true answers. She didn't think the Keeper wanted any of them.

"For when the lights go out," she said.

Ilenne put it over. The Keeper stood a moment with his stylus still. Then something went across his face that she didn't know how to read, and he said a short thing back and touched the slate to his chest, and made a third mark beside the two.

"He says it's a good saying," said Ilenne. "He says the old people say it too. Of a lamp. He writes it down." She paused. "He says you are a sensible woman."

"Tell him I'll take it," said Imani, and Ferra beside her made the small noise through her nose that meant she was keeping a straight face.

Then the Keeper said one more thing, not to Imani, to all of them; and when he'd said it he looked at the sheet of water in the doorway, and away again.

"He says a word," said Ilenne. "For when a dry room breaks. All at once." She brought her two hands together in front of her, fast, palm against palm, without a sound. "He says it here. Outside. Once. So you know it. Inside he does not say it. Nobody says it inside." She said the word herself, quietly. It was short and hard, and it sounded like what her hands had done. "If you hear it inside, you go. You don't ask. You go to the bell."

Bram had gone in at four. Imani thought that if anyone had told Bram that, he'd have spent the evening trying to make the Keeper say it again to hear the vowels.

She didn't go in with them. Not yet.

"I'll walk them down," she said to Emeric. "To the first one. I want to see where the set goes."

He nodded. He knew what she meant; she could see him already thinking it. Then he went in, through the sheet of water, sideways, with his shoulder first and his eyes shut, and came out on the far side wet to the skin, she saw through the water, and shook himself like a dog.

Imani went down with Zale and Ilenne and the captain.

The stair went on past the water door. It went down more steeply and narrower, and the lamps were further apart, and the walls began to sweat. Ilenne went first, barefoot, with her hand on the wall. Zale went behind her. The captain came last, behind Imani, and she could hear him not making any noise on the steps.

"Ward," she said into the set, at the turn. "Ward, Imani. Testing."

"Ward." He was clear as a bell. He'd be at the K Amara's board with his feet up on the rail he'd told everybody else not to put their feet on. "You're loud."

"I'm going down. Say something every twenty steps."

"I've nothing to say every twenty steps."

"Count, then."

He counted. She went down, and listened to him counting, and at the next turn of the stair his voice had a hiss under it like frying, and at the turn after that it came in pieces. ...ty-one. Twenty... She kept going. The stair came out at the bottom into a round chamber cut in the rock with a pool in the floor of it, black and very still, with the lamplight lying on the top of it and a stout line going down into it from an iron ring in the rock. The air was cold. The walls were wet to the touch.

"This is the head," said Ilenne. "The stages. Here, down." She pointed into the pool. "One bell. Under. Then the shaft. Then one more. Then more."

"How far between?"

"A breath." Ilenne looked at Zale. "For him, a breath. I hold his hand."

"Ward," said Imani.

The set gave her a long soft rush, like the sea in a shell, and nothing in it.

"Ward. Imani."

Nothing.

She went to the edge of the pool and held the set out over the water, which was stupid and she knew it, and then she went back up the stair. One step. "Ward." Nothing. Two. Three. At the eleventh step up from the pool, at the turn, something came out of the rushing that might have been a word. At the fourteenth, his voice, small and torn: ...said, are you all right, you went...

"I'm all right," she said. "Stay there." And she stood on the fourteenth step and took out her card and her pencil, and rested the card against the wet wall, and wrote it under Ilenne's mark, in her ordinary clinical hand, the way she'd have noted where a pulse could be found on an awkward patient.

Set: fourteen steps above the head of the stages, faint. Nothing at the pool. Below: nothing past the head of the stages.

Then she went back down the fourteen steps to the pool, where the three of them were waiting for her, and the captain was standing apart and politely not looking at the card.

"You can't hear Below," she said to Zale. "Not from the ship and not from here. Nothing. Once you're under, you're gone till you come up."

"I'd sort of guessed," said Zale. "On account of the sea."

"You'd guessed. I've measured." She put the card away. "If you're sick down there, nobody knows till morning."

"Then I'll not be sick." He looked at the pool. He didn't look at it the way she was looking at it. He looked at it the way he looked at a harbour mouth. "Ilenne says there's a bed."

"There are two," said Ilenne, and something in the way she said it made the captain, behind them, shift his weight.

Zale went first, in the end. He took his shirt off and gave it to Ilenne to carry, and sat on the edge of the pool with his feet in the black water, and Imani watched him do what Emeric did, what all of them did who'd eaten the stones: breathe out, and in, long, and then something she couldn't see go through him from the middle outward, a settling, the way a man settles who's about to lift a weight he knows. He'd have done it on the Isle in the cold. Bracing, Emeric called it. Then Ilenne went into the pool beside him with her dress floating up round her and her legs going to tail as the water took her, all at once, so that there was a woman's shape in the black water and then a long pale shimmer, and she put up her hand.

Zale took it.

"See you for breakfast," he said to Imani, and went under.

The captain went after them, without a sound, as if the pool were a door he went through every day of his life, which it was.

Imani stood at the edge with Zale's boots beside her and watched the black water settle. Down there, under it, there was a bell with air in it and her patient in it, and past that more of them, going down to where the light was. She couldn't see any of it. She couldn't hear any of it. She picked up the boots.



The hard rooms, when she came through the sheet of water into them with her eyes shut and her breath held, were warm.

That surprised her more than the water had. She'd expected cold, and damp, and a cave. She came out of the sheet into a small round room, a bell, Ferra said, with its air pumped down a little so it pushed back against the sea and the door; and from the bell through an ordinary wooden door into the dome; and the dome was a room. A long low room, oval, with a floor of black stone laid flat and smooth and the walls going up into a curve that met overhead. The curve was glass.

Not one sheet. Ribs. Stone ribs ran up the walls and over the roof and down the other side, close together, a little more than a hand's span apart, like the ribs of a boat turned over, and between each pair of ribs the glass was set in short panes, each one no bigger than a tray. It was thick. It was greenish, and it had a faint grain in it like fine sand caught in ice, and on the other side of it was the sea. She could see the sea. Dark, and darker going up, and here and there a slow pale shape going past outside that she didn't look at long.

There were pallets along the walls and a table in the middle, and lamps, the cold steady kind, and somebody had put a jug of water on the table and six cups. At the far end, a row of brass handles and a small iron box set into the rock ticked, quietly and steadily, like a clock in another room. The pumps. The ones Bram wanted to take apart. He was sitting on the floor in front of them with his knees up, looking at them. The pilot who'd come in with them sat by the door, and the Keeper sat by the pilot, and neither of them was going anywhere.

Armand was at the table, in his coat, with a book. Maeve was already on a pallet with her boots off and her eyes shut. Ferra was taking her coat off and folding it, and the revolver with it, and putting them under the head of the pallet she'd decided was hers, which was the one by Emeric's.

And Emeric was standing at the side of the room with one hand flat on the glass, between two ribs, and his head on one side.

Imani knew that look. She'd seen it in the K Amara's hold in the winter and on the Challenger's nose in the shed and once, in the night, on the castle stair in Kaldo when the wind was up and he'd put his hand on the wall. It was how he listened to a hull. He'd told her once that he could feel the stone dust in a thing like that, set in it, holding, and what it was holding against; that it was like hearing a rope under load. He stood with his palm on the dome's glass and the sea on the other side of it and listened, and his face did nothing at all.

Then he took his hand away and went and sat down at the table opposite Armand, and poured himself a cup of water, and didn't say what he'd heard.

She noticed that. She noticed that he didn't look at the Keeper. She noticed that he'd put his pallet nearest the bell door, without discussion, and Ferra's beside it, and that he'd taken off his boots but not his belt.

She put Zale's boots by the door, under the Keeper's eye, and went and sat down by him.

"Well?" she said quietly.

"It's good glass." He drank. "It's very good glass."

"That's not what I asked."

"I know." He put the cup down. "It's good glass, and it's doing a lot of work, and they built it to." He looked at her sideways. "Go to sleep, Imani."

She didn't, not for hours. She lay on the pallet the far side of Ferra's and looked up at the ribs going over in the lamplight, and the sea going past behind them, and listened to the pumps tick, and thought about a word the Keeper wouldn't say in here.



Zale came up out of the black water of the last bell on the end of Ilenne's hand and stood on a floor of glass far under the sea, deeper than any hard room went, and the first thing he thought was that he couldn't feel his ears.

The second thing was that it was light.

Not lamplight. They didn't have fire in a bell, she'd said; the air down here would burn it wild. The light came through the walls. The bell was a tall round thing, glass from the floor up, with a heavy iron chain going down from a ring in the middle of its floor through a sealed collar into the dark below, where it was tied to something he didn't care to think about. And outside the glass, all round, the sea glowed. Green and gold and a deep blue at the bottom of it, coming up from far below, from the floor of the caldera, from the grove, Ilenne said, as a field glows at dusk in late summer when the light's behind it. He could see by it. Not well. Enough to see the chain and the floor and her.

And two pallets, laid on the glass in the middle of the floor.

They were laid side by side, with exactly the width of a hand between them. Not together. Not apart. A hand. Somebody had measured it. He stood and dripped on the glass and looked at that hand's width and understood that he'd been told something by a person he'd never met, in the language of bedding, and that it was the queen.

"Ah," said Zale.

Ilenne stood beside him on her legs again, with the pearl dress clinging and the water running off her, and she was holding her colour. He could see her holding it. It was the strangest thing about the deep light: he could see her colour better down here than he ever had on deck. On South Sentinel she'd been grey and pearl and slate and that was all he'd ever been able to make out. Here, in the glow from below, there was more of it, all over her, moving and held and moving, and he could see all of it and read none of it, and it was like standing in front of a page in a hand he couldn't read with everyone else in the room reading it.

"There is someone," she said, "outside."

He looked where she looked.

The bell had a water door. He'd come in through it: a round hole low in the glass with a sheet of the sea standing upright in it, and on the far side of the sheet, in the open water, a woman.

She was in the sea, not on legs, keeping her place outside the door with long slow movements of a dark tail that he could just see below her. Older than Ilenne. Long dark hair bound up. A coat like the guards' coats but with no bident, and her hands folded in front of her in the water, and her face turned to the door. Her colour was the colour of the water behind her and it didn't move. Not held. Just didn't. He'd seen the queen's do that.

"Thalune," said Ilenne quietly. "The court's eyes."

"The court's what?"

"When a daughter of the queen's house binds. Her first season. The court puts one at her door. They draw it from the great houses, by lot. Not her house. Not mine. She tells the court what she sees. Only the court." Ilenne looked at the woman in the water and the woman looked back, through the sheet, and neither of them moved at all. "She sees colour very well, through the water door. She hears nothing. Almost nothing. Words don't come through water from air. So." She turned her back on the door. "Say anything. Don't show anything."

"I'm not much of a one for showing," said Zale. "I'll manage."

He went and sat down on the end of one of the pallets, the left one, and found that his legs had been waiting to do it for some time. He'd been braced since the pool and he could feel what it had cost him now he'd let it go: a pressure all over, and his ears full, and a long slow ache in his chest that wasn't the deep and was just the day. He'd stood in the sea and said a thing he'd never say again, in a language he didn't have, to a woman he... and then a man had come up under him and called him water at the door, and he'd bowed. He'd bowed. He still didn't know where that had come from. He'd thought about his knife and then Emeric's hand had been on his wrist and the next thing he'd been bowing, and he had a feeling Ilenne's father would have hit him for it, and her mother had given him a look after that said much the same.

Ilenne sat down on the other pallet, the right one, a hand's width off, and told him what she'd told Imani. Water. A man whose word is water. Before the same witnesses. In the season. Or it's your name.

"How long's a season?"

"Till the water turns." She thought. "Your summer. Most of."

"And if I go and call him and he says it? In words? In the water?"

"Then he has to show it. Before the court. That your word is broken." She looked at him. "It isn't."

"Not yet," said Zale. "Give me time."

That got her. Just at the edge, a flick at the corner of her mouth, and then she'd put it away. She reached into the neck of the pearl dress and brought out something on a cord.

It was a sliver of stone. Not much bigger than his thumbnail, long and thin and pale, with a faint glassy shine on it, bound at one end with a twist of something dark and hung on a cord of plaited black hair or weed, he couldn't tell which. She held it out to him on her palm.

"From my house," she said. "For the man who binds a daughter. His first season. It is old. My mother's mother's." She didn't look at it, she looked at him. "Shard. It catches a fast thing. By how fast it is. A thing that comes slow, it does nothing. A thing that comes fast..." She made a small motion with her other hand, quick, and then closed it, as you'd close it on a fly. "For the knife you don't see."

Zale looked at it lying in her hand.

He was aware of the woman outside the water door. He was aware that she could see every bit of this as plainly as if they'd been on a stage with the lamps up, and that whatever Ilenne had just said, Thalune had seen her take a thing out of her dress and hold it out to the land man in the old way, palm up, and had known exactly what it was. He was aware that by the time the court sat next, every hall in the city would know it too.

He was aware that Ilenne knew all of that, and had done it anyway, here, tonight, with the door open.

He took it. He put the cord over his head and the sliver lay on his breastbone, cold and then not. He put his hand flat over it.

"I've never owned any jewellery," he said. "My mother'll want to know where I got it."

"Tell her it's from your wife's mother's mother."

"She'll want to know what the wife's mother's mother was like. You'll have to make something up."

Ilenne lay down on her pallet, on her side, facing him, with a hand's width of glass floor between them and Thalune's face in the water door beyond her shoulder, and looked at him.

Zale got up. He took the blanket that had been folded at the foot of the left-hand pallet and he went three yards off across the glass, to the far side of the chain, and lay down on the floor with it over him and his arm under his head. The glass was hard and cold and perfectly level, and the chain went down past his face into the glowing dark.

Nobody had asked him to. He hadn't asked her. It seemed to him that you didn't, with the court's eyes at the door and a hand's width that somebody's mother had measured, and a thing between the two of you that had been said in the water in front of everyone that morning because it had to be, before either of you had said anything else. You didn't, the first night. You took the floor. He'd have taken the floor on his own ship.

He didn't see what happened behind him, because he'd shut his eyes. Ilenne told him, much later. He didn't see that she lay on her side on the pallet with the empty one beside her and looked at his back across the glass for a long time, in the glow, with the chain going down between them into the light; and that at some point after the first hour, when she was more tired than she'd been since South Sentinel, and home, and bound, and safe for one night in her mother's house with her husband on the floor being an idiot, she forgot.

She forgot to hold it. That was all. She'd held it every waking minute for a day. She let her breath go, and her colour went with it, out of her hands, and up at her throat, where she couldn't see it and he couldn't, the blue came up. Deep-water blue, dark as the sea under a hull, steady, and stayed.

Outside the water door, in the open sea, in the glow, Thalune's colour still didn't change at all.

But she watched it until it went.



Imani woke in the hard room to fog.

For a moment she didn't know where she was. The lamps had been turned down and there was a grey warm mist hanging in the air at head height, thick enough that the far end of the dome was gone in it and the ribs went up into nothing, and the air was wet and smelled of hot wool. Then she saw Emeric.

He was sitting at the table, in the fog, with the king's coat across his knees. Somebody had fetched it in from Zale's boom in the night. He had the skirts spread over his lap and one hand laid flat on the wool, and as she watched he moved the hand along a hand's width and held it, and the wool under it steamed.

Not much. A thread of vapour, coming up between his fingers. He held it there a count she could see him keeping, then lifted the hand, felt the cloth with the back of his knuckles, and moved on a hand's width. The coat smoked gently all the way down one skirt behind him, where he'd been. There was a great deal of coat still to go.

"How long have you been doing that?" she said.

"Since about three." He didn't look up. "If I go faster it scorches. Abloh'd know. He'd smell it from Kaldo."

The Keeper was sitting by the door where he'd sat all night. He was looking at the fog. He'd been looking at it for some time, Imani thought, with the expression of a man who keeps a dry room dry for a living, watching a land king fill it with steam, and he'd plainly decided several times to say something and decided several times not to.

Ferra sat up on the next pallet, and looked at the fog, and at Emeric, and at the coat.

"Three hundred and forty hours," she said. "On the back. Not counting the knights'."

"I know."

"I'm only telling you so you go slower."

He went slower.

By the time the pumps had pulled most of the mist out through the vents, which they did with a steady patient sucking that Bram got up to watch, the coat was dry from the hem to the waist, and the light through the glass overhead had gone from black to green to a pale cloudy blue, and somebody had come in through the bell with a covered basket and gone again. Dried fish. Flat cakes of something grey and sweet that Armand said was a seaweed and Maeve said was a lie. Water. Salt in a shell. They ate it at the table with the Keeper watching them eat it.

And Zale came in through the bell door at half past seven with his hair wet and his shirt over his arm and his face the colour of a man who'd slept on glass, and stood in the doorway, and looked at the table, and at the food, and at all of them looking at him.

"Morning," he said.

"Was the floor level?" said Ferra.

He stopped halfway to the table.

"Only Imani says you slept on it," Ferra went on, buttering nothing, because there wasn't any butter, with a knife. "I'd want to know whether it was level. For the report."

"How do you..." Zale looked at Imani. "I said nothing. I've been up the stages ten minutes. I said nothing to anybody."

"You walk like a man who slept on a floor," said Imani. "Sit down. Would you like a cushion? I can prescribe one. I'll write it on your card."

"I don't have a card."

"You've got one now." She moved the fish toward him. "Eat that. You were braced all the way up and you look it."

Zale sat down. He looked at Emeric for help. Emeric was eating a grey cake with great concentration and the coat over his knees, and didn't look up.

"Thank you," said Zale. "Brother."

"It's good," said Emeric, of the cake.

Zale ate. He ate like a man who'd been told to, fast and without tasting it, and after a while his colour came back, the ordinary land colour, the brown of a man who'd been at sea all his life. The cord was round his neck. Imani could see it at his open collar, a plait of something black, and the thing on the end of it was under his shirt where she couldn't. She didn't ask. Ferra didn't either. Imani watched Ferra notice it and not ask, and thought that the two of them were going to be very good at not asking, here.

It was Imani who said it, in the end, and she said it once. She said it to her cup.

"I asked her," she said. "Alone. In the cabin. If she meant it." She turned the cup round. "And then I watched it from the K Endeavor's rail with a glass, outside the ring, with the rest of the convoy."

The table went quiet.

"I found the gap," said Ferra. "And watched it from the K Amara's bridge. Past the markers."

That was all. Neither of them made anything more of it. Ferra went on eating and Imani drank her water. But Zale had put his cake down, and he looked from one of them to the other across the table with a face she hadn't seen on him before, and then he looked at his plate.

"I'd have had you both on the steps," he said. "If it was mine to say."

"It wasn't," said Ferra. "It was a port order. I read it."

"I know." He picked the cake up again and didn't eat it. "I'm only saying."

"That's mine," said Imani.

"I know. I'm borrowing it."

Emeric moved his hand a hand's width along the coat, and the wool steamed. Nobody watched it.

"I'll come up every dusk," said Zale, when he'd finished. He said it to the table generally, and to Emeric in particular. "For the post. My lot are on their own at the gate with no captain and a set nobody but Linnet can work, and Hesk thinks I've been eaten. I'll come up the stages at dusk and sit on my own deck for an hour and hear Linnet read, and then I'll go back down. Every night." He shrugged. "They'll want to see me. And I want to know what Saban says next."

"Every dusk," said Emeric.

"Every dusk."

Imani put her cup down. She thought of the fourteenth step above the pool, and the set in her hand going to rushing at the bottom, and Ward's voice coming back in pieces.

"Then that's the hour," she said, "you'll be where we can hear you."

Zale looked at her. He'd heard what she meant. He didn't take it up.

"Then I'd best be on time," he said.
