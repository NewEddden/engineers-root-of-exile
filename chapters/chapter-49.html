Chapter 49 - The Road First

Emeric had, from the first week, big plans for connecting the baronies, and the shape they took in his head was always the same shape, two iron lines running clean from Kaldo up into the Grayrock hills, a train on them carrying in a day what a wagon train carried in a week. It was the right ambition. It was also, he understood the moment he let himself want it plainly, the wrong first move, and the difference between those two facts was the whole of what separated a man who had built industry before from a man who had only ever ridden its results. A railway was not a beginning. A railway was near the top of a very tall stack, and every man who ever tried to start at the top of that stack had learned, expensively, that the stack does not care what you can imagine. It only cares what you can stand on.

So he did the thing he always did with a want, which was to turn it over and read it backward, need by need, until it stopped being a wish and became a list. For the railway he needed rails and something to pull a train along them. For rails he needed iron by the ton, not the pound, more iron in a single mile of track than his forge had turned out in its whole life to date, and that iron had to come from a foundry running not merely at work but at surplus, after the wall had taken its share and the tools their share and Fletcher's hull its share, because a rail rolled today was a rail stolen from a thing already promised. For the train itself he needed wheels turned true, axles that ran straight, bearings that did not seize, boiler plate that did not split, and none of those could be beaten out by a good smith with a good eye. They needed machine tools, lathes and boring frames that made metal to a measure and not to a feeling, and machine tools needed the foundry again, and hands trained to run them, so that behind every need he found another need standing in front of it with its arms crossed. He walked the list backward the way a man walks a debt backward looking for the first coin, and he kept walking until, near the bottom, he reached the one need that needed nothing he did not already have.

A graded bed. A road, cut and filled and drained, running the line the rails would one day run. It took no iron. It took no foundry and no lathe and no season of waiting on metal. It took men with picks and barrows and the slow patience of moving earth from where it was to where it was wanted, and men were the one thing the absorbed baronies had given him in surplus. The bed was the bottom of the stack. It was also, and this was the part that made him set down his cup and sit forward, the same object as the road. A wagon road graded well enough to carry ox-carts was a railbed graded well enough to carry rail, built once, used twice, useful from the first day it opened and not merely on the distant day the train arrived. He had been picturing the railway as the first thing to build and the road as a lesser substitute for it. He had it exactly inverted. The road was not the poor man's railway. The road was the railway, in the only order the world permitted it to be built.

And there was a truth under that truth, one he made himself say plainly because it embarrassed the ambition and an embarrassed ambition is a safer one to hold. The river already did half the job for free. Grayrock sat upstream, and ore came down the way water comes down, without a locomotive and without a rail and without a single coin of fuel, and it would keep coming down that way whether or not he ever laid a yard of track. The train, when it came, would not replace the river. No sane man replaces a current that runs downhill on its own. What the river could not do was climb. It could not carry a load of tools and grain and glass back up past the falls into the hills, and it could not pass the falls at all, which was the whole reason the foundry stood at the falls and not above them. So the railway was never really about connecting Grayrock to Kaldo. The water connected them already. The railway was about the leg the water refused, the uphill haul and the barrier of the falls, and once he named the enemy correctly the size of the answer shrank to fit it. He did not need to conquer the distance. He needed to beat the grade.

These, then, were the steps, and he laid them out for himself in the order the stack allowed and not the order desire preferred. First the survey, this season, a line walked and staked from the foundry up to Grayrock along the gentlest grade the hills would grant, because a rail hates a slope far more than it hates a mile and a longer easy line beats a shorter steep one every time. Then the bed, cut and filled by the surplus hands, built to carry carts at once and rail eventually, with the bridges and the drains done properly the first time because water left to find its own way through a roadbed will unbuild in a winter what a hundred men built in a summer. In parallel, and this was the quiet keystone the whole thing turned on, the machine tools, coaxed out of the foundry's early output before it ever rolled a rail, because without lathes there was no train no matter how much track he owned, and the tools took the longest to build and the longest to learn. Only then, with the bed waiting and the tools running and the foundry finally past its earlier debts, the rails themselves. And last of all, smallest of all, the least of the problem though it was the whole of the dream, the train.

Until then the baronies would move the way the land already let them move, and he wrote that down too, so no one would mistake the waiting for idleness. Ore down the river to the falls, as it had always come. Freight up the new road by ox and cart the day the first mile of it was graded, slow and plain and entirely sufficient, earning its keep and packing the bed harder with every load, so that the wagons were not a placeholder for the railway but the railway's first apprenticeship, teaching the route its own traffic before the rails ever learned it. He would not wait on the train to start connecting the baronies. He would start connecting them now, badly, with animals and wood and patience, and let the connection improve under him one layer at a time as the stack beneath it rose.

What he did not settle, and told himself he was deferring rather than dodging, was the last question on the list, the one that pulled the train. There were ways. There was the honest heat of a boiler, which any man could feed and any fool could understand, the kind of power that asked nothing of him personally and would run in his absence and after his death, which was the whole standard he held every invention to. There were the other ways, the crystal-fed ways, the ways that were cleaner and quieter and lighter and that also, if he was honest, leaned on things only he and his people fully controlled, which made them brilliant and made them exactly the kind of single thread he had spent years refusing to hang a barony from. The two answers pulled against each other and he did not resolve them that night. He set the question down where he set all the questions he was not ready to be right about, and turned back to the part he was sure of, the road, the road first, the road that needed no one's permission and no one's crystal and would still be there, cut into the hills, long after the argument over what ran along it was won.

He gave the survey order in the morning, and felt the clean satisfaction of a man who has correctly identified the smallest true first step of an enormous thing, and did not think, because it was not the kind of thing his mind was built to think, about how a graded road climbing to Grayrock would look from a hall in Aldany. He saw a railbed. He saw the bottom of a stack that ended in connected baronies and a moving economy and people fed by work. Roderic Aldane, when the word reached him that the exiled prince was now cutting a permanent military-grade road up into the ore country, would see none of those things. He would see a supply line. He would see the one kind of infrastructure that a man builds when he intends, some day, to move more than grain along it, and he would add it to the growing column of things that could not, any longer, be explained as the harmless industry of a harmless man. Emeric built the road because it was the honest first step. It was also, in the ledger the ridge was keeping, one more line of the case against him, laid down by his own hand, in earth this time instead of stone.
