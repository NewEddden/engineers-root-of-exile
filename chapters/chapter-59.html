Book 2, Chapter 20: Below

Rowena came on the field set at six on the Saturday morning, the thirtieth, before anyone in the hard room was properly up, and Emeric took it sitting on the edge of his cot in his shirt with the blanket curtain still drawn behind him.

"You're awake," she said.

"I am now."

"Good. I haven't long." Her voice came small and dry out of the set, with the long hiss of the relays under it, all the way from home. "Harrold says you go down today."

"This morning."

"Then I'll say it before you do." She stopped. He waited. He'd learned to wait for Rowena; she decided in advance how much she'd allow, and then she allowed exactly that, and if you hurried her you got less. "The stewards' audit came in from the north holdings on Thursday. Corbin sat it. Two days, the whole thing, and I was in the room for the second."

"How did it go?"

"He asked one of the bailiffs why his grain book had been written in one hand for eleven weeks and two for the twelfth. The man had a cousin. Corbin didn't raise his voice. He asked it as if he wanted to know." Another pause, shorter. "People listen to him. They didn't, before. They listened to his title and waited for him to stop. Now they listen to him."

Emeric sat with the set in his hand.

"He's changed," said Rowena. "I'm not going to make a speech about it. I wanted you to have it before you went where nobody can tell you anything." The set hissed. "That's all. Go down. Come up."

"Thank you," said Emeric. "For calling."

"I didn't call for thanks," said Rowena, and was gone.

He stayed where he was. The set sat in his lap with its little lamp still lit, and he looked at it, and didn't put it down. Corbin at a table in the north with a bailiff's two hands of ink in front of him, asking as if he wanted to know. Rowena in the room for the second day, choosing to be. He thought about the last time he and Corbin had been in a room together with nothing between them, and about the box, and the four papers, and the stone. He thought about what it had cost Rowena to say he's changed to him of all people, at six in the morning, across the whole of the sea.

The curtain moved. Ferra was sitting up on the other side of it with her knees drawn up and her book shut on them, and she'd heard all of it, and she was looking at him with the set in his lap.

She didn't ask. He could feel along the bond that she didn't need to; she knew exactly what it was to have somebody's mother tell you the thing you'd wanted to hear, about a person you couldn't do anything for. She reached across the gap and turned the set's lamp off with one finger, and took it out of his hands, and put it on the shelf by the door where it lived.



They ate at the table in the outer room, which was the only table, under the ribbed glass ceiling with the green dark of the morning water pressing on it from above. Bread from the Rim, a bowl of the little salt fish, Imani's tea. Bram had his elbows in the middle of everything. Armand had brought a slate and was copying something off it into his notebook between mouthfuls, and Imani took the notebook away from him twice.

"Before I go," said Emeric.

They looked up. Bram didn't put his bread down, but he stopped eating it.

"This is what it costs," said Emeric. "I'm saying it now, before it happens, so it's said." He put his hands flat on the table. "Every day I'm down there, I'm as far from a ride home as I can be. On the surface I could climb. The racks are low but I could get to the foot of the Band from the K Discovery and ride if I had to. From the floor I can't. A ride needs my body up there, and my body'll be under five hundred feet of water in a bell. And if anybody at home is listening to the relays the way we think somebody was, they know we haven't laid one in five weeks. They know the fleet's sitting at the ring. They know where I am."

"Then don't go," said Bram.

"If I don't go," said Emeric, "the queen asked a land king down to her full court with her hand turned up, in front of every house in this city, and he said yes on the dry and then didn't come. Ushar doesn't need to do anything after that. Everything the binding bought Ilenne, it bought because her mother stood up for it. I'd be making her mother a woman who asks and is refused." He looked at the table. "So I'm going."

"I know," said Bram. "I was saying it so it was said."

Imani laughed, once, into her tea.

"Orders," said Emeric. "Three. Ferra and Imani carry. Not on the shelf, not in the bag. On you, every hour you're out of this room, loaded, the way Ward set it." Imani nodded. Ferra had her pencil out already. "Voll's knights on the gate as now, the watch's rules as now. And nothing about the stone changes. Nobody moves it. Nobody talks about moving it. If Ward has to come in, he knows what I said, and he knows it's mine."

"And if they ask," said Ferra.

"Who?"

"Anybody. The watch. The Rim. Taurre's clerk at the pilots' hall, who asks me something every morning to see what I'll say." She didn't look up from the book. "Where the king is."

"At the court," said Emeric. "Where the queen asked him to be."

She wrote it down, small, and he watched her write it, and knew it would be read out of that book some day to somebody who'd want to know exactly what had been said and when.

Armand had stopped copying. He was looking at Emeric across the table with his pen in the air.

"I'm coming," he said.

"I know you are."

"Not because you need a nursemaid," said Armand. "Because they read their law off stones down there, the old grammar, the cut forms, and I'm the only person in this fleet who can read three words of it without a girl beside him to put it into Kaldo. You'll be stood in front of a court that thinks with its walls. Somebody had better be able to read the walls."

"That's what I said last night."

"I'm saying it so it's said," said Armand, with some dignity, and went back to his slate.



The Mark II went on in the inner room, piece by piece, with Imani doing up the back.

It was the cave suit, mostly: the black skin, rubber over wool, the cut-down plate at the chest and shoulders, the gill pack built into the back where the old tanks had been, its smooth grey shell flat against his spine with the slits down both edges. Abloh had taken it in at the waist before they sailed and grumbled about it. The round glass helm sat on the cot waiting, with the valve inside it and the two short hoses lying loose.

Then the coat.

Ferra held it up by the shoulders. It was the king's coat, Abloh's, cut long, and it went on over the suit and hung there over the black rubber and the grey shell of the pack, and he moved his arms in it and it moved with him, more or less.

"You look ridiculous," said Imani.

"The court should see the coat."

"The court will see a man in a wet coat." She tugged it straight at the back where the pack pushed it out. "It'll weigh what you weigh by the second bell."

"How many hours are you going to be steaming it this time?" said Ferra.

"Not you. Me. I'll do it."

"You'll do it badly and Abloh will know," said Ferra, "from home."

Imani had a card in her hand. One of hers, the stiff ones she kept in a tin in her bag, written in her small square doctor's hand. She held it up so he could read it.

Below the stages' second bell: breathe their bells or the gill pack. NEVER a Kaldo flask. Not once. Not for a minute.

"Say why," he said.

"You know why."

"Say it anyway. Armand's listening."

"Because the air you carry down from the top isn't air any more at the bottom." She didn't turn round to Armand; she said it to the coat. "It's the same air squashed small. Five hundred feet down, every breath of it has fifteen times the oxygen it had up here, near enough, and oxygen at that pressure poisons you. It doesn't hurt. You twitch, and then you fit. In the water, in a helm, you fit and you drown." She tapped the card. "Their bells make their air from the sea and their plates hold it under the line. Ilenne showed me the plates. Our pack copies them. A flask doesn't. Bracing's for your bones and your ears. It does nothing about your blood."

"Never a flask," said Armand from the other room.

"Never a flask," said Imani. She held the card out to Emeric, and when he reached for it she turned it over first and put it into the chest pocket of the suit herself, under the coat, face in, and buttoned the pocket down on it.

"There's something on the back."

"There is."

"Do I get to read it?"

"When you're down," said Imani, and picked up the helm, and gave it to him.



Ferra and Imani walked him down to the head of the stages at nine, with Armand and Ilenne and Zale, and a Keeper's pilot who'd come up for them carrying a lamp: down from the guest rooms, out through the water door into the bell beyond it and up its stair to the Rim's own landing, along under the lit arches to the stage head's door, and down the forty steps, steep and narrow and sweating, to the round chamber in the rock with the black pool in its floor.

Emeric stopped on the last step.

The last step was where Imani's set had found its voice, a fortnight ago. One stair further and it was gone. He held the set at his collar and keyed it.

"Linnet. Emeric."

"Linnet." Faint, frying. From Zale's ship, inside the gate. "Go ahead."

"For Ward and the fleet. I'm at the head of the stages. Going down now with Armand, Ilenne and Zale. Last contact." He thought about what else needed saying, and there wasn't much. "Orders as given this morning. Ferra has them. Nothing about the stone changes."

"Last contact," said Linnet. "Orders as given. Nothing about the stone." A pause on the set. "Captain says your coat'll shrink."

"Tell the captain he's standing right next to me."

"I know," said Linnet. "He told me to say it."

Zale, behind him on the stair, had his hand over his mouth.

Emeric let the set go and looked up the stair the way they'd come. He'd felt it on the way down and he looked now to be sure: up past the turn, at the top of the forty steps where the door stood open onto the Rim's landing, a man was sitting on the stone with his back to the wall. A pilot, by his coat. One of the Rim's own, the long grey coat with the shell buttons. He'd been on the landing when they came through, doing nothing, and he'd come to the top of the stair after them, and he was sitting there now with his hands round his knees, where he could see the pool.

And above him, out on the landing in the light, where the pilot couldn't see him without turning round, a second man in a working coat was leaning on the rail with his back to the sea. He wasn't watching the stair. He was watching the pilot.

Emeric held the set up again.

"Linnet. One more. For Ferra's book." He kept his voice level and low. "A Rim pilot followed us to the top of the stage stair and sat down to watch us go in. There's a second man on the landing watching the pilot. I don't know either of them. That's all."

"One pilot watching," said Linnet. "One man watching him. Logged."

He gave the set to Ferra.

She took it and put it on her belt beside the revolver, and looked at him, and then up the stair, once, at the pilot sitting there with his hands round his knees. She kept whatever it was. Imani took the coat's collar in both hands and straightened it, which it didn't need.

"Breathe their air," she said.

"Never a flask."

"Come back up and say it to me again."

He put the helm on. Ferra did the clips at the neck, both of them, and checked them, and checked them again with her thumb, and stepped back. Through the glass the chamber went small and round and close, and the valve breathed for him, and the pack at his back began its faint steady drawing, and he went down the last step into the black pool with Ilenne's hand round his wrist.



It was cold, and then it wasn't, because he braced.

The water closed over the helm and the light went to a smear on the glass, and he felt the sea take hold of him all over at once, gently at first, a big hand closing on something it meant not to crush. He met it from inside. It was the thing the stone had given them all and he'd never had to think about until the wall: he set himself against it, a shoulder to a door someone was leaning on, and held, and his ears stopped hurting and his chest stopped being squeezed, and he was a man in a suit in the dark on the end of a mermaid's hand.

The first bell came out of the black below him as a dim green dome with a lamp in it. Ilenne pulled him up into it through its open floor. Air. He came up into it to the shoulders, and Armand came up beside him on the end of the pilot's hand with his helm streaming, and then Zale, last, on his own, with his strapped side held stiffly and his mouth grey behind the glass.

"One," said Ilenne. She wasn't wearing anything on her face at all. She was hanging in the water at the bell's rim with her hair loose round her and her legs gone to the long dark tail, and her colour was a careful pale green, polite, the colour she'd been wearing near Zale for six days. "Now the shaft. Long. Then more."

"How many more?" said Armand.

"Many." She considered it. "Some of the morning."

The shaft was a wet one, cut down through the caldera wall, wide enough for two to go down it side by side, with a line running down its middle from ring to ring and lamp stones set in the rock every twenty yards. They went down it on the line. Ilenne went first, and then Emeric, and then Armand and the pilot, and Zale last again, because he'd said so and nobody could make him go anywhere but last.

Zale and Ilenne didn't speak to each other. They were careful. At the turn of the shaft where the line went round a buttress Ilenne waited, holding the line, and looked back at Zale until he'd got round it with his arm held in, and when he had she turned and went on, and he said nothing to her and she said nothing to him, and her colour stayed that same pale polite green the whole way down.

Bell. Air, a lamp, a shelf of rock to sit on with the water to the waist, a minute's rest while the pilot looked at the land men's faces through their glass and decided they'd live. Another shaft. Another bell, deeper, smaller, the air in it thicker in his chest even through the helm. He could feel the depth now through the bracing. Not as pain. As weight, everywhere, very evenly, so that his arms moved slower than he told them to and each kick took more out of him than the last. The pack at his back drew and drew.

At the fourth bell Armand sat on the rock shelf with his helm off for a minute, breathing the bell's air in long careful pulls, and said that he was too old for this.

"You look thirty," said Zale.

"I feel every bit of my real age," said Armand, "and some of somebody else's."

"That's the depth."

"Is it," said Armand. "Thank you, Zale. That's a great comfort." He put his helm back on, and Emeric did his clips for him.



Somewhere past the fifth bell, the shaft opened out.

It didn't end. It stopped being a shaft. The rock on one side of them fell away, and there was no wall there any more, only the water going out and out into a dim blue that had light in it, and they were hanging on the line on the inside face of the caldera wall, a long way up from the bottom of something enormous.

Aumere was below him.

He'd seen the domes from the air in April, from three thousand feet, a scatter of silver in the dark water inside the ring. He hadn't known what he was looking at. Now he hung on the line on the end of Ilenne's hand and looked down into it, and it went down the inside of the wall under him in terraces, tier on tier, and out across the floor of the basin as far as the water let him see: domes, hundreds of them, glass so thin it was only a shine on the water, with light inside them, green and gold and a cold clean blue. Walls of kelp that glowed. Moss on the terraces putting out a faint low light like coals. Towers going up off the floor toward him, slim and tall and dark, with lit windows. And between them, everywhere, people. Merfolk, hundreds, swimming between the domes on the open-water streets in long lines; and below them, on the floor itself and on the terraces and on the bridges, walking.

Walking. Bodies upright on the sea floor, five hundred feet down, on their legs, at a stroll. A woman crossing a bridge between two domes with a basket on her hip. Two old men on a terrace in long coats, talking, with their hair lifting round their heads in the water and their feet on the stone.

"They press down," said Ilenne beside him. She'd seen where he was looking. "With the water. On themselves. So they can walk." She showed him with her free hand, palm down, a little push at the water under it. "You swim, you're a fish. You walk, you're people. In a house you walk. That's why bridges."

The water got warmer as they went down. Not much; a softening of it against the suit, here and there, in bands. The vents, Ilenne said, and pointed, and far off across the floor he saw the water shiver above a long dark seam in the rock with the domes clustered thick round it, the way houses cluster round a well.

And high on the caldera wall to his left, above them now, a great cluster of domes and towers built out on a shelf of black rock with its own lights, gold and green, and a long stair of bells going up the rock beside it.

"My mother's house," said Ilenne.

Zale, on the line above Armand, looked up at it once and then at the rock in front of his glass.

They went down the last of the wall on the line, and out across the floor, swimming low, the pilot in front with his lamp. And at the floor's far edge, past the last of the domes, past a long low wall of black stone that ran across the basin like a field wall at home, the water changed.

He saw it from a long way off. It was the only way to see it. Beyond the wall the water was a different colour: not darker, not lighter, deeper somehow, a blue with green moving in it the way light moves on the bottom of a clear river, and it was very still. Nothing swam over the wall. The lines of merfolk on the open-water streets turned aside before they got there, all of them, without seeming to think about it.

"The Breath," said Ilenne. "The grove. Not for guests." She didn't slow. "Not for most of us."

He looked at it for as long as the pilot's line let him. He didn't know what it was. He didn't know why the water above the grove should be that colour, or why the light moved in it with no sun to move it, or what he'd feel if he went over the wall, and he understood, looking at it, that he wasn't going to find out by looking. It wasn't a thing he could take apart. It was just there, at the bottom of the sea, being what it was.

That was when he noticed that he couldn't feel anything.

He'd been feeling less since the third bell, and putting it down to the work. On the surface his sense went out of him as it always did, without his asking, round a room and out through the walls and across the water, a mile and more at the ring: the pilots in their boats, the hulls, the knights on escort one like a row of warm stones, Ferra and Imani along the bond. In the shafts it had shortened. He'd felt Ilenne's hand and the pilot ahead and Zale behind, and the rock, and not much past the rock.

Here, near the Breath, it went a few yards out of him into the water and stopped. Ilenne, beside him, bright and close. The pilot, a little dimmer, a body's length ahead. Armand. Zale, behind. And then nothing. Not dark. Fog. The water was so thick with what fed everything down here that his sense went into it and was simply swallowed, the way a shout is swallowed by snow, and came back to him with nothing on it at all.

He tried. He pushed it out hard, as he'd push it at home to find one man in a crowd. It went a yard further and drowned.

It was the beast again. The hill in a fog, the thing that had come up out of the blue at Zale and been too big for him to feel the edges of. Only now it wasn't a thing. It was everywhere. He was inside it.

Armand had swum up level with him on the line. Emeric turned his head in the helm and found him through the glass, and spoke, so that his voice would carry flat and close through the two helms and the bit of water between them.

"Armand."

"Yes."

"I can't feel anything past about four yards." He said it plainly. He'd have said it the same if he'd been telling Bram a pump was down to half. "It's the water. It's too thick. It's like the beast. I'm deaf down here."

Armand looked at him through two layers of glass.

"All of it?"

"All of it."

"Well," said Armand, after a moment, "you'll have to use your eyes like the rest of us," and swam on.

Emeric went after him. He didn't tell anyone else. He found that his hand on the line had closed harder than it needed to, and he opened it, and kept it open, and didn't look back at the colour over the wall.



The court sat at the floor in a wet hall a quarter of a mile from the grove's wall, and they came to it a little before noon.

Ilenne put her hand on his arm at its door.

"In there, you say," she said, "it's wet." She said it very carefully, and her colour stood still while she said it. "What you say in there, in the water, before them. For ever. Anything. If you say the sky, the sky is for ever. You know this."

"I know it."

"So you say nothing." She looked at him through the glass. "Or say only what's true for always. Careful people, in there, say very little." Her colour moved at last, a quick dark flick at the collarbone, and was gone. "Ushar did his charging up top. In the dry. You see why."

"I see why."

"Good," said Ilenne, and let go of his arm, and they went in.

The hall was full of water and full of people, and everybody in it was walking.

It was long and high and round-roofed, glass ribs on black stone the way the dry hall was, but bigger, much bigger, and the light in it came from lamp stones set in the ribs, white and steady and cold. At the far end, on a step of black rock, the seat. Before the seat, a clear floor of polished stone. Down both sides, the benches, tier on tier, and the lords of the deep houses on them in their long dull-shining coats with their hair lifting round their heads and stones at their throats. Nobody swam. A hundred people in the water and every one of them on their feet or on a bench, pressed down, walking to their places with that strange slow underwater stride, their coats moving round their legs.

Emeric walked. He'd thought he might have to be shown. He didn't. The bracing and the suit and the coat and five hundred feet of sea held him down on the floor of the hall as surely as gravity, and he went down the length of it in Ilenne's wake at the pace of a man walking into a cold river, and felt every one of those hundred faces turn to watch him come, and couldn't feel one of them.

That was the worst of it. Not the cold. In the dry hall he'd sat with his sense open and felt the room move; he'd felt the old families go still before he saw them. Here he walked down the middle of a full court with his eyes and nothing else, and the hall was a wall of faces in white light that told him exactly as much as faces tell anybody, which wasn't much, and past the first bench he couldn't even feel that there were people there. Only see them.

The queen came in last, by the door behind the seat, and walked to it, and sat.

The told sat. He knew that one. It was the third of the courtesies, the one about level and over and under, turned into furniture: those who were telling the court a thing sat on the benches, level with each other; those whom the court was asking, or accusing, stood on the floor below the seat and looked up. Under. He'd been told where to stand. He went to it, on the polished stone below the step, and stood, and Armand stood a little behind him with his hands folded in front of him under his coat like a man at a funeral, and Ilenne stood at his shoulder, half a pace back, where an interpreter stood.

Zale didn't come onto the floor. He'd stopped at the door with the pilot. Emeric didn't look round for him.

The queen opened the sitting. She said a few words, not many, in her own tongue, in a low voice that came to him through the helm flat and strange, the vowels drawn out in the water. Ilenne didn't put them over. They were the words the court opened with, she'd told him, older than anyone's meaning of them.

Then Ressar stood up.

He was on the first bench at the right of the seat with the two other Keepers beside him and his slab of stone across his knees, and he stood up with it held against his chest and walked out onto the floor until he was level with Emeric, a dozen feet off, and turned, so that he was speaking to the strangers and not to the court. The court knew this already. He was saying it for the land men, and he said it without any patience at all, in the voice that had no tune left in it, flat as a slate.

Ilenne put it over a sentence behind, in Kaldo, low, through the water at Emeric's helm.

"He says: this is the floor. Here is how the floor goes." She listened. "The queen opens, the queen closes. Every sitting. Only her."

Ressar's finger went to the stone.

"A Keeper may stop it. For the stones. If a thing is said and the stones have a line on it, a Keeper may call the court to stop and wait, while the Keepers read what the stones hold. Then it goes on. The watch keeps the record. Always the watch."

The finger moved.

"Wet and dry. What is said here, in the water, before the court, is wet. For ever. If it's shown false, the one who said it, his name is water." Ilenne's voice didn't change on it. "What is said in the dry is heard, and weighed, and binds the way your words bind. He says: when wet and dry don't agree, the wet stands. Until it's shown false." She listened again. "He says: that's why careful people say little here."

The finger came to the bottom of the stone and stopped.

"And a custom with no name," said Ilenne. "Nobody speaks to it. Nobody. A custom nobody has named before the court, the court can't hear argued. So it can't judge a thing that rests on it." Ressar said something more, shorter. "He says: that's all. That's the floor."

It wasn't quite all. Emeric could see that from the way Ressar's finger had stopped short of the slab's edge. He'd been in enough rooms with men who knew more than they'd been asked.

"Ask him," he said to Ilenne, low. "Not in the water. You ask him. How a custom gets its name."

Ilenne hesitated. Then she asked it, in her own tongue, a few words, careful, a pupil asking in front of the class a thing she thought might be stupid.

Ressar looked at her a while. Then at Emeric, through the glass. Then he looked down at his stone and put his finger on a line near its foot, very old, worn almost smooth, and said one sentence.

The hall moved. Not much. A few colours along the benches, there and away. Something like a shrug going through people who'd heard a thing all their lives and didn't want to hear it now.

"He says the stones have one line on it," said Ilenne. Her voice had gone very quiet. "Old. He says: a custom is named by one it binds."

Ressar walked back to his bench with his slab against his chest and sat down between the other Keepers and looked at nobody.

The deep lords asked their question.

It was a man on the second tier on the left who stood up to ask it, a heavy grey merman with a coat that shone like oil and three stones at his throat, and he asked it at length, and the hall listened to him the way a hall listens to a man saying what most of it has come to hear said. Ilenne put it over in pieces.

"He says: the land king gave a word. In the dry. About the stone." She listened. "He says a dry word is a land word. Heard and weighed. He says the court has heard it, and weighed it." A pause while the man went on. "He says the court is in the water now. He says the court asks the land king for a wet word. On the stone, that it won't come past the markers. And on the Terms. That the land will keep them." She swallowed. "For ever. Here. Now."

The lord sat down.

The hall waited.

Emeric stood on the polished floor below the seat, with the sea all round him and the coat heavy on his shoulders and his own breath loud inside the glass, and didn't say anything.

He'd decided it in the third bell, on the rock shelf, with the water to his waist and Armand breathing beside him, and he'd decided it the night before too, and in the end it was simple. Anything he said in this water was for ever. He'd known the sea a month. He didn't know what his ships would have to do in it next week, or what the Church would do, or what the court would ask of him after this. He couldn't stand here and promise any of it for ever. And he couldn't answer and not promise, because whatever he said, here, was wet.

So he'd told it to Ilenne on the dry, in the bell, word for word, and asked her to carry it; and now he turned his head to her, once, and she understood, and stepped up level with him and spoke for him into the water, in her own voice, as his interpreter, which was a thing she could say for ever because it was true.

Emeric couldn't understand a word of it. He knew what it said.

He gave his word in your dry hall, before this court. It stands. He won't say in the water anything he can't keep for ever. He has known your sea a month. He can't promise for ever about a sea he has known a month. He has come down, as the queen asked. He stands where she put him. He says nothing more.

Ilenne stopped. She stepped back half a pace, to where an interpreter stood.

And he stood there.

He didn't know how long. Long enough for the hall to understand that that was the answer, and that there wasn't going to be any more of it: a land king in a wet coat standing under the queen's seat in the water with his mouth shut, behind his glass, looking up at her. He felt nothing. He couldn't. He watched instead, and saw colours go along the benches, the heavy lord's first and then others round him, a hard dark red-brown that he didn't know and didn't like the look of; and on the far side of the hall, other colours, cooler, held. And on the Keepers' bench, nothing at all. Ressar sat with his slab on his knees and looked at the land king with no colour on him, and didn't look away.

The queen let the silence stand until it had been seen by everyone. Then she said a few words, and Ilenne didn't put them over, and Emeric knew from the way the hall began to move that the sitting was closed.



The Keepers' bell stood against the side of the court's hall like a barnacle on a hull, a small dome of thick old glass anchored to the rock by three chains, with a lamp at its door and a Keeper and a pilot in the water outside it. They'd pumped it dry for two land men. Ilenne had told him that the Keepers had argued about it for two days.

It was cold. That was the first thing. It was the coldest place he'd been in a long time, a deep wet cold that came up out of the rock floor and in through the soles of his boots, and the lamp on its hook didn't touch it. There were two pallets of woven weed against the curved wall, a water jar, a shelf, a basket of the Rim's bread that somebody had sent down, and no fire, because there was never fire in a bell.

And there were the stones.

They were set into the curve of the wall all the way round, as high as a man could reach: teaching stones, the same as Ressar's slab but smaller, each the size of a big book, dark and smooth and cut all over in lines of the old grammar, the hard flat cuts that Armand had been copying off the dry hall's walls by lamplight since the first sitting. Dozens of them. He'd counted forty before he stopped.

Armand had his helm off before his feet were out of the water. He went round the wall with the lamp held up, slowly, reading, with his lips moving, and the cold didn't seem to touch him at all until he'd gone the whole way round; and then he stopped, and set the lamp down on the shelf, and put both hands under his arms, and said, in a high furious voice, "It's like being kept in a well."

"You wanted to come."

"I wanted to read the walls." He blew on his hands. "I didn't know the walls would be colder than the sea."

Ilenne had come up into the bell with them, to the waist, at the water's edge in the floor, and was sitting on its lip with her tail still in the sea. Ressar was outside. Emeric could see him through the old glass, a dim tall shape on his legs on the rock, talking to the Keeper at the door. He'd come with them from the hall without being asked. Ilenne said it was his duty, while there were strangers at the floor: someone had to tell them what not to touch.

Armand had found one he liked.

He'd gone back to it with the lamp. It was set by itself at the height of his eyes, a long low stone, older than the others by the look of it, its cuts softer, and he stood in front of it with his face close and his finger moving along the lines without touching, the way he'd have read a stone at the tower.

"This one," he said. "Listen. This is theirs. This is the First City." His finger moved. "Here. Raised on the stones. That's the form for a thing lifted and held; I've seen it four times in the dry hall. Raised on the stones, the city of the first. And here's the fall." The finger stopped and came back. "The stones went out. All the stones went out together. Then a count, I think, a number, and then this mark, which I've seen on the dry hall's door, and I think it's the mark for the dead." He turned with the lamp. "It's a record. Of the lifting and the fall. The day it went down. It's the same story Ressar told in the dry hall, written down."

He was pleased with himself. He had every right to be, Emeric thought. Weeks of copying, and he'd read the oldest stone in a Keepers' bell five hundred feet under the sea, aloud, in Kaldo, and got the story.

The water at the bell's floor moved.

Ressar had come in. He came up the bell's floor out of the sea on his legs, slowly, water pouring off his grey coat, with another Keeper behind him, and a third behind her; and he stood at the edge of the water in the lamplight and looked at Armand in front of the long stone with the lamp in his hand. He'd heard. Emeric didn't know how much Kaldo he had. Enough.

He spoke to Ilenne. Not loud. In that flat voice, every word laid down the same.

Ilenne listened. Her colour went very carefully neutral.

"He says," she said, "it's not a record."

Armand turned round.

"He says it's a song," said Ilenne. "A burial song. For the dead of the city. Not the history of it. For singing at the water, when the dead go down." Ressar went on, and she followed him. "He says a record and a song are cut differently. The record is cut flat. The song is cut so the voice can go on it. These here." She pointed, without looking, at the soft worn lines Armand had been reading. "The rise in them. You read them flat."

Ressar said one more thing, and the two Keepers behind him didn't move at all.

Ilenne hesitated.

"Say it," said Armand.

"He says a man who can't tell a record from a song shouldn't read either one aloud."

It was quite quiet in the bell. The water lapped at the floor. The lamp hissed a little on its hook.

Armand stood with the lamp in his hand and looked at Ressar. Nobody at the tower had spoken to him like that in Emeric's hearing, ever. Emeric watched his face, and saw him feel it go in, and saw him decide.

"He's right," said Armand.

Ilenne looked at him.

"Tell him he's right. I read it the way I'd read a boundary stone, and I had no business to. I didn't know there was a difference." He set the lamp down on the shelf, carefully. "Ask him if he'll teach me the difference. How the song cuts go, against the record. I'll come when he says. I'll do as I'm told."

Ilenne put it over.

Ressar heard it out. He looked at Armand, and then at the long stone, and then at Armand again, and when he answered it was short.

"He says yes," said Ilenne. "He says there's a price."

"Of course there is," said Armand.

"Nothing you learn in this bell, you read aloud outside it. Not to anyone. Not in the dry, not in the water. Not to the king." She glanced at Emeric. "And you come every day the court sits. On time. When the light goes white in the hall, you're here. Every day." She listened. "He says if you're late once, he stops."

Armand didn't look at Emeric. Emeric knew what it meant, and so did he: the court would sit as long as it sat. Days. Weeks, maybe. Every one of them here, in this cold, five hundred feet under the sea, with no set, no word from home, nobody to talk to but a pilot at the door who didn't speak Kaldo and a Keeper who'd just told him he couldn't read.

"Tell him yes," said Armand.

"Armand," said Emeric.

"It's my choice," said Armand, without turning round. "Not yours. You can go up and down those stairs every day like a bucket in a well, and good luck to you. I'm going to learn to read." He put his hands back under his arms. "Tell him yes, Ilenne. Every day. On time."

Ilenne told him.

Ressar inclined his head, once, the way the queen did. Then he turned to go; and at the water's edge he stopped, and turned back, and looked past Armand at Emeric, and said something to Ilenne in a lower voice, not meant for the Keepers behind him.

Ilenne's colour moved at the throat. Warm, a little; she held it.

"He says," she said, when he'd gone down into the sea with the others after him, "in the hall. What you didn't say." She looked at the water where they'd gone. "He says it's the first sensible thing a land man has said at the floor."



Ilenne went up the wall to her mother's house at dusk, with Zale. Emeric watched them go through the old glass: two shapes in the dim blue on the end of the pilot's lamp, one swimming easily and one swimming badly with an arm held in, a yard apart, going up.

Then he took the coat off.

It weighed what he weighed, as Imani had said. He hung it on the shelf's end to drip, which it would do all night onto the rock, and got the suit's top half down to the waist, and sat on his pallet in the lamplight with the cold coming up through the weed into him, and Armand on the other pallet already writing in his notebook with his knees drawn up and a blanket over them.

The chest pocket of the suit had kept dry. He unbuttoned it and took out Imani's card.

The front was what she'd shown him. Breathe their bells or the gill pack. NEVER a Kaldo flask. Her square doctor's hand, the capitals pressed hard.

He turned it over.

There wasn't much on the back. It was in the same hand, smaller, near the bottom of the card, written fast, as if she'd done it last of all with the pencil already going back into her bag.
