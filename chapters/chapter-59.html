Chapter 59 - The Iron Road

The ore came down out of Grayrock that spring the way he had wanted it to for two years and had not, until now, been able to have, which is to say in a flood. The cursed shaft ran clean and deep with the dead air moved out of it by a fire and a flue and no mana at all, and the miners who had scraped the last fat off cold veins now cut into rock that had been waiting a thousand years under the bad air, and the ore came down the river and the graded road together in a volume the foundry had never once been fed and had been built, on purpose, hungrier than it could be fed, because Emeric had learned young that you size a works for the day the supply comes good and not the day you turn it on, or you spend the good day tearing down what you built for the lean one. The foundry ate the flood and asked for more, the way it was meant to, and for the first time since he had lit its first fire there was iron in Kaldo past its debts. Past the wall's share and the tools' share and the fittings for Fletcher's hull. Iron left over. Iron he could spend on a thing that fed nothing back for a year and would pay for a century.

He went to see the mill, because the mill was the thing that made the rest of it possible and the mill had not come from nowhere, though a man arriving fresh might have thought it had, might have looked at the great housing of it standing at the end of the works with its paired rolls the size of ale-casks and thought a thing like that must have been bought or dreamed or handed down, and been wrong on all three counts. Emeric knew where every inch of it came from, because he had watched it crawl up out of the near-nothing one generation at a time. It came from the first lathe, the ugly one, the crude off-square thing whose whole short life had been spent making a second lathe truer than itself. That second had made a third, and the third a fourth, and somewhere up that ladder the lathes had stopped being toys that could barely turn a candlestick and become tools that could turn a roll, a true cylinder of hard iron a man's arm long, round to a thousandth and straight as a sightline, because a rail was rolled between two such cylinders and a rail was only ever as true as the rolls that squeezed it, and rolls that were not true made rail that was not true, and rail that was not true was a derailment waiting for a season. The mill was the top of the lathe ladder. It could not have existed a day before the ladder reached it, and the ladder had been climbing in plain sight since the week the foundry gave up its first honest stock, and that was the whole reason he had made them build the ugly first lathe by hand instead of reaching for the shortcut. So that the mill, when it came, would be a thing the barony had grown and not a thing the prince had conjured.

It was not his mill anymore, in any case, and that was the better half of it. Toller met him at the housing with grease to the elbow and an opinion already loaded, because Toller had opinions the way other men had teeth, and had earned the right to them. They had hung the firebox door the way Toller wanted it, a season back, and found the reversed linkage the two others had chased through half a night, and every engine off the bench since had carried those fixes and a dozen more the men had found by arguing with the machines instead of admiring them, and Emeric had let them, because a man who found the loss himself never built it in again, and now he was collecting the interest on that patience. The mill in front of him was not the mill in his drawing. The men had changed it. They had moved the reheating furnace nearer the rolls so the billet lost less heat in the carrying, because a cold billet rolled hard and tore, and they had learned that by tearing a few, and Emeric had drawn it too far off out of a habit of keeping heat away from precision work and the men had ignored the drawing and been right. They had geared the rolls off the same water that drove the blast, through a train of their own working-out, because to run two great hungry things off one wheel you had to choose which ate first when the water ran low, and they had built the choosing into the gearing so no man had to make it in a panic. It was a better mill than he had designed. He said so, plainly, in front of the crew, because a man who took the credit for his workers' improvements got worse workers every year, and a man who named the improvements got a mill that improved itself while he was three baronies away, and Emeric wanted the second thing far more than he wanted to be thought clever.

They rolled the first rail with him standing there, and he let it be their moment and not his. The billet came out of the furnace the color of a sunset a man should not look at directly and went into the rolls, and the rolls took it with a groan that a man felt in his chest before he heard it in his ears, and it came out the far side longer and thinner and beginning, already, to be a rail, and went back through a tighter set, and again, each pass squeezing it nearer the shape, until the thing that had been a glowing brick lay steaming on the bed a rail's length long with the rail's own profile, the wide foot and the thin web and the running head, pressed into it by iron the men had turned true on lathes their own hands had climbed up to. Toller looked at it the way Fletcher had looked at his hull, which was the look of a man who had made a thing with his hands that would outlast the hands, and Emeric kept his mouth shut and let him have it, because the rail was Toller's the way the ship was Fletcher's, grown out of two years of being allowed to be wrong on the way to being right, and a train that a barony understood as its own would be maintained by that barony for a hundred years, and a train the barony understood as the prince's would rust the season the prince died.

There was a thing in the rail that the men did not know yet and that he was not going to let them learn by disaster, and he brought it to them the way he brought everything, plainly, before it was a problem, because a warning after the failure was a confession and a warning before it was a tool. He had them lay a short test line first, a few rail-lengths on good bed near the works, in the cool of a spring morning, and butt the rails hard against one another, end to end, tight as a joiner would fit a door, because that was how a careful man would lay them and how every instinct the crew had would tell them to. Then he made them wait for a hot afternoon, and come back, and look.

The rails had grown. Not much, a thumb's width across the whole short line, but the line had nowhere to put the growth, because a man had fitted it tight end to tight end on a cool morning with no thought that iron in the sun was not the same length as iron in the shade, and the growth had gone the only place it could, which was up and sideways, the rails shouldering against one another and bowing the line out of true in a slow wave a man could see by sighting down it. Emeric let them see it, and let them work out for themselves what a mile of it would do in the first real heat of summer, a mile of tight iron all pushing at once with the strength of the sun behind it, buckling the line into a snake no wheel could hold, and he watched the understanding land on Toller's face and then on the rest. It was, he thought, a very old truth wearing new clothes. Any smith knew iron swelled in the fire and shrank in the quench. Every one of these men had known it their whole lives and used it, shrunk a tire onto a wheel by heating it and letting it clamp cold. Not one of them had ever connected that daily fact to a mile of rail lying in a summer field, because no one in this world had ever laid a mile of rail, and so the thing that was obvious the moment it was said had needed saying, and the saying of it was the whole of his edge, the trick he did over and over, taking a truth they already held in one hand and a truth they already held in the other and setting the two together in a way no one had yet had reason to.

The fix was mundane and teachable and needed no prince, which was the only kind of fix he trusted. You did not fight the growing. You could not; the sun was stronger than any rail. You gave it somewhere to go. A finger's gap between rail and rail at the joint, sized to the season you laid in, wide in the cold months and narrower in the warm, so that the summer growth had room to close into instead of a neighbor to shove against, and the rails ran a hair short of touching through the hottest afternoon and a hair apart through the coldest night and never once had to put their growth anywhere but into air. It made the ride a little less smooth, a small honest knock at every joint that a passenger would learn not to hear, and it made the line safe in a way tight iron never could be, and it was a thing a crew could be taught in a morning and would carry to the next barony and the next, laying gapped rail without a mage or a prince or a drawing, because they understood the why of it and not merely the how. He drew the gap in the dirt, sized for the spring they stood in, and made Toller draw it back, and only when Toller drew it clean, and named aloud why it grew and why the winter gap was wider, did Emeric let it go. A thing a man could not draw was a thing a man did not yet have. A thing a man could draw and explain was a thing that would outlive the man who taught it, which was the entire point of every teachable, replicable, self-sustaining thing he had ever built, and had been since a plague barony first refused to believe him.

So they laid the iron road up the graded bed toward Grayrock, gap by gap, the bed that had carried ox-carts for a year already earning its keep twice, built once and used twice exactly as he had planned it standing over stakes in the ground a lifetime of chapters ago. The line ran the gentlest grade the hills would grant, long and easy where a shorter road would have gone steep, because a rail hated a slope far more than it hated a mile, and the ore that had been coming down by river and cart would come down now by the wagonload behind an engine, in the dry months when the river shut its road and in the wet months both, up the dry country west of the water that no current ever reached, and the barony that had hung its spine on a one-way seasonal river now had a second spine that ran both ways in every weather, and no single dry summer could cut it in half.

He did not say the other thing the road was for, though he saw it as clearly as he saw the grade, saw it the way he saw most systems, whole and cold and a step ahead of the men living inside it. A road that moved ore in volume in every weather moved anything in volume in every weather. It moved grain to a hungry garrison and stone to a rising wall and, if it ever came to it, and the shape of the season past told him it might, it moved men, and what men carried, fast and dry and in numbers, from the heart of his holdings to their edge, in a day where a march had taken a week. He had built it for the ore, and the ore was true and reason enough and the thing he would say if asked. But a man did not need to be told twice which way the wind off the border had been blowing, and Emeric, who read every system he touched a step further than he was comfortable with, had read this one too, and had graded the gentle grade a little wider than the ore alone required, and had said nothing about the width to anyone, and laid his gapped iron toward Grayrock in the spring sun with the small honest knock of it sounding down the line, and let the barony believe, correctly and incompletely, that it was building a road for stone.
