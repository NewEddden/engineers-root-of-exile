Book 2, Chapter 30: Columns

He sat down to breakfast at half past seven on the Saturday because Imani told him to, and because he'd been sitting on the edge of his pallet since four with the stack in his hands and had run out of slips to read again.

The outer room was cold. It always was at that hour, before the Rim's day warmed the rock above it, and the green of the morning water lay on the ribbed glass ceiling like a hand on a lid. Bram had rigged a warm stone under an iron plate at the end of the table in the second week to make toast on, which the Keepers thought a waste of a stone, and the Rim's bread went brown on it slowly in a row of four slices while Bram watched them as if they might run off. Imani's tea. The little salt fish. Armand, up from the Keepers' bell since yesterday and very quiet about it, with his notebook shut beside his plate for once.

Ferra put a slice of toast in front of him. He looked at it.

He'd read Declan's slip eleven times. He knew because he'd counted, round about the seventh, and then gone on counting because it was something to do with his hands. Killed on the north ridge. The line room's hand, plain, the way Ambrose's people wrote everything. He'd thought about the road south, the first week, before Kaldo was anything; six men on horses and Declan at the back talking to his horse the whole way down from the capital, low, steadily, about nothing, about the weather, about the road, as if the horse had asked. Emeric had thought him simple, the first day. On the second he'd heard what Declan was saying to it and realised the man was telling the horse where they were.

He put the cup down.

Nobody at the table said anything. Bram turned a slice of toast over. Armand looked at his plate. Imani reached across and put her hand on his wrist, flat, and kept it there for about the time it took to count a pulse, and then took it away and poured him tea.

"Eat," she said.

He picked up the toast.

Ferra took it out of his hand.

She did it the way she'd done it a hundred times at home, at the long table, when he was reading at breakfast and forgot to eat: lifted it clean out of his fingers without looking at him, bit the corner off, and put it down on her own side of the table, out of his reach, so that he'd have to reach for it and notice he was hungry. It was an old trick. It always worked.

He reached for it.

And the hand that came across the table wasn't his.

It was his. It was on the end of his arm. But from the wrist down it had gone near-black, in close overlapping scale that caught the lamp like wet slate, the fingers longer than they had any business being, and each one ended in a claw the colour of old horn that had come down onto the table between the cups with a small hard tap, the way a dog's nails tap on a kitchen floor. The tap was the worst of it. He heard it before he saw anything.

He hadn't asked for it. He hadn't thought about it. He'd thought about toast.

The hand lay there on the table between the teapot and Bram's plate. Five claws. A little steam came off the scales where the cold air touched them, as if he'd been holding them over a fire.

Nobody said anything.

Bram stopped chewing. Armand very slowly put his fork down. Ferra had the toast in her fingers still, halfway to her mouth, and she sat quite still with it there, and looked at the hand, and then at him, with her eyebrows up, and her face entirely serious.

Imani laughed.

She hadn't meant to. He could see that. It came out of her before she could stop it, a short helpless snort through her nose; and she put the back of her wrist to her mouth to hold it in and it came out anyway, round her wrist, and then she was laughing properly, with her shoulders, bent over the table with her eyes shut, unable to stop, and every time she opened them and saw the hand lying there by the teapot she went off again.

Bram started. Then Armand, which Emeric had never seen, a thin high wheeze like a kettle that took him by surprise and made him put his hand on his chest. Ferra held out longest. She held out until she put the toast down, very carefully, in the middle of his scaled palm, and then she couldn't.

He sat there with a piece of toast in a dragon's hand and his wife wiping her eyes on her sleeve, and felt the morning come open round him like a window.

He ate the toast with the other hand.

The scaled one went back when it wanted to, about half a minute later, from the claws up: the horn going back into nails, the scale softening into skin from the fingertips, the length going out of the fingers. He watched it go. It left his hand warm and his arm aching to the elbow, like an arm that had carried something heavy a long way, and it left him hungry in a way he hadn't been hungry since he was a boy, a bottomless hollow hunger that sat under his ribs and opened.

He ate the rest of the toast. Then Bram's. Then the fish, all of it, and a second bowl that Imani got up and fetched without a word and put in front of him, and then the bread that was meant for the Keepers' runner, and he was still eating when the set on the shelf by the door gave its two short notes and Ferra got up to answer it.

"Line room," she said. "Saban. Harrold's first."



Saban was rationing the line.

He'd done it since the raid, the slips said: every call out of Kaldo to the fleet booked through the line room's switchboard, in order, with a time on it, and nobody on the fibre or the relays for longer than they'd been given, because the relays were how Kaldo talked to its own Answer Towers too and the Church had shown them what a quarter of an hour bought. Ambrose's voice came first, saying the regent for the king, ten minutes; and then the hiss of all that distance, and his father.

"Emeric."

"I've read it," said Emeric. "All of it."

"Then you know." Harrold didn't wait. He'd never waited, on a set; he'd learned the set the way he'd learned the line on a field, as a thing with a cost in time, and he spent it. "The dam's first stage is gone. The plant house and the store under it. The reservoir came down the gorge and was held at the bridge and let out by morning into the old marsh and the mill sluices. Nobody in the lower ward drowned. The port's oil reserve is burned. Osric saved the yard and the pumps and lost what was in the tanks. The dome's cracked and standing. The tree's not touched. Her body's not touched." A pause. "Declan's dead. On the north ridge. He held their war-knight while his men finished the mages. We have it. Alive."

Emeric sat on the bench by the shelf with the set in his hand and his other hand flat on his knee. It was the same list he'd read in the slips, in the same order, and it was worse in his father's voice. Harrold said every item at the same weight. Declan the same as the oil.

"Should I come home?"

"No."

He'd known it would be no. He asked anyway, because he'd lain awake since four with it, and because if he didn't ask it he'd always wonder whether his father would have said yes.

"The regency's sealed," said Harrold. "You sealed it. The raid's over. It was over by dawn on the Wednesday. If the king flies home over a finished fight, the whole city knows its regent couldn't hold it." The set hissed. "I held it."

"I know you did."

"Then stay where you are."

And that was right. He could hear that it was right, the whole shape of it, and he could hear the other thing under it too, the thing his father had put his body over and wasn't going to say a word about on a set the Church might be listening to. Changed at the dome. All of it at once. His father was a dragon. His father had lain across the top of the dome on cracked glass, white and smoking, and couldn't come back for hours, and the voice on the set was the same flat soldier's voice he'd heard across the castle's training yard all his life.

Harrold didn't say either of those things. Emeric heard both.

"Ferra will send you the fuel," he said. "What the relief pair needs to load, and the tower. By relay, after this."

"Good."

"Father."

The set waited.

"Nothing," said Emeric. "Thank you."

"Bring them home," said Harrold, which he'd said to Ward the morning they sailed, and the line went to Ambrose, who said the regent's son for the king, eight minutes.



"I held a river," said Corbin. "It was mostly standing still."

Emeric shut his eyes.

It was a joke. Corbin had made a joke. It was dry and stiff and it had obviously been ready before the call began, and it was the kind of joke a man makes about a thing so he doesn't have to say anything else about it, and Emeric knew that because he'd made a hundred of them himself.

"How are you holding up?" he said.

It was what he always asked. He asked it on every call, sideways, as if it were the weather, and Corbin always answered it as if it were the weather; and then there was the next thing, the one Emeric couldn't ask, the one that sat on his tongue like a stone every single time. Whether Corbin knew. Whether he'd guessed. What he'd say if he were told on a set, a thousand miles off, with Saban rationing the minutes and the Church listening for any word worth a quarter of an hour.

He didn't ask it.

"Tired," said Corbin. "Stiff. My back's still cold, which the hospital says is nothing and I say is my back." A pause. "Doran's been insufferable since."

"Doran?"

"He stood on the bridge all night. I asked him to go back. He didn't. Now he keeps looking at me as if I've done my homework." The set hissed. "He hasn't said a word about it. That's what's insufferable."

Emeric laughed. It came out of him rough. On the other end, Corbin was quiet a moment, as if he hadn't expected it.

"Tess blew the old marsh dyke," said Corbin. "Did they tell you that? At night. With a cart. And then stood at the end of the bridge counting at me till dawn." He stopped. "I've the stewards' returns to do. The worst-number line. I tried it on Harrold." A breath. "He gave me one."

"What was it?"

"Declan."

The line went quiet.

"I'd better go," said Corbin. "Ambrose is making a face at me through the glass."

He went. Emeric sat on the bench with the set in his lap and didn't put it down. Ferra, at the table, had stopped writing. She didn't look at him. She knew why he asked about Corbin on every call, and why he never asked the next thing; she was the only one who did, and she'd never once said so, and she didn't say so now.

He ate a piece of bread. He hadn't noticed picking it up.



His mother was last.

"You've eaten," said Amara, before he'd said anything but her name.

"How can you tell?"

"You're talking with your mouth full." The set hissed. Her voice came out of it level and close, the voice that came out of the golem's chest, that had come out of the shell on the dome. "What's wrong?"

"Nothing's wrong. I've read the slips."

"That isn't what I asked."

"I'm tired," he said. "I've been at the floor for nearly two weeks, deaf, and I came up into all of this at once. I'm all right."

She was quiet. He waited for her to push. She was the one person who could have, and he'd got the word nothing out badly, and she'd heard it.

She didn't push.

"All right," she said.

That was worse. He sat on the bench and felt her let him go, felt her decide to, a thousand miles off, through a hiss on a wire, and put whatever she'd heard away somewhere to keep.

"Your father," said Amara, "lay on top of my dome until gone four, and when I came down to him he looked at me out of a face the size of a cart and said, 'You missed one.'" A pause. "He's been insufferable ever since."

"Corbin says Doran has."

"It's going round," said his mother. "Eat something else."

He ate something else.



He wrote to Declan's family before noon.

It didn't take long, because there wasn't a great deal he could say that was true and wasn't already in the slips. He wrote that Declan had ridden south with him in the first week, when nobody else would have, and talked to his horse the whole way. He wrote that Declan had asked leave properly for the ridge drills and been right about where the mages would stand, and had held the thing that killed him long enough for his knights to finish the work. He wrote that he was sorry he hadn't been there, and that he'd come to their door when he was home. He read it over twice and changed one word and signed it, and Ferra took it for the relay.

Ward called from the Grey Mark at eleven. Not booked through Saban; ship to shore on the fleet's own net, the K Amara's bridge deck to the Rim, short.

"He called me on the eighth," said Ward. "Declan. About running the ridges at night. He had Osric's leave already, and the regent's." The set hissed. "I told him it was right."

Emeric waited. Ward didn't say anything else about it. He never would. That was all of it, and Emeric heard in the flatness of it what it had cost him to say even that much.

"Ferra," said Ward. "Is she there?"

"She's here."

"His pay record. Since April. He was owed for the drills and the spring watch; I signed them and they'll not have gone through with the fleet gone." A pause. "I want it with the letter. All of it."

Ferra had already turned to the page. She said the sum aloud, to the shilling, and Ward said good, and was gone.

He was hungry again at noon. Properly hungry, as if he hadn't eaten at all, though he'd eaten for two since half past seven. He stood at the shelf with a heel of bread and looked at his right hand, his own, ordinary, with Ferra's toast crumbs still in the creases of the knuckles, and flexed it.

One hand. Half a minute. Two breakfasts and hungry by noon.

"That," said Imani, behind him, "is what I've been afraid of."



She did it properly. She'd been waiting to, he understood afterwards; she'd been waiting since the hospital deck six weeks ago, and she'd decided at breakfast, while she was laughing, that today was the day she'd stop waiting.

She sat him on the bench under the lamp with his sleeve rolled to the shoulder and went over the arm from the fingertips up. His pulse at the wrist, then at the elbow, then at the neck; she wrote each one down. The temperature of the hand, which she took with the back of her own and then with the glass thermometer out of her kit, under his tongue, and noted that too. She made him close his fist and open it, ten times, and watched the tendons. She pressed the nail beds and watched the colour come back. She asked him when he'd last eaten before breakfast, and how much he'd eaten since, and he told her, and she looked at him over the notebook.

"All of that," she said.

"All of that."

"Since half seven."

"And Bram's toast."

She wrote it down. She asked him whether it had hurt, and he said no; whether he'd felt it start, and he said he'd heard it before he felt it; whether the ache in the arm was in the muscle or the bone, and he thought about that one and said the muscle, and she pressed the forearm in three places and he flinched at the second.

"It's not free," she said. "You know it's not. You came up off the hospital deck six weeks ago with half the fire in you burned out and your ribs broken, and I said not on this voyage until I've a reason." She closed the notebook on her pencil. "That's the reason. It's coming whether you ask it or not. A hand, at breakfast, over a piece of toast. If it can do that unasked, it'll do worse unasked, somewhere worse than a breakfast table. So we'll find out what it costs. On purpose, where I can see it, before it finds out for us."

"You're lifting it."

"I'm lifting it on my terms." She held up one finger. "Sessions. I say when. Not you, and not because you're bored on a deck." A second finger. "Logged. Everything. What changes, how long it holds, what you eat after, how long till you're hungry, what aches. Ferra keeps the count. I keep the body." A third. "And when I say stop, that's the end of the session, whatever's halfway through coming out of you."

"Yes."

"That was quick."

"You're right," he said.

She looked at him a moment longer with her doctor's face, as if she'd expected more of an argument and was faintly put out not to get one. Then she put the notebook away in her kit and rolled his sleeve down for him and buttoned the cuff, and her fingers rested on his wrist after the button was done, and stayed there.

"You frightened Armand," she said.

"Armand laughed."

"That's what frightened him," said Imani.



Maeve came in on the afternoon delivery boat to see her brother's gills.

She'd found a reason, as she always found one; Sorrel's empties, this time, and a crate of the K Discovery's spare gaskets for hull four that could have gone on any boat that week. She came up Zale's gangway at three with the crate on her hip and handed it to Linnet without breaking stride and went down the ladder, and Emeric, who'd come across to the gate with Ferra to sit with Zale for an hour, heard her boots on the rungs and then nothing, and then her voice from the after cabin's doorway.

"Show me."

Zale was on the bunk with the scuttle shut and his side strapped, greyer than yesterday and pleased with himself about something. He looked at his sister in the doorway with her hands on her hips.

"Show you what?"

"Everybody on the K Discovery's heard," said Maeve. "Sorrel's crew have a song. Show me."

So he showed her. He sat up, slowly, with his hand on the strapping, and Linnet's bucket was by the bunk where it always was now, with its cloth over it; and he took the cloth off and looked at it, and at Maeve, and then he put his whole face in the bucket.

Emeric watched it come. He'd seen the arm, the week before. He hadn't seen this. Under Zale's ears, on both sides of his neck below the jaw, where there'd been nothing but a sailor's weathered skin, four lines opened, one under the other, thin and dark and fringed red inside, and went on opening and closing in the water, slowly, the way a fish's do on a slab. His hands on the bucket's rim went dark between the fingers and the webbing came. The back of his neck, above the collar, went hard and fine-grained and grey.

He stayed in the bucket. He breathed. The water in it moved.

Then he took his face out, dripping, with the gills still open on his neck and closing slowly in the air, and looked at his sister with sea water running off his nose.

Maeve considered him.

"You look ridiculous," she said.

"Thank you."

"You look like a cod that's been told bad news."

"Mother would say," said Zale, wiping his face on the blanket, "that I've always looked like a cod."

"Mother would be right." She came into the cabin and sat on the end of the bunk and leaned close and looked at the gills going shut, one line at a time, under his ear, with frank and total interest, and put out a finger toward them and stopped it an inch off. "Does it feel odd?"

"It feels like breathing."

She sat back. Emeric, on the locker, saw her start to say something else, and then saw her face change, because she'd turned her head and seen him, and Ferra in the doorway behind him with her book; and she'd seen something in both of them that told her the cabin had more in it than her brother's gills.

"What," said Maeve.

Nobody had told her. He understood that as she said it. She'd been on the K Discovery with Sorrel since the fleet came to the Grey Mark, out on the mark and in on the boats, and the stack had come to Linnet's set at the gate on Wednesday and gone to Ferra, and Ferra had held it; and Maeve had heard about her brother's gills from a song, and not about her father or Corbin from anybody.

He told her. He told her the dome and the bridge, plainly, in the order the slips gave them. Harrold changed, all of him, over the dome. Corbin changed on the old bridge, partly, and held the river all night. Both alive. Both back.

Maeve sat on the end of the bunk and didn't move.

"Father," she said. "And Corbin." She said it carefully. "The same night."

"The same night."

She looked at Zale, who'd stopped smiling. She looked at the bucket. Then at Emeric, on the locker, with his hands between his knees.

"Does it hurt?" she said.

He thought about the Band, and the fire going through him, and Sohra's fist, and the hospital deck after.

"The first time," he said. "Yes."

Maeve nodded slowly, as if that were the answer she'd expected, and looked at her own hands in her lap, and didn't ask anything else.

Ferra had opened her book. Emeric saw her turn to the back, past the columns, to the ruled page with its heading. Three names on it now, and the two lines in her smallest hand across them. She looked at the page, and at Maeve's bent head, and at Zale, and wrote one line more, under Harrold's name and Corbin's, and he read it upside down from the locker.

Why all of them. Why this week.

And beside it, after a moment, in the column where she put the word for what she knew:

Blank.



He started drawing that night.

He hadn't meant to. He'd meant to sleep; he'd sat down at the table in the outer room after the others had gone to bed, with the lamp low and the stack beside him, to read Tess's slip once more about the hush mines, and somewhere in the reading he'd got the Shell's drawings out of the flat case under his pallet and spread them on the table, and then he was looking at them, and then he couldn't stop.

It was all there. Every failure of that night, if he looked, went back to a line in his own hand.

The edge. He'd put the Shell's edge on the wall line, because the walls were where a city stopped, and the Church had come in over the dark faces and dropped everything from inside, where the edge had nothing to say about it. The rules. He'd taught it speed: a fast thing was a missile and a slow thing was a bird, and a hull at a walking pace was a cloud, and a hull at a walking pace had come in through a lit face at the north while everyone watched it. One store. He'd put the liquid mana at the dam's foot, because the plant house was where it was used, and one charge in the plant house had sent the whole of it up the gorge to make the water climb.

He didn't blame Tess for any of it. Tess had built what he'd drawn. He'd drawn it wrong.

He took a clean sheet and started again. The edge first: out past the walls, past the farms on the north side and across the river reach, so that nothing came over a wall without having come through a face first. Then the spires to carry it. He tried six and the north and the river were thin, and he tried eight and it closed, and he drew eight, round the edge like the hours on a clock face. Under each spire, a store, cut down into the rock, its own cells, so that no one charge took the city's liquid mana in one. Then the rules. Not speed alone. Mass: anything big that didn't carry a key, at any speed, held at the edge until a man rang the watch to open it by hand. And every note in a crossing read, every one, so that a stranger couldn't ride through on a citizen's coat-tails or in his cart or in a hull's hold.

He was drawing the river edge again when Ferra sat down beside him.

She'd come out of the inner room in her nightdress with a blanket round her shoulders and her feet bare on the cold rock, and sat without a word. She pulled the stack toward her and squared it, and then pulled his first sheet toward her and looked at it.

"It's wrong," he said. "The first one. It was wrong the night I drew it. The edge on the walls. A speed rule. All the store in one place." He put the pencil down. "I'm drawing it again."

"I can see that."

"Count it."

She looked at him.

"Count it," he said. "Before I go on. I don't want to draw eight spires and find we've money for three. Count what it costs and tell me, and I'll draw what we can build."

So she counted it. She did it at the table in the lamplight with the blanket round her, from his sheet and her book and what she carried in her head of the Works' prices and the quarry's and the yard's, eight spires and their footings, the stores and their cells, the cut feeds, Armand's students' hours to grow the faces, Tess's crews; and he sat beside her and didn't draw while she did it, because she'd asked him once, years ago, never to draw while she was counting what he'd drawn. It took a quarter of an hour. She said the sum.

He sat with it.

"That's most of the summer's surplus," he said.

"It's all of it. And some of the autumn's." She put her pencil down on the sheet. "And it's right."

He picked up his own pencil and drew the eighth spire.



The watch's record room was at the back of the watch house on the Rim's inner face, a long dry room cut into the rock behind the pilots' hall, with slate racks from floor to ceiling and one table down the middle of it under three lamps; and Ferra had the table from eight on the Sunday morning, with Taurre at the other end of it.

He'd given it to her without being asked. That was the first thing she'd noticed about Taurre, weeks ago at the Grey Mark: that he gave you what you were going to need before you'd worked out how to ask for it, and then sat and watched what you did with it. He sat at the far end now, tall and grey on his legs in the watch's long dark coat with the pale stone at his throat, with his slate and his stylus and a jug of the hot green drink, and watched her rule the sheet.

One long sheet. She'd glued four together the night before. Down the left side, the attempts, one to a line, in the order they'd happened. Across the top, the columns: what the piece was; who held it now; and the word. Seen, measured, remembered, guessed, blank. She'd put the five words at the head of the last column in Kaldo and Taurre's officer had written them under hers in the Rim's script, and Taurre had looked at the two rows of words for some time and then nodded once and written something on his slate that he didn't show her.

She started with what she had.

The sliver in Zale's harness, under the back strap. Dagny's find. The piece: a drain sliver, the old families' yard mark on its face. Who held it: the watch, in its cloth. The word: seen, for the sliver and the mark. The old cutter's sale of marked blanks to Maudrec: remembered, the cutter's own word, on the watch's slate. The three bars Maudrec paid him with, Kaldo's own, from the pilot's fee: measured, every one, against the weights in her book from the first week. Tess's match of the stones' cleave to the bident tip from South Sentinel: seen, and Tess's line beside it in her own words, sent by relay, which Ferra copied exactly. Same hand. Years apart.

And then the hand that had put the sliver under the strap, in the queen's house, in the bell, where Zale's suit hung. She looked at that line for some time.

Blank.

The poison. The dark oily fish in Zale's second dish that wasn't on Imani's card. Imani's card itself, which Ferra had in her own book: Ate from all six at 7.50. Z's two dishes held at the head, uncarded. The house sent her. The dishes, which the watch held. Sarrou, at the station, who had eaten from every dish and nearly died at the second bell. Seen for the dishes. For who had sent the fish and why, she left the word out and wrote, waiting, which wasn't one of the five; and Taurre, at the end of the table, saw her do it and let it stand.

The market. Orsaule, a knife for hire, dead at twenty past three on Imani's watch. His knife, turned through Zale's sleeve by the charm. Seen. Who had hired him: blank.

The shot. And here she had more than she'd expected, because at ten Lusse spoke.

He'd been at the station since the second, drained and grey on a pallet with the watch at his door, and he hadn't been able to hold a colour or make half his own words. Orrane had sent to say he could, now. The watch brought him to the record room's door on his legs, walking slowly between two officers, and he stood there in the lamplight with his colour back in him at last, a thin dull green that went darker at his throat when he looked at the sheet on the table and the land woman behind it.

He didn't sit. The told sat. He stood.

He spoke to Taurre, not to her, and Taurre's officer put it into Kaldo at her elbow a sentence behind, and she wrote.

He'd carried for the house since the winter. Letters. Small things. In May, a long box, from a salvager's shed on the salvage quay, to Maudrec's yard, at night. He hadn't opened it. He'd known what was in it by its weight and its length and by what the whole Rim had been saying about the land's lost gun since the hazard board had posted it. He'd carried it anyway. He'd been paid. And he'd gone out to the land king's ship at the Grey Mark with a slate after that, on Maudrec's word, and lain in its storm covers for six days writing down its watches; and when the land knights had found him and he'd gone over the rail, he'd swum back to the Rim and gone to Maudrec's, and Maudrec had put a cuff on his wrist and tied him under the quay.

He said that part without any colour at all. Ferra looked up from the sheet. Lusse was looking at the floor.

"Which salvager's shed?" she said.

The officer put it over. Lusse told him.

The watch went for Oddrin at eleven, and Oddrin came at noon, of his own accord, before they reached his shed, with his tally under his arm.

He was short and wide and bearded and had the salvager's way of standing with his feet apart as if the floor might move, and he put the tally on the table in front of Taurre, a long board with notches cut down one edge and marks scratched beside them, and stood back from it. He didn't look at Ferra at all. He spoke to Taurre for some minutes, low, and once he stopped and put his hand flat on the board, and Ferra saw that his hand wasn't steady.

"He says," said the officer, "the land's gun was on the hazard board. Posted. Lost in the sea, a box, with a position. He says he went out with his animal on the seventh day of the month after the posting, to the position. He says the animal found it on the second dive." The officer listened. "He says he raised it. He says he meant to report the raising. He says Maudrec came to his shed that night, before he had. With iron." A pause. "He says he took the iron. He says it is on the board."

Ferra leaned forward and looked at the tally. A notch, and beside it a mark she didn't know, and the officer put his finger on it: raised. The next notch: sold, and a scratch that was a sum. A third notch, small, at the bottom of the run, with nothing beside it.

"What's that one?" she said.

Oddrin bent over it when the officer asked him, and was slow to answer.

"He says it's for the report," said the officer. "That he didn't make."

She wrote it. The rifle raised from the logged position by a Rim salvager with an animal: remembered, his word, and the tally beside it, seen. Sold to Maudrec for the house, for iron: remembered and seen. The rifle itself, recovered from Pirro's hands on the kelp racks, with its serial and its eight in the box and the fresh marks on its pins: seen, measured, every one. Pirro's roof, the casing in Kaldo brass with the Works' lot stamp, the heat still in the stone: seen, Mira's, and measured, the two hours. Pirro himself, a mage of the Rim of no house anybody would name: seen, dead.

Oddrin went at one. At the door he stopped and said something else to Taurre, and Taurre answered him shortly, and he went.

"He asks what happens to his licence," said the officer.

"What did Taurre say?"

"That the court will say."



Bram came at two with the casings.

The watch's divers had brought them up out of the queen's house on the Saturday, from the floor of the broken bell where the second charge had taken the shell: four pieces of thick grey cast iron, curved, the biggest the size of Ferra's hand, still with the sea's grit in their seams. Taurre had let Bram have them on the record, under the watch's eye, and Bram had taken them to hull four's workshop and sat up with them half the night, and come to the record room now with the four pieces in a tray and the furnace's casting books under his arm, the ones Ferra had carried out of the bench's office in their case on the furnace night with the yard burning behind her.

He put the tray on the table and opened the third book at a page he'd marked with a strip of rag.

"Look," he said.

She looked. A pour, on a date three weeks before the fire, in a clerk's tidy hand in the Rim's script, and a word the officer read for her: sinkers. Net weights. Forty of them, out of one mould, a number cut beside the entry, and a note in a different hand underneath that the officer frowned at and read twice.

"Mould cracked," he said. "Use it once more and break it."

Bram turned the biggest of the four pieces over in the tray. On its inside face, under the grit, a thin raised vein of iron ran across it from edge to edge, crooked, like a hair on a plate.

"That's a crack in a mould," said Bram. "When a sand mould cracks, the iron runs into the crack and sets there, and the casting comes out with a vein on it where the crack was. Every one out of that mould has the same vein in the same place." He put his finger on it. "That one. On all four pieces, where it's on the piece at all. Same line." He tapped the book. "Forty sinkers out of a cracked mould, three weeks before the fire, in the house's own furnace. Only they weren't sinkers. A sinker doesn't need a wall that thick. A sinker doesn't need a fuse hole." He turned the second piece and showed her the edge of one, a neat round half of a hole, bored. "They cast the charges for the bell in their own yard and wrote them down as net weights."

"Seen," said Ferra.

"Seen. And measured. The wall's the same thickness on all four to a hair, and I've got the vein's line on paper against each piece." He put a sheet down, his own careful drawing, four outlines and a crooked line through each. "I'll swear to it."

Taurre had come down the table. He stood at Bram's shoulder and looked at the vein, and at the book, and at the drawing, and then he reached past Bram and turned the biggest piece over himself with one heavy finger, and looked at the vein again; and his colour, which Ferra had never yet seen move, went once from grey to a darker grey at the throat and back.

He said something to his officer.

"He asks," said the officer, "whether the land engineer will teach a watch officer to read a mould's vein."

"Yes," said Bram, at once, and then looked at Ferra as if she might say otherwise, and she didn't.

She wrote the rest of the furnace night while he was still bent over the tray. The cascade rings in the burst fire stones, bank into bank down the yard: seen, Bram's, every ring. The shutter's catch in its block of the seal's stone: seen. The shutter's drop.

She stopped at that line, because she knew what was in the box already, and Bram knew she knew, and was watching her.

His drawing of the chamber, made on the Wednesday before the fire, with the five words at the top in his hand. The hatch, seen. The stair, measured. The catch, seen. The winch, seen. And beside the shutter's drop: guessed. And in the margin beside that, in his hand from the Sunday after, Imani's line: heard it drop, from above the turn; came round and found it down.

Everyone knew how the shutter fell now. The queen's guard captain's men had wound it up again and it had dropped exactly as Bram had drawn it, and the sea had come up to it and stopped. If she wrote seen nobody on the Rim would argue.

"Guessed," she said, and put it in the column.

Bram let his breath out through his nose.

"It's what you knew when you drew it," said Ferra, without looking up. "The drawing's the piece. Not the shutter."

"I know that."

"Then don't look at me like that."

The rest went quickly. Keddrin's hip, Imani's two rounds, and his word at first light that Maudrec had paid him: remembered. Maudrec's word by ten that he'd taken his orders from Ushar: remembered, on the watch's slate, which Taurre read to her himself through the officer, every word. The men in the bell, two dead and the rest fled to report a beast: Thalune's report, remembered. The watch's own times, the zone logged at the watch house by the yard's lamps going out and the land's ship first heard a quarter of an hour after: measured.

By five she had it. One sheet, in columns, from the sliver to the bell. Most of the last column read seen or measured. Four lines read remembered, from people who'd been there. Three read blank. One read guessed, and would always read guessed, because that was the truth of it.

Taurre sat at the far end of the table and looked at it for a long time without touching it. Then he spoke, and the officer put it over.

"He says it's the watch's record," said the officer. "Yours is the copy."

"Yes," said Ferra. "That's right."



Sarrou was sitting up when they got to the station, which Imani said she hadn't been the day before.

The station's back room was quieter than it had been. The burned furnace hands had gone home, most of them, on the Saturday, and the pallets along the wall were empty but for two, and Sarrou's at the end under the high window, where Orrane had put her so she'd have the light. She was the house's taster. Dark green, with the pale amber colour Ferra had seen go out of her on the Thursday of the poison, when she'd gone grey from the gut outward and Imani had held her heart going with her two hands and her head in the sea. It was back now, faintly. She was thin. She looked up when Imani came in, and then past her at Ferra in the doorway with her book, and her colour went down again a little, the way a lamp does when a door opens.

Imani sat on the edge of the pallet. Ferra stayed in the doorway. She'd been asked to come; she'd asked herself whether she should, all the way down the stair, and decided that if Sarrou decided to speak, somebody would have to write it down who could read it back in the water, and Imani couldn't do both.

Imani didn't ask her anything. She took Sarrou's wrist and counted it, and looked at her eyes, and pressed her belly in two places with her flat fingers and watched her face. Then she sat back.

"Better," she said, in Kaldo, slowly, and then the one word of the Rim's she'd learned for it.

Sarrou looked at her hands.

"I don't know if I say," she'd said, on the Thursday night, in her servant's Kaldo. Ferra had Imani's card for it, two words under: Don't push. Imani hadn't pushed. She'd come every day since and counted the pulse and pressed the belly and gone.

Sarrou spoke now without being asked.

"They send me," she said. "The house. To taste." She said it to Imani's hands. "They know what is in. The fish." She stopped and her colour went very dark at the throat, and then she made it come back, which Ferra could see her do; it came up out of the dark slowly, like something being lifted. "I am the house's. Since a child. I taste for them. I taste for the guests. They send me down to taste what they put in, so it is tasted. So nobody says it was not."

Imani didn't move.

"I say it," said Sarrou. "In the water. Before the court. Standing." She looked up at Imani at last. "Against them."

Imani held her wrist a moment longer. Then she put it down on the blanket, very gently, and stood up.

"Tell the watch," she said to Ferra, without turning round.

Ferra had already written it. She went out to the watch officer on the station's step and read it to him, word for word, and he cut it on his slate. Then she went back to the record room and found the line that said waiting beside the poison, and drew one neat stroke through the word, and wrote beside it, small: Sarrou will say it, wet, standing. Sun 14 Jun.



The watch published on the Monday at nine, on the hazard board at the pilots' hall, where it had published the land's lost rifle in May.

Ferra went to see it go up. So did half the Rim. They stood in the long open front of the pilots' hall in the morning light with their colours held, on their legs, pilots and boatmen and porters and the old families' people up from the shallows, and two watch officers nailed the board to the post where the hazards went, and stood back, and one of them read it aloud for those at the back, in the water of the long trough along the hall's front, so that it was said wet.

Ferra had the Kaldo of it from Taurre's officer before it went up. She stood at the back of the crowd and read along.

The rifle: declared lost by the land in the watch's own hazard log on the sixth day of the month of May, with its position and its mark, and posted on this board. Raised from that position by a salvager of the Rim, who did not report the raising. Bought from him for the Rim's house. Fired on the breakwater at dusk on the fifth day of June by a mage of the Rim, who is dead. The dead zone over the big kiln yard: a cascade of fire stones laid on purpose in the furnaces of the Rim's house's own yard, bank into bank. Logged at the watch house a quarter of an hour before the land's ship could be heard.

It said the shot at the husband of the queen's daughter. It didn't say whether he'd lived.

Ferra watched the crowd read it. She'd learned by now to watch the throats, not the faces. A man near her, a pilot by his coat, went slate at the line about the salvager and stayed slate. Two old women of the shallows beside him went a slow warm colour she'd seen only once before, at the court, when the old families had voted, and looked at each other. Somebody at the front said something sharp in the water and somebody else answered, and the watch officer at the trough said one word, and they stopped.

By noon it was everywhere. She heard it in the market going back, in the Rim's own words, the pieces she knew: the land's gun, and the house, and a salvager, and the kiln yard. She heard it in the porters' voices on the guests' stair. The house's line, that Sevrin had spoken from Ushar's empty seat at the floor for a week, that the land's gun had shot the queen's daughter's husband and the land's stone had made the zone, was dead. Nobody on the Rim said it after that morning. Nobody had to stop them. It simply wasn't a thing that could be said any more, in front of that board.

She stood on the guests' stair at noon with her book open on her arm and looked at the page where she kept the rifle.

The entry from the morning after the first dragon: lost with its case, 05.41, Sun 3 May; box full, ten. The declaration under it, three days later, in her hand: declared to the watch's hazard log, Wed 6 May, with position and mark. She'd made it because that was what you did. You declared a hazard in a host's water. You wrote down what you'd lost and where. And somebody had read the board, and gone out with an animal to the position she'd written, and raised it, and sold it to a man who had wanted to kill Zale with something that would look like Kaldo's.

That was the leak. Her own honest line.

And this morning the same line was the proof. The only proof. Nothing else on that board would have stood without it.

She wrote both in the margin, small, one above the other. How they found it. How it was proved. She read them twice. Then she closed the book on her pencil without drawing a line between them, and went down the stair.



Ilenne came up to the hard rooms at ten that night, alone, through the sheet of water at the door with her eyes shut, and stood on the rock inside it on her legs with the sea running off her.

Ferra had asked her to come. She'd sent the slip down by the queen's runner at six: When you can. Alone. F. She hadn't been sure Ilenne would. They hadn't been alone together since the furnace night, and before that only on the K Endeavor, over the word book, in the first weeks.

The others had gone through to the Keepers' hall. Emeric had taken them without being asked, Bram and Imani and Armand, and said they'd be an hour, and shut the tunnel door behind him; and Ferra had put the lamp in the middle of the table and the sheet beside it, folded, and a cup of the hot green drink at the other place, and sat.

Ilenne sat. She held the cup and didn't drink it. Her colour was nothing at all; it had been nothing at all at the gate steps on the furnace night, Imani had said, and Ferra didn't think it had come back since, not in front of anyone.

"You saw the board," said Ferra.

"I see it."

"Then you know the house's line is dead. The gun. The zone." Ferra kept her voice level. It was the accounts voice. She'd thought about using another and decided she didn't have one that would do this better. "But the board can't say why. Nothing on it can. Every attempt on that sheet was on the husband. The sliver, the fish, the knife, the shot, the bell. And the only reason in your water to kill a bound heir's husband is the one nobody says."

Ilenne's hands were still on the cup.

"The court can judge a fire," said Ferra. "And a poison, and a shot. It can't judge what they were for. Not while nobody's named it." She paused. "And it's the throne, after. Your mother's held the seat as regent since your father died, and the court gave her till your binding. The binding's made. They'll put the question."

"I know they put it."

"Then here's how it reads," said Ferra. "Like a contract they've already signed. They want a man on the throne, by custom, and custom gives two roads. Your husband rules. Or you take the change and rule as one." She laid the three out on the table with her finger, three places on the wood, the way she'd lay out three bids. "So. They can have your husband. A land man of the cursed shore, the old king's son, with Randillia behind him; who doesn't want it, and who they can't stomach. Or they can have the change. Which you've refused once, when they asked, and will refuse again in front of them, married. Or they can have you." She took her finger off the wood. "Those are their terms. All of them. Laid out by their own custom. They pick."

Ilenne looked at the three places on the table where Ferra's finger had been.

She was quiet a while. The lamp hissed. Through the glass ceiling the black water lay on the room, and somewhere above it, a long way up, at the gate, there was a ship with a man on it whom the whole court thought the sea had taken, and whom she hadn't seen since the furnace night, by her own order.

"Him," said Ilenne at last. "Or the change. Or me."

"Yes."

"They hear him. The first." Her voice was quite steady. "They hear a widow. Bitter."

"Yes," said Ferra. "They'll hear that."

"And it is true. Every word."

"Every word."

Ilenne put the cup down. She put her hand out, flat, palm down, on the folded sheet beside the lamp, and held it there, and Ferra saw her colour move, for the first time that night: not much, a thin cold light at the throat that came and went.

"This," said Ilenne. "The sheet."

"I'll make you a copy. In your script. Taurre's officer can..."

"No." Ilenne didn't take her hand off it. "Not a copy. This. In the order you write it." She looked up. "I say it myself. All. Your order. I say it in the water, with my mouth, and it is mine."

Ferra looked at the hand on her sheet. Four pieces glued together the night before, ruled in her own hand, every line of it, with Bram's vein and Oddrin's notch and Sarrou's stroke through waiting; the best thing she'd made at Aumere, and the only one of its kind, and the watch's record room had the original and this was hers.

She took her own hand off the table.

"It's yours," she said.
