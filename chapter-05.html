The room was cold again by the time he sat down. It had a chill the same way it had been every night since he'd arrived. Whatever the steward's kitchen had sent up sat mostly untouched at his elbow, going cold faster than he was eating it. He didn't notice. He had a quill, ink, a blank page, and a day's worth of unsorted input finally ready to become something with a shape. The ink and paper were terrible, but he had no choice but to use them.

He started a list and worked his way from the top to the bottom. This reminded him of the way he'd have worked through a punch list on a job site, root causes first, symptoms sorted underneath them instead of listed as their own separate problems.

The water was the one that mattered most and the one he already had a plan for. Waterborne, almost certainly bacterial, and it didn't take a villain to explain it, just a burial ground sitting upriver of the wells and latrines dug too close to the bank by people who'd never had a reason to map the two against each other. Active, ongoing, killing people right now. Everything else on the page could wait longer than that one could.

Under it, not a problem so much as a permanent condition he'd have to build around: the town had lost more than half its people to war and then plague, two separate failures stacked on top of each other, not one. Whatever he did next, he'd be doing it with fewer hands than the job actually needed, and no amount of clever engineering made that number go back up.

Wexler went on the page too, and he was careful with the wording, even writing it only for himself. Disengaged, at minimum. The man hadn't ridden out to greet him, had corrected him out of spite rather than courtesy, hadn't shown a flicker of interest in his own barony's condition in the one real conversation they'd had. That was consistent with simple apathy. It was also consistent with something worse, and he didn't have evidence for worse yet, so he wrote down apathy and made himself leave it there. Acting on a theory he hadn't earned was how competent people became the story's next cautionary tale.

The fallow fields went down as an open question, not a conclusion. Could be the labor shortage explaining itself. Could be something else entirely. Not enough data either way, and it would keep until the water problem stopped eating every free hour he had. The idle mill sat right next to it on the page for the same reason: noticed, not understood, maybe connected to the fields and maybe not, and he was careful not to assume a shared cause just because two things happened to be broken in the same town.

Last was the castle itself: rope where a chain should be, wall damage hidden rather than fixed, a steward doing the work of three people. He almost didn't write it down at all. It wasn't a cause of anything; it was what every other item on the page looked like from the outside, and fixing the causes above it would probably fix this without him lifting a finger toward it directly. The ledgers could help with understanding the castle's issues, but he doubted he could get it from the baron without a fuss.

He read back over what he had. Six problems, and only one of them had a next step he could actually take tomorrow. That was fine. That was how it always started.

The day came. Emeric finally felt like he could do something worth doing. He'd sent the maids to the tainted stretch of the river instead of upstream, no filtering, no detour. They hated it; hauling contaminated water back to a prince's washroom felt backward to them, wrong in a way that had nothing to do with the walk. But he was still a prince, and telling them it would make sense later got them moving anyway. Emeric was already missing plumbing. Showering in this barony was inefficient enough without turning the water source into an experiment.

Emeric, accompanied by Rylan and shadowed by Ward, went straight to Bram. Bram was already waiting by the cart when Emeric came down, arms crossed, looking like a man who'd rather be at his forge.

"You said the parish house," Bram said. "You didn't say why."

"Records." Emeric climbed up. "Every death this town's logged, going back as far as they keep them."

Rylan fell in beside the cart on foot, one hand loose near his belt out of habit more than expectation. He didn't ask questions. He knew this town's roads without looking at them, and he picked the shorter route past the mill without being told.

The parish house smelled like tallow and old paper. The priest who met them, Emeric, didn't bother learning his name, was polite in the specific way people were polite to nobility they didn't trust yet, and reluctant to hand over the ledgers until Bram said something low and local that Emeric didn't catch. Whatever it was, it worked.

They carried four books out between them. Rylan took the heaviest without being asked.

Back at the smithy, Emeric cleared a table and started stacking pages by year, then by month, then by something none of the other two could see yet. Names, ages, cause when one was written down, and next to each entry, a location: which well, which street, which row of the burial ground.

"You're not reading it," Bram said, watching him move faster than reading allowed. "You're sorting it."

"Same thing, further out." Emeric didn't look up. "One death near the north well doesn't mean anything. Six in the same month, all on the same side of it, means something."

Rylan leaned over the table, quiet for a while. Then he put a finger on one line, an entry near the bottom of a page three years back.

"That's my aunt's row." He said it flat, no weight on it, but his hand stayed on the page a second longer than it needed to. "Same well."

Emeric marked it and kept going. He didn't say anything about the pause. He didn't need to; the pattern would say it for him soon enough, and it would say it to a hundred people who'd lost someone the same way, not just to a councilman table.

By the time the candle burned low, he had the beginning of a map on cheap paper: dots for deaths, clustered thick around the north well, thinning out toward the east side of town where the water ran higher and cleaner. Not proof yet. A shape.

"That's it?" Bram said, looking at the scatter of ink. "That's your evidence?"

"That's the start of it." Emeric sat back. "Now I go find the households near the thick part of that map, and I ask them to try something different for a season. Not everyone. Enough."

Emeric and the group decided to go house to house, explaining what the ledgers had told him. He kept it simple, plain terms, no lecture: the water near the burial ground wasn't safe to drink, hadn't been for a long time, and the sickness that kept coming back wasn't a curse on the town; it was that simple, ugly problem going unnoticed for decades. He showed them where the clean water was, said little else, and left it at that. He couldn't force anyone to change how they lived. That was how riots started, and it wasn't a road he had any interest in walking.

It took two weeks to know anything for certain, and what he learned wasn't clean.

The Hallards, three doors down from the north well, switched without complaint, boiled every cup before it touched a mouth, and nobody in that house so much as coughed wrong the whole fortnight. The widow Corwin tried it for four days, decided the extra walk to the clean well wasn't worth the ache in her knees, and went back to the water she'd always used; her youngest ran a fever by the end of the week, mild, survivable, but a fever all the same. Old Pell refused outright, told Rylan to his face that he'd drunk from that well sixty years and wasn't about to let a boy prince tell him different, and nothing happened to him at all, which did more to undercut the case on that street than any failure could have. The Talbrooks split the difference, boiled water for the children and didn't bother for themselves, and it was the parents who got sick, not the kids.

No clean line. Nothing a determined skeptic couldn't poke a hole through. But enough of a pattern, laid next to the ledger's dots, that anyone looking carefully could see it: boil the water and the odds moved, even if they didn't move for everyone, and even if some who ignored every word of it walked away fine regardless. Emeric filed each result the way he'd have logged test data that refused to behave, not proof the method had failed, just proof it was real.

By the time Bram invited him back for a drink, weeks further on, the argument had mostly made itself. The holdouts didn't come around all at once. They came around one bad week at a time, one neighbor's fever weighed against another neighbor's dry summer, until boiling the water before it touched a mouth was just what people did on that end of town without needing to be told twice. Old Pell was still drinking straight from the well the last anyone checked, daring the rot to try him again. Nobody was betting on him changing his mind. But he was the exception people talked about now, not the rule.

Since the smithy was closed for the evening, Bram invited Emeric back to his home to share a drink. The house looked almost the same as the others from the outside, but the upkeep showed once he was through the door. Living with a blacksmith clearly had its perks.

Emeric sat down in a chair in Bram's living room, a cup of something herbal going cold in his hand, Bram's daughter having poured it for him without a word before disappearing back into the house. He thought of the list he'd written down the night before, six items, one of them finally starting to show results.

"I plan on solving the town's problems while I'm here, Bram," Emeric said, the look on his face doing most of the talking before the words caught up. "I'm going to need a lot of help. I can't do this alone. If we're going to get the town out of its current state, we need the town on board, not just watching from a distance."

"That's easier said than done." Bram sipped at his own cup without looking up. "Hope's costly in this town, and nobody's willing to pay for it twice."

"The water I can handle, I think I already have. The fields are going to be a bigger problem. I can grow that field myself; I've got enough in me to do it, but what happens the day I'm not here to do it again? Growth magic needs a steady hand behind it, and if that hand's mine, it's mine instead of everything else this barony needs me for. It makes no sense that the capital hasn't sent anyone down here already; it's like there's no report of what's actually happening in this town, like somebody's decided there won't be one. Fixing the fields properly isn't the hard part. I know ways to get more out of a harvest than anyone here's used to seeing: faster growth, a bigger yield, ways to collect and store it that don't rot half of it before winter. All of that needs metal. And it needs a smith."

Bram's cup paused halfway to his mouth. "Are you trying to hire me, boy?"

"Yes. You and your workers. Exclusive, long-term. The whole barony's smithing, if you'll take it."

"Tell me more of these ideas of yours, and I'll think about it."

"All right. So, this is how farming actually works."

Emeric spent the rest of the evening explaining how a plant grew, root to leaf, in terms he hoped would land without sounding like something out of a heretic's mouth. He skipped past the parts he couldn't prove to a man with no way to check them, nitrogen, photosynthesis, the invisible machinery running underneath every green thing Bram had ever grown or sold metal to someone else for growing, and kept to what he could show: rotate what you plant so the soil doesn't get bled dry of the same thing every season, feed it what it's missing instead of guessing, water it on a schedule instead of whenever someone remembers. None of it needed magic to work. The magic was just going to make it work faster, and bigger, and less dependent on however many hands the plague and the war had left behind.

Bram listened without interrupting, which Emeric had already learned was the closest thing the man had to enthusiasm. When it was done, he set his cup down and studied Emeric the same way he had at the forge, like a piece of metal he still hadn't found the flaw in.

"You talk like you've already built this in your head," Bram said.

"I have. I just don't have the hands or the metal to build it yet. That's what I'm asking you for."

Bram was quiet for a long moment. Somewhere in the back of the house, his daughter was doing something that involved the occasional clatter of a pot, close enough to be listening, far enough to pretend she wasn't.

"I'll think on it," Bram said again, which, from him, Emeric was starting to understand, was not a no.

"By the way, Bram, you haven't introduced me to your family," Emeric said to break the silence.

"It's just me and my daughter Ferra; the wife died of the plague a couple of years ago, and since then it's just been us," Bram said with a quiet sadness behind his words.

Emeric felt awful having asked, given he had also lost someone that meant a lot to him. "Condolences," he said, realizing he soured the mood while trying to escape the initial silence.

Realizing he didn't mean to sadden the overall mood of the conversation, Bram said, "I know my daughter is cute, but no, you cannot have her"

Emeric almost burst out laughing, tea almost erupting through his nose. " I would never ask you to do that for me. Not only would she have to accept my advances, but I would also take it upon myself to ask for your consent before anything, of course."

Emeric could sense Ferra around the corner where the kitchen was; although he couldn't gauge her emotional state, this is the scene in all the movies he's watched where a female character would start blushing, realizing they were talking about her in such a light. Emeric decided to pretend he saw nothing.

With that short interaction, the mood had gone back up. At day's end, he decided to walk back to the castle to sleep. Ward was still sneaking around following him. At this point, it was a ritual.

Today presented a surprising sight. The baron of Kaldo, Wexler, was waiting for him. For what, he didn't know. He'll surely find out, though.

"Hello, Emeric," he said in a flat tone hiding any and every possible emotion.

This was the silent, sporadic disdain Emeric felt since his arrival. Most people lower on the royal hierarchy would call someone higher than them by their title, then their name. Although Emeric didn't care for such pleasantries, as part of him is not used to it being a blend of a man from Earth and all. Still, the baron, knowing royal customs, actively doesn't follow them. Nonetheless, Emeric can't really call him out on that. Why create bad blood in a place where most would blatantly see you as the villainous aggressor?

"Hello Wexler, what can I help you with?" Emeric said, matching the baron's silent disrespect.

" I heard you've been making trips around my barony. Is everything ok?" Baron Wexler said with a snide tone.

Emeric didn't feel a need to lie here; he knew the baron knew exactly where he'd been and where. Most rules don't let anyone who has some measure of power roam their lands untethered. While Emeric very much doubts there was a spy following them, as Ward would handle those silently, he knows the baron can just wait and trace their steps. Information wasn't hard to get; most people caved to pleasure, payment, or pain.

Emeric prompted the baron to talk inside. This simple move irked the baron, although he didn't show it. Who was this young boy treating him like he was leading things? As far as he knew, he, the baron, was the only one allowed to move a conversation to another room.

Emeric proceeded to tell the baron about the field surveys he was doing through the town in an effort to explain why the plague was collecting so many bodies. The baron heard this and had no type of emotion on his face, but he listened.

Wexler didn't sit forward. He didn't need to. "Surveys," he repeated, like he was turning the word over for a hidden edge. "You spent a full day in my town doing surveys."

"Water sources, burial sites, and dates of first symptom onset, cross-referenced against household proximity to the river," Emeric said it the way he'd say it to Bram, or to himself. Not performing an answer, just giving one. "The wells nearest the latrine outflow correlate with the earliest and worst cases. That's not proof yet; it's a pattern. I need two more days of data before I'd act on it."

Something shifted behind Wexler's eyes, small, and gone almost as fast as it came.

"And what would you do with two more days of data, Your Highness?"

"Move the latrines upriver, or move the wells. Whichever's cheaper. Boil the water in the meantime, tell people why, not just that they should." Emeric shrugged, like it was obvious, because to him it was. "It won't be, though. Not without buy-in from someone people already trust. I can hand a stranger a theory and get nothing. Bram can hand them the same theory and get compliance by dinner."

He hadn't meant that as a display of anything. It was just true, the same way water finds the low ground.

Wexler was quiet for a moment too long.

"Bram," he said finally, "doesn't take to strangers."

"He didn't take to me either, at first." Emeric didn't dress it up. "I gave him something worth taking to."

The baron's disdain, the lazy, performance-of-minimum-respect kind he'd worn since the road, wasn't in his face anymore. What replaced it wasn't respect either. It was the particular stillness of a man recalculating a threat he'd priced too cheap. A wasted prince asks where the sickness comes from and gets told a story. This one had built a testable model in under a day, out of nothing, and already had the town's most trusted man speaking for him before the castle even knew he'd left it.

"Get some rest, Your Highness," Wexler said, standing this time, which he hadn't bothered to do before. "You've had a long day."

It wasn't a dismissal. It was a man buying time to think.
